# India: Economic Surveys 2020-21 and 2021-22 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of the "Pandemic shock and response" chapter (2020-2021: the national lockdown, the fall in output, the fiscal, monetary and regulatory response, the external surplus, the second wave and the 2021 recovery, and the world as the Surveys saw it). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book. ES 2022-23 is used only for retrospective revisions of the 2020-21 and 2021-22 figures (ES22-1 to ES22-4). ES 2020-21's retrospective items on 2019-20 are in `India_ES_2015-2020.md` (ES20-1 to ES20-3 there) and are not repeated; the item numbers below restart.

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2020-21 (two volumes, January 2021) and 2021-22 (one volume, January 2022), with four items from 2022-23 (one volume, January 2023). Official English PDFs from indiabudget.gov.in; archive pages listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md` (2020-21: indiabudget.gov.in/budget2021-22/economicsurvey/; 2021-22: /budget2022-23/economicsurvey/; 2022-23: /budget2023-24/economicsurvey/). Read from the extracts in `Econ Subject/sources/text/es<YEAR>.txt`. ES 2020-21's two volumes and each Survey's Statistical Appendix are stitched into one file per Survey.

**Data cut-offs, as the texts show them.**
- ES 2020-21 (January 2021): NSO advance estimate for 2020-21 (−7.7%) and quarterly estimates to Q2 2020-21; reserves to 8 January 2021; CPI to December 2020; FPI to December 2020; BoP to H1 2020-21; vaccination to 22 January 2021; market records to 20 January 2021; IMF figures from the October 2020 WEO and Fiscal Monitor (V2) and, in V1, a −3.5% IMF figure for 2020.
- ES 2021-22 (January 2022): NSO First Advance Estimates for 2021-22 (9.2%) and Provisional Estimates for 2020-21 (−7.3%); reserves to 31 December 2021; CPI to December 2021; BoP to H1 2021-22; vaccination to 16-19 January 2022; IMF WEO Update of 25 January 2022.

**GDP vintage.** All growth figures are on the 2011-12-base series. Each year's number moves from AE to PE to revised estimates between Surveys. 2020-21: −7.7 (AE, ES 2020-21) → −7.3 (PE, ES 2021-22) → −6.6 (1st RE, derived from ES 2022-23 levels). 2021-22: 11.0 forecast (ES 2020-21) → 9.2 (AE, ES 2021-22) → 8.7 (PE, ES 2022-23). ## Economic Survey 2020-21 (January 2021)

### The pandemic and the lockdown

**ES20-1 — A once-in-a-century, synchronised recession.** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 18); paras 1.4-1.5, p. 4 (st. 21).
The pandemic is presented as a once-in-a-century crisis, with 90% of countries expected to see GDP per capita fall in 2020. Para 1.4 puts the world contraction in 2020 at 4.3% (World Bank) and 3.5% (IMF). Para 1.5 says the share of economies with falling per capita income is the highest since 1870, above 90%, against about 85% at the height of the Great Depression; the event is called once in 150 years.
Quote (para 1.5): "It is aptly called the 'Great Lockdown'."
FLAG (vintage): V2 gives −4.4% (IMF, October 2020; ES20-34). ES 2021-22 Table 8 gives −3.1% (IMF, January 2022; ES21-14).

**ES20-2 — "Lives and livelihoods": the framing.** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, pp. 1-2 (st. 18-19); paras 1.6-1.7, p. 4 (st. 21); V2 Ch 1 summary, p. 1 (st. 373).
The chapter title is "Saving Lives and Livelihoods Amidst a Once-in-a-Century Crisis". The short-run trade-off is between health and lives on one side and the economy and livelihoods on the other (para 1.6). The Survey grounds India's choice in a Mahabharata verse on saving a life in jeopardy: GDP will recover, lives lost cannot be brought back. The claim is that India turned the short-run trade-off into a medium- to long-term win-win. V2 calls it the policy dilemma of lives versus livelihoods.
Quote (V1 summary, p. 2): "transformed the short-term trade-off between lives and livelihoods into a win-win".

**ES20-3 — The lockdown: dates and stringency.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.25, p. 15 (st. 32); V2 Ch 1, para 1.45, p. 37 (st. 409); V2 Ch 1 Table 1, p. 38 (st. 410); V2 Ch 10, para 10.1, p. 326 (st. 698).
V1 para 1.25: India imposed a national lockdown when there were only 500 confirmed cases; the stringent lockdown ran from 25 March to 31 May. V2 para 1.45: a nationwide "stringent" lockdown for 21 days was declared on 24 March 2020 and extended to 31 May 2020; on the Oxford Government Response Tracker India was among the first to impose a stringent lockdown (index at 100) with few cases. V2 Table 1 lists school closures, a complete nation-wide lockdown for 21 days, travel bans, red/orange/green zoning, and mandatory masks. V2 para 10.1 dates the complete lockdown from 24 March to 31 May 2020.
Quote (V2 para 1.45): "lockdown for 21 days was declared on March 24, 2020".
FLAG (internal): case count at imposition — 500 confirmed cases (V1 para 1.25) vs 100 (V2 Ch 1 summary, p. 1, st. 373). Start date — declared 24 March (V2 1.45), from 24 March (V2 10.1), from 25 March (V1 1.25). Use "announced 24 March, in force from 25 March" only if the book can carry both; otherwise cite each text.

**ES20-4 — Why front-load: worst-case planning, Spanish Flu, "barbell".** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 18); paras 1.22-1.25, p. 15 (st. 32).
The Survey cites Hansen and Sargent (2001) on minimising losses in the worst case under high uncertainty, and Spanish Flu evidence that early, extensive lockdowns delayed and lowered peak mortality without harming local economies afterwards. It cites an estimate (Klein et al., 2020) of 30 crore cases in India by end-May 2020. Its stated vulnerabilities are a huge population, high density, a large elderly population in absolute numbers and overburdened health infrastructure. Policy hedged for the worst outcome first and then updated step by step ("Bayesian updating"), under the slogan "Jaan Hai to Jahan hai".
Quote (para 1.25): "an approach similar to the Barbell strategy in finance".
Note: the "barbell" label is already here in ES 2020-21; ES 2021-22 refers back to it (ES21-4).

**ES20-5 — The lockdown as time to build health capacity.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.26-1.27, pp. 15-16 (st. 32-33); V2 Ch 1, para 1.45, p. 37 (st. 409).
The lockdown period was used to build the "5 T" strategy (Test, Track, Trace, Treat, Technology). Testing went from fewer than 100 tests a day at one lab (January 2020) to 10 lakh a day at 2,305 labs, and more than 17 crore cumulative tests by January 2021. V2: by 29 December 2020, 2,70,710 oxygen-supported isolation beds, 81,113 ICU beds (40,627 ventilator beds) and 12,669 quarantine centres had been created; India became the second-largest producer of PPE kits.
FLAG (internal): para 1.26 speaks of a 40-day lockdown period, while para 1.25 gives 25 March to 31 May.

**ES20-6 — Lives saved: the counterfactual estimate.** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, p. 2 (st. 19).
Comparing actual cases and deaths with numbers "expected" from population, density, demographics, testing and health infrastructure, the Survey estimates that India restricted the spread by 37 lakh cases and saved more than 1 lakh lives. It finds that State-level stringency correlates with both outcomes and with the speed of economic recovery, and infers a causal effect of the lockdown.
Quote: "saved more than 1 lakh lives".

**ES20-7 — "Avoiding the second wave".** V2 Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 374); para 1.3, p. 3 (st. 375); V1 Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 19); para 1.59, p. 40 (st. 57).
India's caseload peaked in mid-September 2020, after which daily cases fell despite rising mobility. The Survey says India avoided a second wave as of writing and calls India sui generis. Para 1.3: advanced economies were in third waves at end-2020; other EMDEs in second waves.
Quote (V2 summary): "India has been able to avoid the second wave".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2021-22 records a second wave in April-June 2021 that was more severe in health terms (ES21-2).

**ES20-8 — Global and Indian case counts.** V2 Ch 1, para 1.1, p. 3 (st. 375); para 1.5, p. 5 (st. 377); para 1.11, p. 9 (st. 381).
WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern on 30 January 2020 and a pandemic on 11 March 2020. Para 1.5: India's cases had passed 1.06 crore, around 11% of the world total.
FLAG (internal): world deaths are "over 2 lakh" in para 1.1 but "over 21 lakh fatalities" in para 1.11, both with over 10 crore cases. The para 1.1 figure looks like a typesetting error. Do not use it.

### The fall in output

**ES20-9 — Q1 2020-21: −23.9%; Q2: −7.5%; the "V-shaped" claim.** V1 Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 19); para 1.42, p. 29 (st. 46); V2 Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 374); para 1.50, p. 42 (st. 414).
GDP contracted 23.9% in Q1 (April-June 2020) and 7.5% in Q2. The Survey calls the recovery V-shaped from July, citing power demand, e-way bills, GST collections and steel consumption. It says the Q1 contraction matched one of the most stringent lockdowns on the Oxford index.
Quote (V2 summary): "the lockdown resulted in a 23.9 per cent contraction in GDP in Q1".
FLAG: no later revision of the Q1 figure was found in ES 2021-22 or ES 2022-23 (see Not found).

**ES20-10 — 2020-21: −7.7% (advance estimate), the fourth contraction since 1960-61.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.51-1.54, pp. 42-43 (st. 414-415); summary, p. 2 (st. 374).
NSO estimates real GDP at −7.7% in 2020-21, against +4.2% in 2019-20: −15.7% in H1 and −0.1% in H2. It is the fourth contraction since 1960-61 (after 1965-66, 1971-72 and 1979-80, all tied to droughts and falling farm output); this time agriculture was the "silver lining". Government consumption's share of GDP rose to 14.0% from 12.0%; gross investment fell to 26.7% of GDP, the lowest in the 2000s. Net exports cushioned the fall because imports fell more (−29.1% in H1) than exports (−10.7%). For H2 the Survey expects government consumption +17%, private consumption −0.6% and GFCF −0.8%.
Quote (para 1.51): "This is the fourth contraction in India's GDP since 1960-61".
FLAG (vintage): −7.7 (AE, here) → −7.3 (PE, ES 2021-22) → −6.6 (1st RE, derived from ES 2022-23 SA). 2019-20: 4.2 here → 4.0 (1st RE) in ES 2021-22 Table 3.

**ES20-11 — Outlook for 2021-22: 11%, "highest since independence".** V2 Ch 1, para 1.61, p. 47 (st. 419); "at a glance", p. 49 (st. 421).
Real GDP is projected to grow 11.0% in 2021-22 (nominal 15.4%), which would leave real GDP 2.4% above 2019-20, so the economy takes two years to pass its pre-pandemic level. The IMF projects 11.5% (2021-22) and 6.8% (2022-23). The chapter summary calls 11% the highest growth since independence.
Quote (p. 49): "11 per cent is the highest since independence".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): 9.2% AE (ES 2021-22, ES21-1) and 8.7% PE (ES 2022-23, ES22-2).

### Fiscal response

**ES20-12 — Demand policy: essentials first, "accelerator while the brakes are clamped".** V2 Ch 1 summary, pp. 1-2 (st. 373-374); V1 Ch 1, paras 1.47-1.49, pp. 32-33 (st. 49-50).
The Survey treats the pandemic as hitting supply and demand together. While uncertainty and lockdowns kept precautionary saving high, India chose not to spend fiscal resources on discretionary consumption and spent on essentials. It ramped up spending in the unlock phase. V1 cites a US study (Coibion et al., 2020) that only 15% of stimulus-cheque recipients spent the money, 33% saved it and 52% paid down debt.
Quote (V2 summary): "pressing on the accelerator while the brakes are clamped only wastes scarce fuel".

**ES20-13 — Size of the package: ₹29.87 lakh crore, 15% of GDP.** V2 Ch 2, Box 2, p. 59 (st. 431); Ch 8, para 8.2 and Box 1, p. 262 (st. 634); Ch 10, para 10.1, p. 326 (st. 698).
Government and RBI together announced stimulus worth ₹29.87 lakh crore, 15% of GDP, of which 9% of GDP was provided by the Government under the Atma Nirbhar Bharat package. The tranches are dated 13-17 May 2020 (special economic and comprehensive package), 12 October 2020 (consumer spending) and 12 November 2020 (Atma Nirbhar Bharat 3.0). Ch 10 instead gives a first relief package of ₹1.70 lakh crore under PMGKY (March 2020) and a comprehensive stimulus-cum-relief package of ₹20 lakh crore under Atma Nirbhar Bharat (May 2020).
Quote (Box 2): "stimulus worth 9% of GDP has been provided by the Government".
FLAG (internal): three totals — ₹29.87 lakh crore / 15% of GDP (Government plus RBI), 9% of GDP (Government), ₹20 lakh crore (Ch 10). They measure different things; say which.

**ES20-14 — A phased mix: relief, then consumption, then investment.** V2 Ch 2, Box 2 and Figure 6, pp. 59-60 (st. 431-432); paras 2.1-2.2, p. 52 (st. 424); V1 Ch 1, para 1.49, p. 33 (st. 50).
Figure 6 gives the size of each round: March 2020 ₹1,92,800 crore (88% poor: food and livelihood); May-June 2020 ₹11,85,561 crore (37% liquidity support to firms, 30% agricultural credit and infrastructure); October 2020 ₹73,000 crore (consumption and investment boosting, about half each); November 2020 ₹2,65,080 crore (59% investment boosting). The Survey contrasts this with the one-time, front-loaded packages of other countries.
Quote (para 2.2): "Government of India adopted a step-by-step approach".

**ES20-15 — Free food grain (PMGKAY).** V2 Ch 7, paras 7.48-7.49, p. 249 (st. 621); Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 374).
PM-GKAY gave 5 kg of food grain per person per month free, on top of NFSA rations, to all TPDS beneficiaries for April-June 2020: about 121 LMT to about 80.96 crore beneficiaries, a subsidy of about ₹46,061 crore. It was extended for July-November 2020 (about 201 LMT, about ₹76,062.11 crore). Separately, about 8 crore migrants not covered by NFSA were allotted free grain for May-June 2020 under the Atma Nirbhar Bharat package.
Quote (V2 summary): "the world's largest food subsidy programme targeting 80.96 crore beneficiaries".

**ES20-16 — Direct transfers.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.49 and Table 3, pp. 33-34 (st. 50-51); V2 Ch 1, para 1.46, pp. 36-37 (st. 408-409).
V1 Table 3 (as on 31 December 2020): PMGKY direct benefit transfers totalled ₹68,914 crore to 42.1 crore beneficiaries. They comprised three instalments to about 20.6 crore PMJDY women account-holders (about ₹10,300 crore each), front-loaded PM-KISAN payments (₹17,891 crore to 8.94 crore), NSAP payments (₹2,814 crore), support to construction workers, the EPFO contribution and free Ujjwala cylinders (₹9,700 crore). V2 1.46 credits the JAM trinity (Jan Dhan, Aadhaar, mobile). Garib Kalyan Rojgar Abhiyaan was launched on 20 June 2020 for 125 days in 116 districts of 6 States.
Quote (V2 1.46): "direct cash transfers to 42 crore individuals".

**ES20-17 — Credit guarantees: ECLGS.** V2 Ch 8, Box 1, p. 262 (st. 634); Ch 1, para 1.46, p. 37 (st. 409).
ECLGS (Atma Nirbhar Bharat 1.0) offered up to ₹3 lakh crore of collateral-free, fully guaranteed additional credit to MSMEs with up to ₹25 crore outstanding and ₹100 crore turnover; no guarantee fee and no fresh collateral. The same box lists ₹20,000 crore of subordinate debt for stressed MSMEs, ₹50,000 crore of equity through an MSME Fund of Funds, a new MSME definition, and no global tenders below ₹200 crore. ECLGS 2.0 extended guarantees to identified stressed sectors.
Quote: "100 per cent credit guarantee cover to Banks and NBFCs on principal and interest".
FLAG (vintage): ES 2021-22 gives the ECLGS ceiling as ₹4.5 lakh crore (ES21-6).

**ES20-18 — Supply-side reforms during the shock.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.51-1.52 and Table 4, p. 35 (st. 52); summary, p. 2 (st. 19).
The Survey argues that simply reflating demand amid supply constraints would have produced runaway inflation. It therefore pairs relief with structural reforms under the Atmanirbhar Bharat Mission. Table 4 (pp. 35-36, st. 52-53) lists the three farm Acts of 2020 (including the Essential Commodities (Amendment) Act, 2020), the new MSME definition, the four labour codes, power-tariff reform, privatisation of PSUs in non-strategic sectors, commercial coal mining, PLI for 10 sectors, and opening of space and geospatial data.
Quote (para 1.51): "India is the only country to have undertaken structural reforms on the supply-side".

**ES20-19 — The fiscal year 2020-21: borrowing up, slippage expected.** V2 Ch 2 summary, pp. 51-52 (st. 423-424); para 2.5, p. 52 (st. 424).
Gross market borrowing for 2020-21 was raised from ₹7.8 lakh crore (Budget) to ₹12 lakh crore. Monthly GST collections had crossed ₹1 lakh crore for three months, with a record in December 2020. Capital expenditure grew 129% (October), 249% (November) and 62% (December) year on year. Ministries were split into categories A-C for spending limits in H1. The Survey expects a fiscal slippage but calls it potentially transient. It gives no deficit figure for 2020-21 (the Budget came after the Survey).
Quote: "is expected to register a fiscal slippage in 2020-21".

**ES20-20 — The world's fiscal response.** V2 Ch 2, para 2.1, p. 52 (st. 424); Figure 5 and Table 3, p. 59 (st. 431); V2 Ch 1 summary, p. 1 (st. 373).
Fiscal measures worldwide amounted to 12% of global GDP (IMF Fiscal Monitor, October 2020, measures to 11 September 2020). Table 3 (liquidity support, % of GDP) gives India 0.3 below the line, 4.5 in guarantees and 0.5 in quasi-fiscal operations, against guarantees of 16.5 in the UK, 14.8 in France and 24.8 in Germany. The V2 summary lists the tools used abroad: lower policy rates, quantitative easing, loan guarantees, cash transfers and fiscal stimulus.
Quote (para 2.1): "massive fiscal measures, amounting to 12 percent of global GDP".
Caution: the per-country values in Figure 5 (above-the-line measures) cannot be matched to countries reliably in the extract; read them from the PDF.

**ES20-21 — Growth leads to debt sustainability, not vice versa.** V1 Ch 2, title and summary, pp. 43-44 (st. 60-61).
The chapter argues that in India the interest rate on government debt has been below the growth rate as a rule rather than an exception, so the interest rate-growth rate differential (IRGD) is negative, as Blanchard (2019) argued for advanced economies, but in India because growth is high rather than rates low. Growth causes debt to become sustainable in high-growth countries; that causal direction is unclear in low-growth countries. With IRGD expected to stay negative, growth-supporting fiscal policy would lower, not raise, the debt ratio.
Quote: "growth leads to debt sustainability in the Indian context but not necessarily vice-versa".

**ES20-22 — Counter-cyclical fiscal policy, "not fiscal irresponsibility".** V1 Ch 2 summary, p. 44 (st. 61); paras 2.2-2.3 and Box 1, pp. 44-45 (st. 61-62).
Fiscal multipliers are higher in crises than in booms; in India, segmented public and private labour markets limit crowding out. India's fiscal policy has historically not been counter-cyclical (correlation of public and private balances near −0.9 in the US and UK, not in India). Simulations to 2030 show debt sustainable even in the worst scenarios. The Survey says it wants to break an intellectual anchoring that biases policy against fiscal expansion.
Quote: "not a call for fiscal irresponsibility".

**ES20-23 — The debt simulation's assumptions.** V1 Ch 2, para 2.39 and Box 7, p. 74 (st. 91).
The worst-case simulation assumes real growth of −7.7% in 2020-21 (MoSPI) and 11.5% in 2021-22 (IMF); general-government debt of 73.8% of GDP in 2019-20 (RBI revised estimate); a combined primary deficit of 6.8% of GDP in 2020-21 (1.3 baseline plus 5.5 for revenue shortfall and the Atmanirbhar package), falling to 1.5% by 2023-24; a 6% nominal interest rate; and 5% inflation. It finds debt-to-GDP sustainable in the worst case by 2028-29 (Figure 26a, "FY29").
FLAG (vintage): ES 2022-23 gives general-government debt of 75.7% at end-March 2020 and 89.6% at end-2020-21 (ES22-3).

### Monetary and financial response

**ES20-24 — Repo cuts: 115 bps in March and May 2020.** V2 Ch 4 summary, p. 123 (st. 495); paras 4.1-4.2 and Table 1, p. 124 (st. 496).
The MPC brought its first two meetings of 2020-21 forward (to end-March, and to 20-22 May). Table 1: repo 5.15% (6 February 2020) → 4.40% (27 March 2020), reverse repo cut 90 bps to 4.0% (an asymmetric corridor to discourage banks parking funds); CRR 4% → 3% (28 March); reverse repo 3.75% (17 April); repo 4.0%, reverse repo 3.35%, MSF 4.25% (22 May 2020). The repo was unchanged in August, October and December 2020. Para 4.26 (p. 133, st. 505) counts 250 bps of cuts since February 2019.
Quote: "The repo rate has been cut by 115 bps since March 2020".

**ES20-25 — Looking through inflation above the band.** V2 Ch 4, para 4.3, p. 124 (st. 496).
Inflation was above the tolerance band for some months, but the MPC judged the drivers to be supply shocks that would fade as the economy unlocked, and kept rates unchanged. In its latest meeting the RBI revised its 2020-21 growth projection from −9.5% to −7.5%.
Quote: "essentially supply shocks that should dissipate over time".
FLAG (vintage): RBI's −7.5% vs NSO's −7.7% advance estimate cited elsewhere in the same Survey.

**ES20-26 — Liquidity: OMOs, LTROs, TLTROs and special windows.** V2 Ch 1, para 1.47, p. 37 (st. 409); Ch 4 summary, p. 123 (st. 495).
RBI injected durable liquidity of more than ₹2.7 lakh crore through OMO purchases (6 February-4 December 2020) and ₹20,000 crore through SDL OMOs. It added ₹1 lakh crore through TLTROs of up to three years, ₹1.25 lakh crore through LTROs (February-March 2020), about ₹1.37 lakh crore through the CRR cut (from 28 March 2020), a ₹50,000 crore special liquidity facility for mutual funds, and ₹75,000 crore of refinance for NABARD, NHB, SIDBI and EXIM Bank. An SPV was set up to buy short-term paper of NBFCs and HFCs. Systemic liquidity stayed in surplus.
Quote: "via Targeted Long Term Repo Operations (TLTROs) of up to three years' tenor".

**ES20-27 — Moratorium, IBC suspension, restructuring.** V2 Ch 1, para 1.48, p. 37 (st. 409); Ch 4 summary, p. 123 (st. 495).
RBI allowed a loan moratorium from 1 March to 31 August 2020, with an asset-classification dispensation and a resolution framework for COVID-19 stress (lenders could grant up to two more years of moratorium). Standard MSME accounts with exposure up to ₹25 crore could be restructured without downgrade. Fresh insolvency proceedings (IBC sections 7, 9, 10) were suspended for defaults from 25 March 2020: for six months, then extended twice by three months (24 September and 22 December 2020), to 25 March 2021.
Quote: "RBI, too, announced loan moratorium from 1st March 2020 to 31st August 2020".

**ES20-28 — Against prolonged forbearance: the 2008-15 lesson.** V1 Ch 7, title and summary, pp. 199-200 (st. 216-217); para 7.1, p. 200 (st. 217).
Chapter 7 is titled "Regulatory Forbearance: An Emergency Medicine, Not Staple Diet!". Forbearance on restructured loans began in 2008 and should have ended in 2011, when GDP, exports, IIP and credit had recovered, but lasted seven years, to 2015. Banks restructured unviable loans, inflated profits, paid dividends, became undercapitalised and lent to zombies; firm governance deteriorated. Restructuring rose sevenfold and NPAs nearly doubled. After the Asset Quality Review, gross NPAs rose from 4.3% (2014-15) to 7.5% (2015-16) and peaked at 11.2% (2017-18).
Quote: "when an emergency medicine becomes a staple diet, it can be counterproductive".
FLAG (vintage): ES 2018-19 gave gross NPAs of 11.5% in March 2018 (`India_ES_2015-2020.md`, ES18-10); 11.2% here, repeated in ES 2021-22 (ES21-11).

**ES20-29 — Lessons for the current forbearance.** V1 Ch 7, para 7.42, p. 234 (st. 251).
Five recommendations: withdraw forbearance at the first sign of recovery, with thresholds announced in advance; restrict zombie lending; run an AQR immediately after withdrawal, covering all forms of ever-greening; pair any clean-up with mandatory recapitalisation; improve bank governance and consider penalties on auditors.
Quote: "Prolonged forbearance is likely to sow the seeds of a much deeper crisis."

**ES20-30 — Banks in 2020: NPAs, capital, credit.** V2 Ch 4 summary, p. 123 (st. 495).
Gross NPAs of scheduled commercial banks fell from 8.21% (end-March 2020) to 7.49% (end-September 2020), which the Survey says must be read with the asset-classification relief. CRAR rose from 14.7% to 15.8%. Bank credit growth slowed to 6.7% (1 January 2021). Lending rates on fresh loans fell 94 bps (March-November 2020). The Nifty 50 and Sensex closed at records of 14,644.7 and 49,792.12 on 20 January 2021.
Quote: "this has to be seen in conjunction with the asset classification relief".

### Inflation

**ES20-31 — CPI above 6%, driven by food.** V2 Ch 5 summary, p. 161 (st. 533); para 5.2 and Table 2, p. 163 (st. 535); Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 374).
CPI-C inflation was 4.8% in 2019-20 and 6.6% in April-December 2020, easing to 4.6% in December 2020, a 15-month low. Food (CFPI) inflation was 9.1% in April-December 2020, peaking at 11.0% in October 2020 and falling to 3.4% in December. Monthly headline CPI was 6.7, 6.7, 7.3, 7.6 and 6.9 from July to November 2020. Core inflation averaged 5.4%. CPI for April-May 2020 is imputed from limited observations. The chapter argues for more focus on core inflation and a new CPI base.

### External sector

**ES20-32 — The first annual current-account surplus in 17 years (expected).** V2 Ch 3 summary, p. 90 (st. 462); para 3.19, p. 105 (st. 477); para 3.26, p. 112 (st. 484).
The current account showed a surplus of 0.1% of GDP in Q4 2019-20, the first quarterly surplus in 13 years (since Q4 2006-07). It stayed in surplus in Q1 and Q2 2020-21: US$34.7bn, 3.1% of GDP, in H1, as current payments fell 30.8% and receipts 15.1%. The Survey expects a surplus of at least 2% of GDP for the year, the first annual surplus in 17 years. The CAD had averaged 2.2% of GDP over the previous ten years.
Quote (summary): "current account surplus during the current financial year after a gap of 17 years".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): the outcome was US$23.9bn, 0.9% of GDP (ES 2021-22, ES21-16).

**ES20-33 — Capital flows, reserves and the rupee.** V2 Ch 3, paras 3.20-3.22, pp. 105-106 (st. 477-478); paras 3.28-3.30, pp. 113-114 (st. 485-486).
Net FDI was US$27.5bn in April-October 2020, 14.8% higher year on year; net FPI was US$28.5bn in April-December 2020 (US$12.3bn a year earlier), helped by global liquidity and MSCI index inclusion. This followed FPI outflows of US$15.92bn in March 2020. Reserves reached an all-time high of US$586.1bn on 8 January 2021, about 18 months of imports; India was the fifth-largest holder at end-September 2020. The rupee hit its low of ₹76.86 per US$ on 16 April 2020 and then appreciated; RBI intervention curbed volatility and one-sided appreciation.
Quote (para 3.29): "unprecedented FPI outflows of US$ 15.92 billion in March 2020".

### The world as ES 2020-21 saw it

**ES20-34 — World output and trade in 2020.** V2 Ch 3, paras 3.1-3.3, p. 91 (st. 463); Ch 1 "at a glance", p. 48 (st. 420); para 1.2, p. 3 (st. 375).
Global goods trade in the first five months of 2020 was about 20% lower than in 2019, a sharper fall than at the start of the global financial crisis. **ES20-35 — Vaccination begins (dated "2020" in the text).** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.62-1.63, p. 48 (st. 420).

## Economic Survey 2021-22 (January 2022)

**ES21-1 — 2021-22: 9.2% (First Advance Estimate), past pre-pandemic output.** Ch 1 summary, p. 1 (st. 27); para 1.2, p. 3 (st. 29); para 1.44, p. 38 (st. 64).

## Problems and caveats

1. **Layout.** Both Surveys are largely two-column and the extracts interleave columns in tables, boxes and figures. Figure values (e.g. ES20 V2 Figure 5, ES21 Figure 21) cannot always be tied to their labels; check the PDF before using them. ₹ is rendered as "`".
2. **GDP vintages.** 2020-21 moved from −7.7 to −7.3 to −6.6; 2021-22 from an 11% forecast to 9.2 to 8.7; 2019-20 from 4.2 to 4.0. The −6.6 is computed from levels in ES 2022-23's Statistical Appendix; the Survey's text does not print it. Always give the vintage.
3. **Internal inconsistencies in ES 2020-21.** Cases at lockdown: 500 (V1) vs 100 (V2). Lockdown start: 24 vs 25 March; "40-day" lockdown vs 25 March-31 May. World deaths: "over 2 lakh" vs "over 21 lakh". World 2020 output: −4.3/−3.5 (V1) vs −4.4 (V2). Package: ₹29.87 lakh crore/15% vs ₹20 lakh crore vs 9% of GDP. Vaccination start printed as 2020. RBI's −7.5% projection vs NSO's −7.7%.
4. **Internal inconsistencies in ES 2021-22.** Vaccination 157/91 crore (summary) vs 156/88 crore (para 1.28). An RBI facility "announced on 31.04.2021". ECLGS ceiling ₹4.5 lakh crore vs ₹3 lakh crore a year earlier, because of later additions.
5. **Forecast vs outcome.** Second wave "avoided" (ES20) → severe second wave (ES21). Annual CA surplus "at least 2%" → 0.9%. 2021-22 growth 11% → 9.2 → 8.7. "Looking through" supply-shock inflation in 2020 (ES20) → CPI back within band in 2021 (ES21) → above band for ten months from January 2022 (ES22).
6. **Definitions.** FDI figures mix gross (DPIIT) and net (BoP) bases. NPA ratios are affected by the moratorium and asset-classification standstill, as ES 2020-21 itself warns.
7. **The "barbell" label.** It first appears in ES 2020-21 V1 para 1.25 (lockdown policy) and is restated in ES 2021-22 Ch 1 as the frame for the whole response, with the new term "Agile".
