# India: Economic Surveys 2010-11 to 2014-15 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of the "Uneven recovery" chapter (2010 to mid-2014: euro crisis, slow rich-world recovery, the 2013 taper tantrum). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book.

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2010-11, 2011-12, 2012-13, 2013-14 and 2014-15 (official English PDFs from indiabudget.gov.in; archive pages listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md`). Read from the `pdftotext -layout` extracts in `Econ Subject/sources/text/es<YEAR>.txt`.

**Data cut-offs, as the texts show them.** ES 2010-11 uses the CSO Advance Estimates of 7 February 2011. ES 2011-12 uses the Advance Estimates of 7 February 2012 and data to about mid-February 2012. ES 2012-13 uses Advance Estimates for 2012-13 and data to early February 2013. ES 2013-14 covers the full year 2013-14 with data to 20 June 2014 (it is the post-election Survey). ES 2014-15 is in two volumes, with data to about 13 February 2015, and introduces the new 2011-12-base GDP series.

**How pages are cited.** "p." is the **printed** page (running head or footer). "st." is the page of the stitched PDF (form feeds + 1).

| Survey | Chapters used | Printed vs stitched |
|---|---|---|
| 2010-11 | Ch 1 State of the Economy and Prospects; Ch 3 Fiscal Developments and Public Finance; Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management; Ch 6 Balance of Payments | printed = st. throughout (chapter-opening pages carry no number but fit the sequence) |
| 2011-12 | Ch 1; Ch 3 Fiscal Developments and Public Finance; Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management | printed = st. |
| 2012-13 | Ch 1; Ch 3 Public Finance; Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management; Ch 6 Balance of Payments; Ch 7 International Trade | printed = st. |
| 2013-14 | Ch 1 The State of the Economy; Ch 2 (Issues and Priorities); Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management; Ch 6 Balance of Payments; Ch 7 International Trade | printed = st. Layout is one main column plus side-notes, not two columns |
| 2014-15 | **Vol 1**: Ch 1 Economic Outlook, Prospects, and Policy Challenges; Ch 2 Fiscal Framework; Ch 4 The Investment Climate. **Vol 2**: Ch 1 State of the Economy-An Overview; Ch 4 Monetary Management and Financial Intermediation | Vol 1: printed = st. − 6. Vol 2: printed = st. − 150 (Vol 2 restarts at p. 1 on st. 151) |

In the 2010-11 to 2012-13 extracts the two columns run side by side, so lines from both columns are mixed. In the 2013-14 extract many ligatures come out split ("f iscal" for "fiscal"). I picked quotes that avoid split ligatures. Quotes that run across a line break are joined, and a word hyphenated at a line end is closed up. Each item has at most one verbatim quote, under 15 words. Everything else is paraphrase.

## Economic Survey 2010-11

**ES10-1 — The rebound, and industry's wobble played down.** Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 1), and paras 1.11-1.12, pp. 4-5 (st. 4-5).
Growth was 8.0% in 2009-10 (Quick Estimates, 31 January 2011) and 8.6% in 2010-11 (Advance Estimates, 7 February 2011), at factor cost in 2004-05 prices. GDP at constant market prices was put at 9.7% for 2010-11. Agriculture rebounded and manufacturing kept its momentum. The November 2010 IIP showed industry slowing. The Survey calls this a concern but short-run, more like bumps in the road than a long-run problem. Real GDP grew 8.9% in each of the first two quarters of 2010-11 (para 1.12).
Quote: "the turnaround has been fast and strong".

**ES10-2 — Two shocks, and Europe's fiscal stress already named.** Ch 1, paras 1.2-1.3, p. 1 (st. 1).
The economy is said to have withstood two shocks in quick succession. The first was the collapse in world growth, finance and trade in 2007-09, whose effects carried into 2010-11, with fiscal stress in Europe given in parentheses. The second was domestic: negative farm growth in 2008-09, drought in 2009-10 and unseasonal late rains in 2010-11.

**ES10-3 — Inflation: WPI peak of 11% in April 2010; food inflation 17.05% in January 2011.** Ch 1, paras 1.5-1.7, pp. 3-4 (st. 3-4).
Headline WPI inflation peaked at 11% (year-on-year) in April 2010. It was 7.48% in November 2010 and 8.23% in January 2011. Food-article inflation (weekly data) was in double digits for 76 weeks from 5 June 2009. It was above 20% in 22 of the 23 weeks between 15 January and 19 June 2010, and 17.05% on 22 January 2011. The drivers had changed. In 2009-10 they were pulses, cereals and sugar, blamed on the monsoon. In 2010-11 the Survey reads the cause as demand pressure, even though supply was higher. Para 1.7 adds outside pressure from high global commodity prices and easy money in several industrial countries. The IMF's baseline oil price was raised from US$79/bbl (WEO October 2010) to US$90/bbl (January 2011 update).
Quote: "this year inflation seems to be driven by demand factors".

**ES10-4 — Box 4.1 on the unexpected food-price surge.** Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management, Box 4.1, pp. 72-73 (st. 72-73).
Good rain in 2010 did bring cereal and pulse prices down. Vegetables, fruit, dairy, oilseeds and spices then drove food inflation back up. The Box gives three explanations. (1) The global food-price rise looks like a generalised commodity surge, not the result of Chinese or Indian demand or biofuels (it cites a July 2010 study by Baffes and Haniotis). (2) India's prices have been more stable than world prices. (3) For non-cereals the Box rejects pure Engel's-law demand and points to temporary local cartelisation and speculation. The example is onions, which rose from Rs 15 to over Rs 80 per kg in a matter of weeks; the Box blames APMC restrictions and mandi fees.
Quote: "Food inflation has been unexpectedly high in recent weeks".

**ES10-5 — RBI tightening: six moves in 2010-11, repo up 175 bps to 6.5%.** Ch 1, para 1.51, p. 17 (st. 17); Ch 4, paras 4.30 and 4.68, Table 4.17, pp. 82 and 96 (st. 82, 96).
The RBI began withdrawing accommodation in October 2009. Para 1.51 says policy rates were raised six times in the year. It says the repo rate rose a cumulative 175 bps to 6.5% and the reverse repo 225 bps to 5.5%, with the CRR at 6% of NDTL. Para 4.30 repeats this. On 25 January 2011 the repo was 6.5% and the reverse repo 5.5% (Ch 4, p. 81). The RBI's April 2010 projection of 5.5% WPI inflation for end-March 2011 was later revised to 7.0% (para 1.52).
Quote: "The RBI raised the policy rates six times during the current fiscal".
**FLAG (count vs size):** Table 4.17 (p. 96) lists six repo moves dated inside 2010-11: 20 April, 2 July, 27 July, 16 September and 2 November 2010, and 25 January 2011. They take the repo from 5.00% to 6.50%, which is 150 bps. The 175 bps figure is measured from 4.75%, so it includes the 19 March 2010 move, which falls in 2009-10. Counting that move makes seven hikes. Use "six" and "175 bps" only with this caveat. ES 2011-12 (ES11-6) counts from March 2010.

**ES10-6 — Fiscal exit begins.** Ch 1, paras 1.56-1.58, p. 18 (st. 18).
The Budget for 2010-11 went back to fiscal consolidation and partly withdrew the stimulus. The fiscal deficit was budgeted at 5.5% of GDP. The Medium Term Fiscal Policy Statement pointed to 4.8% in 2011-12 and 4.1% in 2012-13. Measured against the larger Advance-Estimate nominal GDP (Rs 78,77,947 crore), the 2010-11 target comes to 4.8%. The Survey credits buoyant revenue and the 3G/BWA spectrum auctions. The Thirteenth Finance Commission target was combined public debt of 68% of GDP by 2014-15. A November 2010 status paper projected central debt at 43% of GDP and a fiscal deficit of 3.0% in 2014-15.
Quote: "partial exit from the stimulus measures".

**ES10-7 — Subsidy reforms begun: oil bonds ended, nutrient-based fertiliser subsidy, fuel price rises.** Ch 3, paras 3.6 and 3.8, p. 44 (st. 44); Ch 4, paras 4.7 and 4.68, pp. 70 and 96 (st. 70, 96).
Issuing bonds outside the budget to cover oil companies' under-recoveries was stopped; the money now goes through the budget as cash expenditure. The Nutrient Based Subsidy policy for fertilisers took effect on 1 April 2010. Domestic administered petroleum prices were to be adjusted in line with world prices. Administered fuel prices were raised on 25 June 2010. PDS kerosene went up by Rs 3 per litre and domestic LPG by Rs 35 per cylinder (Delhi).

**ES10-8 — External: CAD doubles in H1 2010-11, but capital inflows cover it easily.** Ch 1, paras 1.44-1.48 and 1.50, pp. 16-17 (st. 16-17).
The current-account deficit was US$27.9bn in April-September 2010, against US$13.3bn a year earlier. Para 1.44 notes worries that the CAD was becoming unsustainable. Net capital flows were US$36.7bn in H1 (against US$23.0bn), mostly FII portfolio money, short-term trade credit and ECBs, while net FDI eased. Reserves were US$279.1bn at end-March 2010 and US$297.3bn at end-December 2010. The rupee moved within ₹44-47 per dollar between April and December 2010.
Quote: "the elevated levels of current account deficits were easily financed by rising capital flows".
Para 1.50 adds that concerns about sustainability had emerged.

**ES10-9 — The euro crisis seen from Delhi (Ch 6 opening).** Ch 6 Balance of Payments, opening summary and paras 6.2-6.7, pp. 134-135 (st. 134-135).
Peripheral euro-area debt could spread to banks and insurers. The Survey reports doubts that the EFSF is large enough to rescue Spain, or high-debt Italy and Belgium. Low interest rates and uncertain growth in the advanced economies push carry-trade money into emerging markets, causing asset bubbles, currency appreciation and inflation (para 6.7). The main risks it lists for India are volatile FII flows, slowing FDI and a further slowdown in advanced economies.
Quote: "The sovereign debt crisis in the peripheral euro-zone countries is contributing to the uncertainty".

**ES10-10 — Outlook: 9% (±0.25) in 2011-12; a double dip thought very unlikely.** Ch 1, paras 1.69-1.70, p. 21 (st. 21).
Real GDP was forecast to grow 9% (±0.25) in 2011-12, and the Survey expected growth to pass 9%. The risks named are weather, an oil-price spike, and uncertainty in Europe and the USA, plus Japan. India would be hurt by a serious crisis in a major industrial economy, but the baseline assumes none.
Quote: "a second dip recession is a very low probability event".
See ES11-1 for the outcome (6.9% AE).

## Economic Survey 2011-12

**ES11-1 — Growth falls to 6.9%, almost all of it industry.** Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 1); paras 1.2-1.3, pp. 1-3 (st. 1-3).
GDP growth is estimated at 6.9% in 2011-12 (AE, 7 February 2012), after 8.4% in each of the two previous years. Apart from 2008-09 (6.7%), this is the lowest in nine years. Manufacturing grew 2.7% in Q2 and 0.4% in Q3 of 2011-12, and industry 3.9% for the year. Para 1.2 records the miss: ES 2010-11 had expected about 9% (±0.25), and the Mid-Year Analysis had already cut this to 7.5%.
Quote: "India’s slowdown can be attributed almost entirely to weakening industrial growth".
**FLAG (revision):** 2009-10 and 2010-11 are now 8.4% each. ES 2010-11 had 8.0% (QE) and 8.6% (AE).

**ES11-2 — Explaining the slowdown: euro zone, oil, rate rises, politics.** Ch 1, paras 1.3, 1.5, 1.82-1.83, pp. 3-4 and 20 (st. 3-4, 20).
The global causes named are the euro-area crisis and near-recession in Europe, slow US growth, Japan's stagnation and high crude prices. The domestic cause is repo-rate rises to control inflation, which slowed investment and growth, especially in industry. Para 1.5 adds higher borrowing costs and other costs that squeezed internal accruals. Para 1.3: fiscal and monetary room is much narrower than in 2008-09. Para 1.82 adds persistent inflation above 9%. Para 1.83: GFCF/GDP in Q3 2011-12 was 30%, down from 32.3% a year earlier.
Quote: "pressures of democratic politics, which slowed reforms".
Note: the phrase "policy paralysis" does not occur in ES 2011-12 (searched).

**ES11-3 — The euro zone turns sharply adverse in September 2011.** Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1; paras 1.44, 1.49, 1.87, 1.89, pp. 13-14, 21-22 (st. 13-14, 21-22).
The global environment worsened sharply in September 2011 with the euro-zone turmoil, and rating agencies raised questions about the US outlook (opening summary). Greece's sovereign debt problem reached India as unusually high market volatility (para 1.44). Export growth was 40.6% in H1 2011-12 but slowed from October 2011, reaching −0.5% in November 2011 (para 1.49). The euro area could slow software exports (para 1.87). New regulatory requirements on European banks raise deleveraging fears. Indian banks have almost no direct exposure, but funding pressure could hit them indirectly (para 1.89). Para 1.44 also notes a recent rise in bank NPAs, while saying banks remain robust.
Quote: "turned sharply adverse in September 2011 owing to the turmoil in the eurozone".

**ES11-4 — Inflation sticky at about 9%; food inflation collapses to near zero.** Ch 1, paras 1.35-1.39, pp. 11-12 (st. 11-12).
Headline WPI inflation started 2011-12 at 9.7%, briefly reached double digits in September 2011, and fell to 6.6% in January 2012. Para 1.36 names three causes: (a) prices of vegetables, eggs, meat and fish, put down to changing diets; (b) high global prices of metals and chemicals; (c) Brent crude averaging about US$111/bbl in 2011, against US$80 in 2010. WPI food inflation fell from 20.2% (February 2010) to 1.6% (January 2012). CPI-IW was 10.1% in September 2011 and 5.3% in January 2012.

**ES11-5 — Box 4.2: protein-food inflation and the MGNREGA explanation questioned.** Ch 4, Box 4.2, p. 81 (st. 81).
The Box sets out the popular view, citing Subbarao 2011 and Gokarn 2011. That view holds that a dietary shift to protein foods, helped by MGNREGA-driven rural wages, weak supply response and global food inflation, drove food prices. The Box says the causes are more complicated. Incomes of the bottom two rural deciles would have to rise to the level of the sixth decile to add Rs 100 a month in per-capita protein spending. The average urban household spends far more on protein foods.
Quote: "the shift to more expensive proteins is very unlikely to be from rising incomes".

**ES11-6 — 13 repo hikes, 375 bps; then a pause and a CRR cut.** Ch 1, paras 1.41-1.42 and 1.88, pp. 13 and 22 (st. 13, 22).
The repo rate was raised 13 times, a cumulative 375 bps. In the Third Quarter Review the repo (8.5%) and reverse repo (7.5%) were left unchanged and the CRR was cut from 6.0% to 5.5%. A new Marginal Standing Facility was set 100 bps above the repo inside a fixed 200 bps corridor. The bank rate was aligned with the MSF at 9.5% on 13 February 2012. Para 1.88: the tightening slowed growth in the short term, as expected, and helped bring inflation down.
Quote: "The RBI hiked the repo rate 13 times between March 2010 and January 2012".
**FLAG (dates):** ES 2012-13 Table 4.13 (p. 97) shows the last hike on 25 October 2011 (to 8.50%). ES 2012-13 para 1.22 gives the same 375 bps for March 2010 to October 2011. The January 2012 end-date is the review date, not the date of a hike.

**ES11-7 — Rupee slide of 2011: low ₹54.23 on 15 December 2011; reserves fall from US$322bn.** Ch 1, paras 1.46 and 1.55-1.57, pp. 14-15 (st. 14-15).
On monthly averages the rupee fell 12.4% between March 2011 (₹44.97) and January 2012 (₹51.34). Point to point, it fell 16.2% from 31 March to 30 December 2011 (₹53.26). From its peak of ₹43.94 (27 July 2011) to its low of ₹54.23 (15 December 2011) the fall was 19.0%. Para 1.46 blames weak FII inflows and, above all, foreign investors fleeing to safety amid Europe's troubles and emerging-market inflation. Reserves hit an all-time high of US$322.0bn at end-August 2011 and were US$292.8bn at end-January 2012, partly because the RBI intervened to slow the rupee's fall (para 1.56).
Quote: "much of the depreciation was due to ‘flight to safety’ by foreign investors".

**ES11-8 — External: oil and gold widen the CAD.** Ch 1, paras 1.50 and 1.52-1.54, pp. 14-15 (st. 14-15).
The H1 2011-12 CAD was US$32.8bn, against US$29.6bn a year earlier. The trade deficit was 9.4% of GDP, against 8.9%, mainly because import prices for oil, gold and silver rose. In April-January 2011-12, imports of POL rose 38.8% (to US$118bn) and of gold and silver 46.2% (to US$50bn). Portfolio inflows fell to US$1.3bn in H1, from US$23.8bn. FDI (US$12.3bn) and ECBs (US$10.6bn) rose.
**FLAG (revision):** Key Indicators (p. 2) puts the 2010-11 CAD at 2.7% of GDP. ES 2012-13 and ES 2013-14 later give 2.8%.

**ES11-9 — Fiscal: 4.8% in 2010-11, slippage expected in 2011-12.** Ch 1, paras 1.60 and 1.64-1.67, pp. 16-17 (st. 16-17).
The fiscal deficit fell from 6.5% of GDP in 2009-10 to 4.8% in 2010-11, and 4.6% was budgeted for 2011-12. By April-December 2011 the fiscal deficit had reached 92.3% of BE. Corporation tax grew 6%, against the 20.2% budgeted, and expenditure grew 13.9%, against 4.9% budgeted. The Survey blames crude prices with little room to raise domestic prices, missed disinvestment and lower tax revenue. Para 1.67 calls 2011-12 an exceptional year.
Quote: "a slippage is likely in the revenue and fiscal deficits budgeted this year".
**FLAG (revision):** the 2009-10 fiscal deficit is 6.5% here; ES 2010-11 Key Indicators gave 6.3%.

**ES11-10 — Subsidies above 2% of GDP; fuel pass-through limited.** Ch 3, para 3.28, Figure 3.5, pp. 60-61 (st. 60-61); Box (Twelfth Plan resources), p. 63 (st. 63).
Major subsidies were Rs 1,31,212 crore in 2010-11 and budgeted at Rs 1,34,411 crore for 2011-12. Given crude prices, the outcome was expected to be much higher. With headline inflation high, global prices were only partly passed on to domestic prices. Major subsidies were above 2% of GDP in 2008-09 and 2009-10. The Nilekani Task Force's direct-transfer proposal is noted in Box 3.2.
**FLAG (internal):** para 3.28 puts BE 2011-12 subsidies at 1.5% of GDP. The p. 63 box puts them at an estimated 1.6% of GDP. The two may use different definitions (major vs total subsidies); neither says.

**ES11-11 — Forecast: 7.6% (±0.25) for 2012-13, 8.6% for 2013-14.** Ch 1, paras 1.82-1.83, p. 20 (st. 20).
The Survey endorses the 6.9% AE for 2011-12 and forecasts 7.6% (±0.25) for 2012-13 and 8.6% for 2013-14. The assumptions are a normal monsoon, stable oil prices, fiscal consolidation back on track, and RBI rate cuts as inflation eases.
See ES12-1 (5.0% AE for 2012-13) and ES13-1 (4.7% for 2013-14).

## Economic Survey 2012-13

**ES12-1 — The diagnosis: domestic causes as well as external ones.** Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 1); paras 1.2-1.5 and 1.64, pp. 1-3 and 20 (st. 1-3, 20).
Growth was 8.6% (2009-10) and 9.3% (2010-11), then 6.2% (2011-12) and 5.0% (2012-13 AE). The ten-year CAGR is 7.9%. Para 1.5 gives three reasons. First, the post-crisis monetary and fiscal stimulus was large: final consumption grew more than 8% a year in 2009-10 to 2011-12, which produced inflation and a strong monetary response. Second, from 2011-12 corporate and infrastructure investment slowed because of bottlenecks and tighter money. Third, two further shocks hit: a world economy held back by the euro-area crisis and US fiscal uncertainty, and a weak monsoon. Para 1.64 describes a vicious circle in which slower growth and the withdrawal of stimulus feed each other.
Quote: "the boost to consumption, coupled with supply-side constraints, led to higher inflation".

**ES12-2 — Investment: rate rises, weak exports, and policy bottlenecks.** Ch 1, paras 1.21-1.23 and 1.26, pp. 8-9 (st. 8-9).
The investment rate fell from 36.8% of GDP (2010-11) to 35.0% (2011-12, First Revised Estimates). The private corporate share of investment fell from 36.1% to 29.8%; corporate investment was about Rs 90,000 crore lower. Three reasons are given: repo up 375 bps between March 2010 and October 2011, weaker export demand from advanced economies, and policy bottlenecks (environmental permissions, fuel linkages, land acquisition) that stalled big projects. Para 1.26 singles out the sharp fall in corporate investment, because it supplies future capacity and growth.
Quote: "Particularly worrisome is the sharp slowing of corporate investment".

**ES12-3 — Box 1.1: stalled projects up, new projects down.** Ch 1, Box 1.1, p. 8 (st. 8).
The Box uses CMIE Capex data. Stalled projects have risen in value and volume since early 2009. At December 2012, six sectors held about 80% of stalled projects: electricity, roads, telecom services, steel, real estate and mining. New government projects peaked in March 2010 and private ones two quarters later. A CMIE study found that 20 projects made up almost 70% of the cost of projects shelved in 2011-12. The main causes were land acquisition, coal linkages and mining bans, along with telecom spectrum policy. High interest rates contributed. Remedies listed: the Cabinet Committee on Investments for projects over Rs 1,000 crore, the LARR Bill, PSU investment, and the Delhi-Mumbai Industrial Corridor.
Quote: "Lack of growth and slowdown in investment are feeding into each other".
Contrast ES14-5: by 2014-15 the Survey finds private stalling driven mainly by market conditions, not clearances.

**ES12-4 — Savings fall to an eight-year low of 30.8%; households move to physical assets and gold.** Ch 1, paras 1.42-1.44 and 1.49-1.50, Table 1.7, Box 1.4, pp. 14-17 (st. 14-17).
Gross domestic saving peaked at 36.8% of GDP (2007-08) and fell to 30.8% (2011-12), an eight-year low. Household financial saving fell from 10.4% of GDP to 8.0%, and was 36% of household saving in 2011-12 against about 55% in the 1990s. Para 1.49: GFCF fell more than 2 points of GDP between 2007-08 and 2011-12, but saving fell about 6 points, so the gap is the CAD. Box 1.4 links high inflation and weak equity returns to gold becoming a safe haven.

**ES12-5 — Fiscal slippage of 2011-12, the Kelkar Committee, and FRBM amendments.** Ch 1, paras 1.31-1.33, pp. 10-12 (st. 10-12); Ch 3, opening summary and paras 3.4-3.7, pp. 56-57 (st. 56-57).
The central fiscal deficit was 6.0% (2008-09) and 6.5% (2009-10). It fell to 4.8% (2010-11) partly because nominal GDP grew more than 20%. It then widened to 5.7% in 2011-12 (provisional actuals), against a budget of 4.6% (ES11-9). The causes: slower industry hit corporate tax, inflation and tight money hit investment, disinvestment fell short, and oil and fertiliser subsidies were not passed through. Ch 3 para 3.7: receipts explain about 58% of the 2011-12 slippage and expenditure 42%. Ch 1 para 1.32 splits receipts into 35% revenue and 23% disinvestment and loan recovery, which adds up to the same 58%. Ch 3 paras 3.4-3.5: the Budget 2012-13 Finance Bill amended the FRBM Act. It introduced the effective revenue deficit (which excludes grants for capital assets) and a Medium Term Expenditure Framework Statement.
Quote: "the government appointed a committee headed by Dr Vijay Kelkar".
**FLAG (internal):** para 1.32 gives the 2011-12 fiscal deficit as 5.7% of GDP; para 1.49 (p. 17) gives 5.8%.

**ES12-6 — The October 2012 roadmap: 4.8% in 2013-14, 3.0% by 2016-17; diesel and LPG.** Ch 1, paras 1.34-1.35 and 1.38, Box 1.3, pp. 13-14 (st. 13-14).
The roadmap set a fiscal deficit of 4.8% of GDP for 2013-14, then cuts of 0.6 points a year to 3.0% in 2016-17. Diesel prices were raised in September 2012. From January 2013 oil companies could raise them in small regular steps. Subsidised LPG cylinders were capped at nine. Subsidies reached Rs 1,66,824 crore in April-December 2012, 92.9% of BE. The Mid-Year Analysis expected the 2012-13 slippage to be only 0.2 percentage point. Box 1.3 (Banerjee and Chiplunkar): the poorest rural quintile gets 0.07% of LPG subsidies and the richest 52.6%.
Quote: "Open-ended commitments such as uncapped subsidies are particularly problematic for fiscal credibility".

**ES12-7 — Inflation: core inflation down, food (now cereals) up; CPI near double digits.** Ch 1, paras 1.52-1.55, pp. 17-18 (st. 17-18).
WPI inflation was 7.18% in December 2012, a three-year low. It averaged 7.55% in April-December 2012, against 9.35% a year earlier. Core inflation fell from 8.35% (November 2011) to 4.24% (December 2012). Food inflation reached double digits again in December 2012. This time cereals drove it, reaching 17.05% in Q3, because of higher MSPs and too little open-market wheat. In 2011-12 protein foods had driven it. The CPI-WPI gap widened to 3.91 points. Para 1.55 sets out the RBI's dilemma. Standard theory says look through supply-driven food prices and set rates on core inflation. But entrenched CPI inflation pushes savers toward gold.

**ES12-8 — Monetary turn: 50 bps cut in April 2012, 25 bps in January 2013.** Ch 4, para 4.40, Table 4.13, p. 97 (st. 97).
From January 2010 to October 2011 the repo rate rose 375 bps, from 4.75% to 8.5%. The RBI's stance shifted from October 2011. It cut the repo 50 bps on 17 April 2012 (to 8.00%) and 25 bps on 29 January 2013 (to 7.75%), and cut the CRR in steps to 4.00% (9 February 2013).
**FLAG (cross-Survey):** para 4.40 puts the April 2010 inflation peak at 10.9%. ES 2010-11 para 1.5 gave 11% (likely revised data, or rounding).

**ES12-9 — CAD: a record 4.2% of GDP in 2011-12, 4.6% in H1 2012-13.** Ch 1, paras 1.28-1.29, Table 1.6, pp. 9-10 (st. 9-10); Ch 6, para 6.13, p. 133 (st. 133); Box 6.1, p. 135 (st. 135).
The 2011-12 CAD was US$78.2bn, or 4.2% of GDP, against US$48.1bn (2.8%) in 2010-11. This was the highest ever both in dollars and as a share of GDP (para 6.13). The trade deficit was US$189.8bn (10.2% of GDP). In H1 2012-13 the CAD was US$39.0bn (4.6% of GDP) and the trade deficit 10.8%. Box 6.1 explains the euro channel: exports fell but oil imports held up, unlike after Lehman. In 2008-09 the CAD was only 2.3% because invisibles covered more of the trade gap.
Quote: "The CAD widened to its highest ever level".

**ES12-10 — Gold: Box 1.2 on the gold rush, and the first duty rise.** Ch 1, Box 1.2, pp. 11-12 (st. 11-12); para 1.25, p. 9; para 1.57, p. 18 (st. 18).
Box 1.2: the value of gold imports rose ninefold between January 2008 and October 2012. Gold returned 23.7% a year (April 2007 to March 2012), against 7.3% for the Nifty and 8.2% for savings deposits. Little can be done about oil imports, so the Box says gold is what must be held down to bring the CAD back to comfort. It treats gold demand as a symptom of inflation and of poor access to financial products. Para 1.25: valuables rose from 2.8% (2007-08) to 7.6% (2011-12) of total investment. Para 1.57: import duty on gold was raised from 4% to 6%.
Quote: "the government has 'thrown sand in the wheels'".

**ES12-11 — Box 7.2: gold duty steps and the CAD counterfactual.** Ch 7 International Trade, Box 7.2, p. 155 (st. 155).
Gold was 11.3% of imports and 30% of the trade deficit in 2011-12. Import value rose 39.0% and quantity 11.2%. Duty steps: in Budget 2012-13 standard gold went from 2% to 4% and non-standard gold from 5% to 10%; on 21 January 2013 gold and platinum went from 4% to 6%. The RBI working group is cited: had gold imports grown 24% instead of 39%, the CAD would have been about US$6bn lower, and 3.9% of GDP instead of 4.2%. Gold imports in April-December 2012 fell 14.7% to US$38.02bn.

**ES12-12 — Rupee: all-time low of ₹57.22 on 27 June 2012; growing reliance on debt flows.** Ch 1, paras 1.58-1.62, pp. 18-20 (st. 18-20).
The rupee hit an all-time low of ₹57.22 on 27 June 2012, down 10.6% from ₹51.16 on 30 March 2012. It recovered to ₹51.62 on 5 October 2012, and from October 2012 to January 2013 the monthly average was ₹53.02-54.78. The REER fell about 11% from mid-2011. Net capital inflows in H1 2012-13 were US$40.0bn (4.8% of GDP), with NRI deposits at US$9.4bn and portfolio at US$5.8bn. External debt was US$365.3bn at end-September 2012. Para 1.62 warns about firms that leave foreign borrowing unhedged to get cheap money.

**ES12-13 — Euro crisis and risk-on/risk-off flows (Box 6.2).** Ch 6, Box 6.2, p. 136 (st. 136).
The US downgrade (early August 2011) and a worsening euro crisis brought net FII outflows of US$3.7bn (August-October 2011). The ECB's LTRO (more than €1 trillion, December 2011 and February 2012) brought US$16.9bn of FII inflows (December 2011-February 2012). As the euro crisis worsened and Greek exit loomed, US$2.3bn flowed out (March-June 2012). Conditions improved from July 2012 after the ECB President's pledge, the banking-union proposal and the ESM launch. US QE is named as an additional push factor for inflows.
Quote: "The main fallout of euro zone crisis is global uncertainty".

**ES12-14 — NPAs rising: 2.36% (March 2011) to 3.57% (September 2012).** Ch 1, paras 1.65-1.66, p. 20 (st. 20).
Gross NPAs rose from 2.36% of credit (March 2011) to 3.57% (September 2012), with the sharpest rise in industry and infrastructure. The sectors named are textiles, chemicals, iron and steel, food processing, construction and telecoms. The rise is larger at public-sector banks. Part of it is technical (public banks moving to system-based identification). The Survey calls for distress resolution that removes ineffective promoters and imposes losses.
Quote: "stress also stems from slow growth and project delays".

**ES12-15 — Outlook 6.1-6.7% for 2013-14; investor risk tolerance flagged.** Ch 1, paras 1.74-1.76, pp. 21-22 (st. 21-22).
Little support is expected from the world economy. Two downside risks are named: shifts in international investors' risk tolerance, and oil prices. Growth of 6.1-6.7% is forecast for 2013-14, assuming a normal monsoon, easing inflation and RBI relaxation, and a mild global recovery. The Survey says the range is wide because forecasting at a possible turning point is hard.
Quote: "India is exposed to shifts in the risk tolerance of international investors".
Outcome: 4.7% (ES13-1), later 6.9% in the new series (ES14-7).

## Economic Survey 2013-14

**ES13-1 — Two years below 5%, the first time in 25 years.** Ch 1, opening summary and paras 1.2-1.3, pp. 1-3 (st. 1-3); Table 1.1, p. 4 (st. 4); para 1.8, p. 6 (st. 6).
GDP at factor cost (2004-05 prices) grew 4.5% in 2012-13 (1R) and 4.7% in 2013-14 (PE). Average growth fell from 8.3% a year in 2004-05 to 2011-12 to 4.6% in the last two years. Over the same period emerging and developing economies slowed from 6.8% to 4.9% (calendar years), so India's slowdown is the deeper one. Manufacturing averaged 0.2% a year in 2012-13 and 2013-14. Causes given: the euro-area crisis, the general global slowdown, domestic structural constraints and inflation.
Quote: "last witnessed a quarter of a century ago".
**FLAG (revision):** para 1.8 says 2011-12 manufacturing growth was revised from 2.7% to 7.4% once ASI data came in. That weakens the story of collapsing manufacturing that ES 2011-12 and ES 2012-13 told for that year. Table 1.1 also puts 2011-12 GDP at 6.7% (2R, against 6.2% in ES 2012-13) and 2010-11 at 8.9% (against 9.3% in ES 2012-13).

**ES13-2 — Structural constraints and the investment slump (Boxes 1.1 and 1.4).** Ch 1, Box 1.1, p. 4 (st. 4); paras 1.17 and 1.22-1.24, Box 1.4, pp. 8-11 (st. 8-11).
Box 1.1 asks how fixed investment of about 30% of GDP can coexist with growth below 5%. Its constraints include slow decisions on projects (delays, missing complementary investment), ill-targeted subsidies, a weak capital-goods base and labour-absorption problems. Fixed capital formation fell 2.1 points of GDP between 2012-13 and 2013-14, to 28.3%, mainly in private corporate machinery and equipment. Box 1.4 cites policy uncertainty (land, environmental clearances, coal linkages, mining bans). It also cites Anand and Tulin (IMF 2014): real interest rates explain only a quarter of the investment slowdown, and uncertainty and weak confidence explain much of the rest.
Quote: "they account for only one quarter of the explained investment downturn".

**ES13-3 — Hindsight: the stimulus was excessive.** Ch 2, paras 2.17-2.18, p. 32 (st. 32).
Inflation began rising after 2005-06, driven by credit growth that came from the difficulty of sterilising foreign-exchange intervention. The post-crisis stimulus kept output above trend from the first quarter of 2010 and kept the output gap positive until early 2012.
Quote: "proved to be excessive and fostered high inflation".
**FLAG (likely typo):** the sentence dates the excessive stimulus to 2011-12. The context (post-crisis stimulus, output above trend from Q1 2010) points to 2008-09 to 2010-11. Check the PDF before quoting the year.

**ES13-4 — Over-leveraged firms, stalled projects, bad assets.** Ch 2, paras 2.19-2.21, pp. 32-33 (st. 32-33).
Many projects were started in 2004-07 on fast credit growth. When conditions changed, firms that had over-leveraged in the pre-crisis credit boom could not finish projects, settle claims or repay loans. Costs escalated and banks were left with bad assets. The Cabinet Committee on Investment was set up in January 2013 with the Prime Minister as chairman.
Quote: "they faced balance sheet distress".
This is the origin of the ES 2014-15 balance-sheet-syndrome theme (ES14-6).

**ES13-5 — NPAs at 3.90% by March 2014; five stressed sectors.** Ch 1, para 1.87, p. 26 (st. 26).
Gross NPAs rose from 2.36% of credit (March 2011) to 3.90% (March 2014, provisional). The RBI's Financial Stability Report (December 2013) named five stressed sectors: infrastructure, iron and steel, textiles, aviation and mining. Public-sector banks are heavily exposed to them.
Quote: "the deteriorating asset quality of the banking sector emerged as a major concern".

**ES13-6 — Fiscal: 4.9% (2012-13) and 4.5% (2013-14), achieved by spending cuts.** Ch 1, paras 1.4 and 1.29-1.32, pp. 3 and 12-13 (st. 3, 12-13).
The fiscal deficit was 5.7% of GDP in 2011-12, 4.9% in 2012-13 and 4.5% in 2013-14, against a target of 4.8%. Tax and disinvestment revenue fell short, and subsidies, interest and pensions ran over budget. Major subsidies were Rs 2,47,596 crore in 2013-14 (CGA provisional). Total subsidies were 1.42% of GDP (2007-08), 2.56% (2012-13) and 2.26% (2013-14 RE). Petrol is decontrolled; diesel rises Rs 0.50 per litre a month; the LPG cap went from 9 to 12 cylinders from April 2014.
Quote: "Much of this improvement has been achieved by reduction in expenditure".

**ES13-7 — Inflation: WPI 6.0% in 2013-14, CPI 9-10%; protein foods now put down to rising incomes.** Ch 1, paras 1.3 and 1.38-1.42, pp. 3 and 15-16 (st. 3, 15-16).
WPI inflation averaged 6.0% in 2013-14, against 8.9% (2011-12) and 7.4% (2012-13). Food-article inflation averaged 12.2% a year in the five years to 2013-14. Core inflation was 2.9%. CPI (combined) held at around 9-10% and fell to 8.0% in February 2014. Para 1.40 puts protein-item inflation down to higher consumption as incomes rise, which differs from the scepticism of ES 2011-12 Box 4.2 (ES11-5). Para 1.41 blames APMC Acts and layers of middlemen. Fuel inflation was in double digits in the last three quarters (electricity tariff revisions, diesel pass-through, rupee depreciation).

**ES13-8 — The taper signal: May 2013, and the FOMC of 22 May.** Ch 1, para 1.44, p. 16 (st. 16); Ch 4, paras 4.21 and 4.50, pp. 80 and 86 (st. 80, 86); Ch 6, para 6.9, p. 107 (st. 107).
The RBI began 2013-14 with a 25 bps repo cut. In May 2013 there were signs that the Fed would taper. The FOMC signal of 22 May 2013 set off a sell-off in government securities, and the 10-year yield touched 9.27% on 19 August 2013 (para 4.50). Para 6.9: from the second half of May, markets took the Fed's communication to mean action was close, and money left debt markets worldwide. Countries with large CADs saw bigger outflows. In India the outflows were concentrated in FII debt. The Fed chairman is not named.
Quote: "13.0 per cent depreciation of the rupee between May 2013 and August 2013".

**ES13-9 — RBI's July 2013 defence: MSF up 200 bps to 10.25%, liquidity squeeze; unwound from 20 September.** Ch 1, paras 1.45-1.48, pp. 16-17 (st. 16-17); Ch 4, paras 4.22-4.25, pp. 80-81 (st. 80-81).
The mid-July 2013 measures: (1) the MSF rate went up 200 bps to 10.25%, so the repo-MSF corridor was 300 bps; (2) MSF/LAF access was capped at 0.5% of NDTL; (3) the minimum daily CRR balance was raised; (4) weekly cash-management-bill auctions were held. The call rate rose to the MSF rate, which became the effective policy rate. The FOMC did not begin tapering in September as expected, and from 20 September 2013 the RBI unwound the measures (para 4.23). The MSF came down to 8.75% in three steps by 29 October 2013. The repo went up 25 bps twice to 7.75%, and to 8% on 28 January 2014. It was held at 8% on 3 June 2014, with the SLR cut from 23% to 22.5%.
**FLAG (internal):** Ch 1 para 1.45 says the minimum daily CRR rose from 70% to 99%. Ch 4 para 4.22(3) says from 70% to 90%. Both then say it was cut back from 99% to 95%. So 90 is probably a typo for 99; check against RBI.
Note: para 4.21 gives no repo level for the start of 2013-14 after the 25 bps cut. Do not infer one.

**ES13-10 — Rupee: ₹68.36 on 28 August 2013; ₹63.75 average in September; ₹61 by March 2014.** Ch 1, paras 1.4 and 1.59, pp. 3 and 19-20 (st. 3, 19-20); Ch 6, para 6.29, Table 6.5, pp. 113-114 (st. 113-114).
The rupee fell to ₹68.36 per dollar on 28 August 2013 and averaged ₹61 in March 2014. It fell more than 5% month on month in June and August 2013. Monthly averages (Table 6.5): ₹54.38 (April 2013), ₹58.40 (June), ₹63.21 (August), ₹63.75 (September), ₹61.62 (October), ₹61.01 (March 2014). The 2013-14 average was ₹60.50, against ₹54.41 in 2012-13. The RBI's net dollar sales were US$2,252mn (June 2013), US$5,976mn (July), US$2,464mn (August) and US$3,548mn (September); it was a net buyer from October.
Quote: "triggered by the expected taper of quantitative easing in the United States".

**ES13-11 — Government and RBI response: gold duty to 10%, the FCNR(B) swap window (US$34bn).** Ch 1, para 1.54, p. 19 (st. 19); Ch 6, para 6.10, p. 108 (st. 108); Annex (customs changes), p. 59 (st. 59).
Measures: restrictions on non-essential imports such as gold; customs duty on gold and silver raised to a peak of 10%; quasi-sovereign bonds; liberalised ECBs. The RBI opened a special swap window for FCNR(B) deposits and banks' overseas borrowing. Para 6.10 notes that without these one-off receipts, net inflows in 2013-14 would have fallen that much further. The p. 59 customs list shows gold duty raised from 8% to 10% and silver from 6% to 10%; no date is given in the lines read.
Quote: "through which US$ 34 billion was mobilized".
Not found: the 80:20 gold import rule is not mentioned (searched ES 2013-14 and ES 2014-15). The step from 6% to 8% duty is not dated in the text read.

**ES13-12 — CAD: a historic peak of 4.7% (US$88.2bn) in 2012-13, down to 1.7% (US$32.4bn).** Ch 1, paras 1.4 and 1.52-1.56, pp. 3 and 18-19 (st. 3, 18-19); Ch 6, paras 6.8 and 6.11-6.13, Table 6.2, pp. 107-109 (st. 107-109).
The CAD was US$78.2bn (4.2% of GDP) in 2011-12 and US$88.2bn (4.7%) in 2012-13. Para 1.52 calls these levels dangerously unsustainable. The balance-of-payments stress began in 2011-12, the Survey says, as a result of the euro-area crisis together with inelastic demand for oil and gold. Para 6.13: the 2012-13 ratio also reflects dollar GDP staying at about US$1.8 trillion because the rupee fell. In 2013-14 the CAD fell to US$32.4bn (1.7% of GDP). The trade deficit (BoP basis) fell from 10.5% to 7.9% of GDP.
Quote: "to a historic peak of 4.7 per cent in 2012-13".

**ES13-13 — Gold imports from 1,078 to 664 tonnes; what closed the trade gap.** Ch 1, paras 1.27, 1.50 and 1.55, pp. 12 and 18-19 (st. 12, 18-19); Ch 6, para 6.11, p. 109 (st. 109); Ch 7, para 7.19, p. 125 (st. 125).
Gold imports were 1,078 tonnes (2011-12), 1,037 tonnes (2012-13) and 664 tonnes (2013-14). Gold and silver imports by value were US$42.6bn (2010-11), a peak of US$61.6bn (2011-12, para 6.11) and US$55.8bn (2012-13). They fell 40.1% to US$33.4bn in 2013-14. Of the US$48.0bn cut in the BoP trade deficit, lower gold and silver imports gave about 47%, lower non-POL non-gold imports 40% and higher exports 25%; higher POL and other imports took away 12%. Valuables fell from 2.6% to 1.5% of GDP.

**ES13-14 — Flows in 2013-14: outflow in July-September, swap-led surge in October-December.** Ch 1, paras 1.4 and 1.57-1.58, pp. 3 and 19 (st. 3, 19); Ch 6, paras 6.23-6.25, Table 6.3, pp. 112-113 (st. 112-113).
Net capital flows fell from US$92.0bn (2012-13) to US$47.9bn (2013-14). Within the year: Q2 (July-September 2013) saw a reserve drawdown of US$10.4bn even though the CAD fell sharply; Q3 saw a US$19.1bn accretion as FII flows turned and NRI deposits poured in through the swap. NRI deposits for the year were US$38.9bn and portfolio US$4.8bn. Reserves rose from about US$275bn in early September 2013 to US$304.2bn at end-March 2014 and US$314.9bn on 20 June 2014. The BoP accretion was US$15.5bn but the stock rose only US$12.2bn, the difference being valuation losses including gold.

**ES13-15 — Verdict: high-deficit countries hit hardest; rupee steady once the taper came.** Ch 2, paras 2.23 and 2.26, pp. 33-34 (st. 33-34); Ch 1, para 1.4, p. 3 (st. 3); Ch 6, para 6.28, p. 113 (st. 113).
Para 2.26: in June-August 2013, countries with high fiscal deficits and high CADs faced the most pressure on their currencies. The taper and the sudden fall of the rupee added to business uncertainty. Para 2.23: India, Brazil and Turkey raised interest rates. Para 1.4: India is now in better shape to face global policy reversals. Para 6.28 blames the May 2013 vulnerability on the elevated CAD and heavy withdrawals from FII debt.
Quote: "the rupee became resilient when the US Fed taper actually happened subsequently".
Not found: "fragile five" does not appear in any of the five Surveys (searched all five extracts).

**ES13-16 — External debt jumps on the one-off deposit drive.** Ch 1, para 1.60, p. 20 (st. 20).
External debt was US$360.8bn at end-March 2012, US$404.9bn at end-March 2013 and US$426.0bn at end-December 2013. The Survey links the rise to the RBI's one-off deposit mobilisation.
**FLAG (cross-Survey):** ES 2012-13 para 1.61 put the end-September 2012 stock at US$365.3bn, about US$20.0bn above end-March 2012. That implies about US$345bn for end-March 2012, against US$360.8bn here. This is a revision of the end-March 2012 stock; check which figure the RBI now uses.

## Economic Survey 2014-15

**ES14-1 — Most vulnerable country in 2012: the macro-vulnerability index.** Vol 1 Ch 1, section 1.2, p. 3 (st. 9).
The MVI combines the fiscal deficit, the CAD and inflation. In 2012 India's value was 22.4: inflation 10.2%, budget deficit 7.5% and CAD 4.7% of GDP. The CAD had fallen from a quarterly peak of 6.7% of GDP (Q3 2012-13) to an expected 1.0% in the coming year. Inflation had fallen more than 6 points since late 2013. Turkey had passed India by 2014.
Quote: "In 2012, India was the most vulnerable country as measured by its index value".
Note: the 7.5% budget deficit is not defined in the lines read; it is presumably general government, not the Centre's 4.9%. Do not mix it with the central-government series.

**ES14-2 — The near-crisis of July/August 2013.** Vol 1 Ch 2 Fiscal Framework, section 2.2 Background and History, p. 48 (st. 54); section 2.1, p. 44 (st. 50).
The Survey's own retrospective: the 2013 near-crisis came from the Fed's decision to taper together with India's growing CAD, high inflation and still-large fiscal deficits, which drove capital out of the country. The lesson drawn is that India needs more fiscal space as a buffer. Section 2.1: weak spending control, and so too little fiscal space, contributed to the near-crisis.
Quote: "India experienced a near-crisis during July/August 2013".

**ES14-3 — Three phases of fiscal history; excessive counter-cyclical policy.** Vol 1 Ch 1, p. 20 (st. 26); Vol 1 Ch 2, section 2.2, p. 48 (st. 54).
Ch 1: the first phase (2002-08) improved every fiscal aggregate. Weak control of revenue spending at its end, together with too much counter-cyclical policy in the second phase (2009-12), led to a loss of fiscal control. The third phase runs from 2013 to the present. Ch 2: the second phase began with Lehman in 2008-09 and lasted four years. The deficit rose about 4 points, split between tax cuts and spending. In 2008-09 to 2011-12 subsidies rose about 1 point of GDP, the Sixth Pay Commission added 0.4 and MGNREGA 0.3. Inflation and financial repression held down the government's real cost of borrowing.
Quote: "excessive counter-cyclical policies in the second phase (2009-12)".
**FLAG (internal):** the two chapters date the phases differently. Ch 1 has 2009-12 and then 2013 onward; Ch 2 has 2008-09 for four years, then 2012-13 to 2014-15. They also differ on the tax-GDP gain over the boom: about 2-2.5 points in Ch 2 section 2.1 (p. 44), against about 2.7 points (9.2% in 2003-04 to 11.9% in 2007-08) in Ch 1 (p. 20).

**ES14-4 — Kelkar Committee (2012) and the 3% anchor.** Vol 1 Ch 2, section 2.3.2, p. 48 (st. 54); Vol 1 Ch 1, p. 20 (st. 26).
The FRBM Act and the Kelkar Committee (2012) both set the aim of a central fiscal deficit of 3% of GDP. The government keeps the 2014-15 target of 4.1%, helped by new excise on diesel and petrol (about Rs 20,000 crore), lower subsidies and spending cuts. The Survey argues for the golden rule: eliminate the revenue deficit and borrow over the cycle only for investment.

**ES14-5 — Stalled projects: Rs 8.8 lakh crore (7% of GDP); market conditions, not clearances, in the private sector.** Vol 1 Ch 4, sections 4.1 and 4.2.1-4.2.2, pp. 66-67 (st. 72-73); Table 4.4, p. 71 (st. 77).
GFCF growth fell from a peak of 24% (Q4 2009-10) to around zero. At end-December 2014 stalled projects stood at Rs 8.8 lakh crore, 7% of GDP, down from 8.3% a year earlier and 8-9% over the previous three fiscal years. The private sector held Rs 7 lakh crore and the public sector Rs 1.8 lakh crore. For every Rs 100 under implementation, Rs 10.3 was stalled, and Rs 16 in the private sector. Regulatory reasons explain most public stalling. Clearing the top 100 projects would deal with 83% of the problem by value. (Section 4.2.2, headed "Tapering", is about the stalling rate, not the Fed.)
Quote: "Unfavourable market conditions (and not regulatory clearances) are stalling a large number of projects".
**Contrast:** ES 2012-13 Box 1.1 (ES12-3) blamed land, coal and mining bans first.

**ES14-6 — Balance sheet syndrome with Indian characteristics.** Vol 1 Ch 4, section 4.4, pp. 71-72 (st. 77-78).
Corporate balance sheets remain over-extended (citing the Mid-Year Economic Analysis 2014-15). Credit Suisse data on 3,700 listed firms: about 33% of US$450bn of debt (US$140bn) sits with firms whose interest coverage ratio is below 1, against 17% four years earlier. What the Survey calls Indian about it: debt overhang alongside 6-7% growth and high inflation, public exposure through PPPs, and lending almost entirely by public-sector banks, with rising NPAs.
Quote: "a classic debt overhang problem in the aftermath of a debt fuelled investment bubble".
Not found: the phrase "twin balance sheet" does not appear in ES 2014-15 (searched). The term used is balance sheet syndrome.

**ES14-7 — New GDP series rewrites 2012-14: 5.1% and 6.9%, which the Survey finds puzzling.** Vol 2 Ch 1, para 1.3, Table 1.1, p. 4 (st. 154); Vol 1 Ch 1, Box 1.1, p. 6 (st. 12).
On the new 2011-12 base, GDP at constant market prices grew 5.1% (2012-13) and 6.9% (2013-14). The 2004-05 series (May 2014) gave 4.7% and 5.0%. GVA growth is 0.4 points higher in 2012-13 and 1.9 points higher in 2013-14. Vol 2 finds the 2013-14 figure puzzling, because savings and investment ratios were low and real imports fell 8.4%. New-series manufacturing grew 6.2% (2012-13) and 5.3% (2013-14), against 1.1% and −0.7% in the old series (ES13-1).
Quote (Vol 1 Box 1.1): "2013-14 was a crisis year".
Note for the book: the growth numbers used at the time (ES 2013-14) and the current official series disagree for exactly the taper-tantrum year.

**ES14-8 — Rupee retrospective: a sharp fall on the taper talk, stable when the taper happened.** Vol 2 Ch 4, paras 4.36-4.37, p. 64 (st. 213-214); Vol 1 Ch 1, p. 17 (st. 23).
Para 4.37: in May 2013 the rupee fell sharply on worries about the Fed's taper talk, and steadied once the taper actually began. By 8 January 2015 it had fallen 4.6% from end-March 2014, against the rouble (40.4%), real (14.2%), Mexican peso (10.7%), rupiah (10.4%) and rand (8.5%). Vol 1 (p. 17): India is more resilient than in 2013 or 2014, with more reserves and a healthier macro position. It warns that US$23.8bn of the US$38.4bn of portfolio inflows since March 2014 were interest-sensitive debt.
Quote: "India is more resilient today than in 2014 or 2013".
**FLAG (internal):** the rupee on 13 February 2015 is ₹62.14 in Vol 2 para 4.36 (p. 64) and ₹61.76 in Vol 2 para 1.60 (p. 16). Both give a 3.3% fall from ₹60.10 on 28 March 2014.

**ES14-9 — CAD financing has a lasting cost: investment-income outgo.** Vol 2 Ch 1, paras 1.56 and 1.59, p. 16 (st. 166).
The wider CADs of 2011-12 and 2012-13 were financed by foreign capital, so investment-income outgo rose. It was 28.2% of the CAD in 2007-08 and 72.6% in 2013-14. So a CAD keeps costing money through investment income even when it is fully financed. Reserves were US$330.2bn on 6 February 2015; short-term debt fell to 27.5% of reserves (end-September 2014) from 29.3%. Among major economies with a CAD, India is the second-largest reserve holder after Brazil.
Quote: "the levels of CAD have a cascading impact through investment income outgo".

## Problems and caveats

1. **Layout of the extracts.** In ES 2010-11 to ES 2012-13 the two columns are mixed line by line, so I worked out paragraph numbers from column order (nearly all were printed close to the cited text). ES 2013-14 has one main column with side-notes, and splits the "fi"/"ff" ligatures; I chose quotes without them. ES 2014-15 has two volumes with different page offsets (Vol 1: st. − 6; Vol 2: st. − 150).
2. **GDP revisions are large and change the story.**
   - 2010-11 growth: 8.6 → 8.4 → 9.3 → 8.9.
   - 2011-12 growth: 6.9 → 6.2 → 6.7.
   - 2011-12 manufacturing: 2.7 → 7.4 (ES 2013-14 para 1.8).
   - The new 2011-12-base series (ES 2014-15) raises 2012-13 and 2013-14 to 5.1 and 6.9 (GDP at market prices), and 2013-14 manufacturing from −0.7 to 5.3. ES 2014-15 itself finds this puzzling.
   - For the book: say which vintage is used. The two-years-below-5% claim (ES 2013-14) holds only on the old series.
3. **Internal inconsistencies.** Details are in the items.
   - ES 2010-11: six hikes and 175 bps, against Table 4.17 (six moves in the fiscal year = 150 bps; 175 bps includes the March 2010 move) (ES10-5).
   - ES 2011-12: repo hikes counted to January 2012, but the last hike was 25 October 2011 (ES11-6). BE subsidies 1.5% vs 1.6% of GDP (ES11-10).
   - ES 2012-13: 2011-12 fiscal deficit 5.7% (para 1.32) vs 5.8% (para 1.49) (ES12-5). The April 2010 WPI peak is 10.9% here vs 11% in ES 2010-11 (ES12-8). Revision labels conflict: Tables 1.1, 1.5 and 1.7 and the Key Indicators mark 2010-11 as 2R and 2011-12 as 1R, but Table 1.4 marks 2010-11 as 1R and 2011-12 as 2R.
   - ES 2013-14: minimum daily CRR raised to 99% (Ch 1) vs 90% (Ch 4) (ES13-9). The excessive stimulus is dated 2011-12, probably wrong (ES13-3).
   - ES 2014-15: fiscal phases dated differently in Ch 1 and Ch 2, and the tax-GDP gain is given as 2-2.5 vs 2.7 points (ES14-3). Rupee on 13 February 2015: ₹62.14 vs ₹61.76 (ES14-8).
4. **Cross-Survey revisions:**
   - 2009-10 fiscal deficit: 6.3 → 6.5.
   - 2010-11 CAD: 2.7 → 2.8.
   - End-March 2012 external debt: about 345 implied (ES 2012-13) → 360.8 (ES 2013-14).
   - The record CAD: ES 2012-13 called 4.2% in 2011-12 the highest ever; ES 2013-14 then gave 4.7% in 2012-13 as the historic peak. ES 2014-15 gives a quarterly peak of 6.7% (Q3 2012-13). Keep annual and quarterly figures apart.
5. **Not in the Surveys, or not found by search.** Do not fill these in from memory.
   - "Fragile five" (no hit in any of the five).
   - "Policy paralysis" (ES 2011-12).
   - "Twin balance sheet" (ES 2014-15 uses "balance sheet syndrome with Indian characteristics").
   - The gold 80:20 rule.
   - The date of the gold duty rise to 8% and to 10%.
   - The Fed chairman's name in connection with the taper.
   - The repo level at the start of 2013-14 after the 25 bps cut.
   - ES 2013-14 does give the FOMC date (22 May 2013) and the 15 July 2013 jump in domestic borrowing costs (Ch 2 para 2.22).
6. **The budget deficit in ES 2014-15's vulnerability index** (7.5% for 2012) is not the Centre's fiscal deficit (4.9%); the Survey does not define it in the lines read (ES14-1).
7. **Rupee figures** come on several bases: point-to-point, monthly average, annual average, single-day low. State the basis with each number. ₹68.36 (28 August 2013) is a single-day figure; ₹63.75 (September 2013) is a monthly average.
8. **Paragraph numbers worked out from column order** that should be checked against the PDF: ES11-3 para 1.87 and ES11-6 para 1.88 (end of Ch 1). Also ES14-1 and ES14-8: the Vol 1 Ch 1 text has section numbers, not paragraph numbers, so I cite those sections.
