# India: Economic Surveys 2022-23 and 2023-24 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of the "Inflation, tightening, war and energy" chapter (2022-2023: the war in Ukraine and commodity prices, inflation above the band, RBI tightening, the rupee and reserves, the widening current-account deficit, growth and the capex push, the Centre's deficit path, and the world as the Surveys saw it). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book. ES 2024-25 is used only for retrospective revisions of the 2022-23 and 2023-24 figures (ES24-1 to ES24-4). The earlier file `India_ES_2020-2022.md` already carries ES22-1 to ES22-4 (ES 2022-23's retrospective items on 2020-21 and 2021-22); they are not repeated here, and **the item numbers below restart at ES22-1**. Where an item here overlaps one of those (inflation above the band for ten months: earlier ES22-4, here ES22-15), it says so.

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2022-23 (one volume, January 2023) and 2023-24 (one volume, July 2024: the post-election edition; its Preface notes the new government took office in June), with four items from 2024-25 (one volume, January 2025). Official English PDFs from indiabudget.gov.in; archive pages listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md` (2022-23: indiabudget.gov.in/budget2023-24/economicsurvey/; 2023-24: /budget2024-25/economicsurvey/; 2024-25: /budget2025-26/economicsurvey/). Read from the extracts `Econ Subject/sources/text/es2022-23.txt`, `es2023-24.txt` and `es2024-25.txt`; each Survey's Statistical Appendix is stitched into the same file. No interim Survey or January 2024 review document is held in `sources/` (INDEX.md lists only the July 2024 edition for 2023-24), so none is used.

**Data cut-offs, as the texts show them.**
- ES 2022-23 (January 2023): NSO First Advance Estimate for 2022-23 (7.0%), Provisional Estimate for 2021-22, 1st Revised Estimate for 2020-21; CPI to December 2022; reserves to end-December 2022; BoP to Q2 (H1) 2022-23; merchandise trade and FPI to December 2022; Union finances to November 2022; IMF figures from the October 2022 WEO; Fed hikes counted to December 2022 (425 bps). The Preface says the Survey rests on nine months of data at best, eight mostly.
- ES 2023-24 (July 2024): NSO Provisional Estimate for 2023-24 (8.2%, released in May 2024) and 1st Revised Estimate for 2022-23; CPI to June 2024; reserves to 21 June 2024; full-year BoP and Union Provisional Actuals for 2023-24; IMF figures from the April 2024 WEO; ECB cut of June 2024.
- ES 2024-25 (January 2025): retrospective only. Its Statistical Appendix still carries 2022-23 at the 1st Revised Estimate and 2023-24 at the Provisional Estimate.

**GDP vintage.** All growth figures are on the 2011-12-base series. 2022-23: 8.0-8.5 forecast (ES 2021-22) → 7.0 (1st AE, ES 2022-23; 6.95 derived from SA levels) → 7.0 (1st RE, ES 2023-24 Preface; 6.99 derived) → unchanged in ES 2024-25 SA. 2023-24: 6.5 baseline, range 6.0-6.8 (ES 2022-23) → 8.2 (PE, ES 2023-24) → unchanged in ES 2024-25 SA. The earlier years were revised sharply in ES 2023-24: 2021-22 from 8.7 (PE, ES 2022-23) to 9.7, and 2020-21 from −6.6 (derived, ES 2022-23 SA) to −5.8. State the vintage with every growth number used in the book.

Most chapters are set in two columns, and the extracts mix the columns line by line in places, especially in charts, tables and boxes. Paragraph numbers were matched by column order; nearly all are printed close to the cited text. Quotes are taken only from spans that run inside one column (or from headings, summaries and box text). Quotes that run across a line break are joined, and a word hyphenated at a line end is closed up. Quotes avoid the ₹ sign. Each item has at most one verbatim quote, under 15 words; all were checked by script against the extract, ignoring case, spacing, curly vs straight quote marks and line-end hyphens. Everything else is paraphrase.

## Economic Survey 2022-23 (January 2023)

### The war and the world as ES 2022-23 saw it

**ES22-1 — The Preface: war, food-fuel-fertiliser, a stronger dollar, then respite.** Preface, p. vii (st. 8).
Barely had the pandemic receded when the war broke out; food, fuel and fertiliser prices rose sharply, advanced-economy central banks tightened, and many developing countries, especially in South Asia, came under severe stress from weaker currencies, dearer imports and a stronger dollar that raised debt-service costs. In H2 2022 commodity prices peaked and fell, though crude stayed well above pre-pandemic levels. A US-led global slowdown is described as a triple relief for dollar-importing countries (lower commodity prices, a peak in US rates and in the dollar). China reversed its Zero-Covid policy as 2023 began; a warm winter raised hopes that the Eurozone would narrowly avoid recession.
Quote: "the war in Ukraine broke out in February 2022".

**ES22-2 — "At least three shocks" since 2020.** Ch 1, chapter summary, p. 1 (st. 44).
The sequence: the pandemic contraction; the Russia-Ukraine conflict and a worldwide surge in inflation; synchronised rate hikes led by the Fed, which drew capital into the US, lifted the dollar, widened current-account deficits and added to inflation in net importers. The IMF cut its 2022 and 2023 forecasts in the October 2022 WEO; China's frailties weakened them further. The summary warns of possible financial contagion from advanced economies, where non-financial debt had risen most since the global financial crisis.
Quote: "At least three shocks have hit the global economy since 2020."

**ES22-3 — Six simultaneous challenges.** Ch 1, para 1.2, p. 3 (st. 46).
The pandemic (first); the conflict from February 2022, a swing in commodity prices and faster inflation (second); monetary tightening that weakened growth, drove capital to the US, raised sovereign yields and depreciated most currencies against the dollar (third); slower cross-border trade as nations protected their economic space under the threat of stagflation (fourth); China's policy-induced slowdown (fifth); and medium-term scarring from lost education and income (sixth). India withstood them better than most.
Quote: "A simultaneous occurrence of several challenges to growth is perhaps unprecedented."

**ES22-4 — Commodities: crude, gas, fertilisers, wheat.** Ch 1, para 1.3 and Figure I.1, pp. 3-4 (st. 46-47); Ch 4, para 4.1, p. 79 (st. 122).
The conflict, in its eleventh month, disrupted supply chains already damaged by lockdowns and drove up critical commodity prices, adding to inflation built up by large stimulus and ultra-easy policy in 2020. Advanced-economy inflation breached historical highs; EMEs, which had stimulated more cautiously, also saw higher inflation. Figure I.1 (IMF indices: all commodities, base metals, crude, food, fertiliser, natural gas, coal) is titled as showing prices not yet back to pre-conflict levels. Para 4.1: the conflict ended the debate on whether the price build-up was transient.
Quote (para 1.3): "crude oil, natural gas, fertilisers, and wheat to soar".

**ES22-5 — The Fed: 425 bps, the fastest since the 1970s.** Ch 1, para 1.4, p. 5 (st. 48); Figure I.5, p. 6 (st. 49); Ch 4 summary, p. 78 (st. 121).
Central banks that had treated price pressures as transient belatedly tightened, led by the Fed. The Fed had raised rates by 425 bps; Ch 4 adds 300 bps by the ECB and 250 bps by the Bank of England, against 225 bps by the RBI. Synchronised hikes had not yet tightened financial conditions enough for central banks to stop.
Quote (para 1.4): "the quickest since the inflationary episode of the 1970s".
FLAG (internal): para 1.4 counts the 425 bps "since March 2022"; the Figure I.5 title says "since Jan 2022". The March date matches the first hike; cite para 1.4.

**ES22-6 — The strong dollar.** Ch 1, para 1.5, p. 5 (st. 48); Ch 5, para 5.3, p. 118 (st. 161).
Inflation and tightening hardened bond yields and pulled equity capital into the US; unlike earlier episodes, capital also left advanced economies. The dollar index rose 16.1% between January and September 2022; depreciation of other currencies widened deficits and raised inflation in net importers. Para 5.3: the stronger dollar made dollar-priced fuel imports dearer.
Quote (para 1.5): "the US Dollar index strengthened by 16.1 per cent between January and September 2022".

**ES22-7 — The global slowdown: IMF October 2022 and WTO.** Ch 1, para 1.6 and Table I.1, pp. 6-7 (st. 49-50); para 1.30, p. 22 (st. 65); Ch 11, para 11.2, p. 312 (st. 355).
Global output slowed from H2 2022; the global composite PMI had been below 50 since August 2022. Table I.1 (IMF, October 2022), growth in %, 2022 and 2023: world 3.2, 2.7; advanced economies 2.4, 1.1; US 1.6, 1.0; euro area 3.1, 0.5; UK 3.6, 0.3; Japan 1.7, 1.6; EMEs 3.7, 3.7; China 3.2, 4.4; India (fiscal year) 6.8, 6.1. WTO: trade growth 3.5% in 2022 and 1.0% in 2023. Para 11.2: 6.0% in 2021 falling to 3.2% and 2.7%, the weakest profile since 2001 outside the global financial crisis and the acute pandemic.
Quote (para 1.30): "global growth is forecasted to slow from 3.2 per cent in 2022".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2023-24 gives world growth of 3.2% in 2023 (IMF April 2024), above the 2.7% projected here (ES23-17).

**ES22-8 — China's slowdown and reopening.** Ch 1, para 1.7 and Figure I.8, pp. 7-8 (st. 50-51); Preface, p. vii (st. 8); para 1.33, p. 23 (st. 66).
China's slowdown is put down to the zero-Covid policy, a contracting real-estate sector and tepid fiscal expansion; most restrictions had been ended or relaxed. Para 1.33 lists, as upside risks for India, limited fallout from China's Covid surge and reopening inflation that proves neither significant nor persistent.
Quote (para 1.7): "It is possible that economic activity picks up in China sooner than expected."

**ES22-9 — Supply-chain diversification (the "China+1" idea, unnamed).** Ch 2, para 2.37, pp. 39-40 (st. 82-83).
Trade tensions, pandemic disruptions and the conflict in Europe exposed multinationals to the risk of concentrating production in one country; they are diversifying production bases. UNCTAD is cited on reshoring, diversification and regionalisation of value chains; the WEF Chief Economists Outlook 2023 on India's opportunity. The term "China plus one" does not appear in ES 2022-23; it does in ES 2023-24 (ES23-19).
Quote (para 2.37): "India presents itself as a credible destination for capital diversifying out of other countries".

**ES22-10 — The G20 presidency.** Preface, p. x (st. 11).
India assumed the G20 presidency in December 2022 under the theme "Vasudhaiva Kutumbakam: One Earth, One Family, One Future". The listed priorities include strengthening multilateral development banks, debt, global food and energy insecurity, and urban infrastructure finance; the presidency is a platform to share India's digital public infrastructure.
Quote: "an opportunity for India to bind an otherwise fragmented global order".

**ES22-11 — Sanctions: only "price caps".** Ch 5, Box V.3 ("How is the Current Inflation Different from the 1970s?"), p. 138 (st. 181).
The box compares 2022 with the 1973 and 1979 oil shocks: oil price rises proportionally smaller; price increases spread across many commodities; supply disruptions a smaller role; inflation after years of low inflation rather than rising inflation. It warns that disruptions could intensify, as the war lowers world output of wheat, maize and fertilisers. This is the only reference to measures against Russia found in ES 2022-23; Russian crude imports or discounts are not discussed (see Not found).
Quote: "while price caps on Russian oil and gas".

### Growth

**ES22-12 — 2022-23: 7% (First Advance Estimate); fifth-largest economy.** Preface, p. vii (st. 8); Ch 1, Figure I.9, p. 9 (st. 52); SA Table 1.7, p. 21 (st. 444).
2022 marked India's 75th year of Independence; India became the world's fifth-largest economy in current dollars; nominal GDP would be about US$3.5 trillion by March. Real growth of 7% for 2022-23 follows 8.7% in 2021-22. Exports of goods and services in April-December were up 16% on a year earlier. The high oil price inflated the import bill and swelled the merchandise trade deficit, but concerns over the deficit and its financing had ebbed. SA Table 1.7 levels (₹ crore, constant prices): 1,47,35,515 (2021-22, PE) and 1,57,60,363 (2022-23, 1st AE), implying 6.95% (derived in these notes).
Quote: "expected to grow at 7 per cent for the year ending March 2023".
FLAG (vintage): ES 2021-22 forecast 8.0-8.5% for 2022-23. ES 2023-24 gives 7.0% (1st RE) and revises 2021-22 from 8.7 to 9.7 (ES23-1).

**ES22-13 — "The fastest-growing major economy"; consumption replaces exports.** Ch 1 summary, p. 1 (st. 44); para 1.14 and Figure I.17, p. 12 (st. 55); para 1.15, p. 13 (st. 56).
Agencies, and the NSO's advance estimate, put 2022-23 growth at 6.5-7.0%, despite downward revisions; Figure I.17's average across eleven forecasters is 6.9%. The IMF puts India among the top two fast-growing significant economies in 2022. The Survey stresses that this comes without help from a base effect. Private consumption replaced exports as the main driver: 58.4% of GDP in Q2 2022-23, the highest second-quarter share since 2013-14, with contact-intensive services up 16% quarter on quarter.
Quote (summary): "agencies worldwide continue to project India as the fastest-growing major economy".

**ES22-14 — Capex and the investment cycle.** Ch 1 summary, p. 2 (st. 45); paras 1.20-1.23, pp. 15-17 (st. 58-60); para 1.29, pp. 21-22 (st. 64-65).
Central capex rose 63.4% in the first eight months of 2022-23 and had been crowding in private capex since January-March 2022; the full-year capex budget looked likely to be met. Para 1.21 presents the capex push as part of a strategic package to crowd in private investment, citing buoyant direct taxes and GST, restrained revenue spending and a pick-up in private projects. Axis Bank data: corporate capex of ₹3.3 lakh crore in H1 2022-23. Bank credit growth had been in double digits since January-March 2022. Para 1.29: budgeted capex rose 2.7 times from FY16 to FY23.
Quote (summary): "crowding in the private Capex since the January-March quarter of 2022".
FLAG (internal): MSME credit growth averaged "over 30.6 per cent" in January-November 2022 in the chapter summary (p. 2) but "over 30.5 per cent" in para 1.23 (p. 17).

**ES22-15 — Outlook for 2023-24: 6.5%, range 6.0-6.8%.** Ch 1, paras 1.31-1.34, pp. 22-23 (st. 65-66).
Risks: commodity prices still above pre-conflict levels; strong domestic demand raising the import bill; plateauing exports; a wider deficit putting the rupee under pressure; rates higher for longer. The two silver linings of slow world growth are low oil prices and a smaller deficit than projected.
Quote (para 1.34): "projects a baseline GDP growth of 6.5 per cent in real terms in FY24".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2023-24 gives 8.2% (PE) for 2023-24 (ES23-2).

### Inflation

**ES22-16 — Above the band for ten months.** Ch 1, paras 1.11-1.12, pp. 10-11 (st. 53-54).
Retail inflation rose above the RBI's tolerance range in January 2022 and returned below 6% in November 2022. International commodity prices, excessive heat and unseasonal rains kept food prices high. The government cut excise and customs duties and restricted exports; the RBI raised the repo rate and withdrew excess liquidity. Overlaps `India_ES_2020-2022.md` ES22-4, which quotes the same para.
Quote (para 1.11): "It remained above the target range for ten months".

**ES22-17 — Three phases: peak 7.8% in April 2022; WPI 16.6% in May.** Ch 5 summary, pp. 117-118 (st. 160-161); para 5.5, pp. 119-120 (st. 162-163); Preface, p. viii (st. 9); Ch 1, para 1.24, p. 19 (st. 62).
CPI inflation rose to a crest of 7.8% in April 2022, held around 7.0% to August, and fell to about 5.7% by December. The rise is put down to the war and heat-damaged harvests. WPI inflation reached 16.6% in May 2022, widening the gap with CPI; the gap closed as commodity prices fell. Core inflation stayed sticky at nearly 6%. The overshoot above the band is called one of the lowest in the world. Para 1.24: the RBI projects 6.8% for 2022-23, outside the target range but, the Survey argues, neither high enough to deter consumption nor low enough to weaken investment.
Quote (Ch 5 summary): "Consumer price inflation in India went through three phases in 2022."
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2023-24 gives a 2022-23 average of 6.7% (ES23-8), against the RBI's 6.8% projection here.

**ES22-18 — Global inflation, and India "chose not to overstimulate".** Ch 5, paras 5.4-5.5, pp. 118-120 (st. 161-163).
IMF (October 2022): advanced-economy inflation from 3.1% (2021) to 7.2% (2022), the highest since 1982; EMDEs from 5.9% to 9.9%. Euro area 10.0% (September 2022); US a 40-year high of 9.1% in June 2022, 6.5% in December; UK 9.2% and Germany 8.6% in December; Turkey above 80% from August to November. Para 5.5 credits India's calibrated pandemic stimulus for containing the price effects of the war.
Quote (para 5.5): "India chose not to overstimulate".

**ES22-19 — Food: vegetables, cereals, milk, spices.** Ch 5, para 5.8, p. 120 (st. 163); para 5.11, pp. 122-124 (st. 165-167).
Food inflation ranged from 4.2% to 8.6% between April and December 2022; core stayed around 6% except in April. CFPI food inflation rose to 7.0% in 2022-23 (April-December) from 3.8% in 2021-22, led by vegetables (tomatoes), cereals, milk and spices. Cereal inflation was in double digits from September 2022.
Quote (para 5.11): "Since September 2022, double-digit inflation was observed in cereals."
FLAG (vintage): CFPI 2022-23 is 7.0% here (April-December) and 6.6% in ES 2023-24 (full year, ES23-10).

**ES22-20 — Wheat: heat wave, low procurement, export curbs.** Ch 5, Box V.1, p. 126 (st. 169); para 5.11, p. 122 (st. 165); Ch 8, p. 245 (st. 288); para 8.32, pp. 256-257 (st. 299-300).
Box V.1 dates: 13 May 2022, wheat flour exports prohibited; from 12 July 2022 permitted subject to an inter-ministerial panel; from 14 August 2022 maida and suji only with the panel's approval; 27 August 2022 notification prohibiting exports of wheat or meslin flour (atta), maida, semolina and related products (HS 1101). Ch 8 (p. 245): an early heat wave during the 2022 wheat harvest hurt production. Para 8.32: wheat procurement in RMS 2022-23 was 187.9 LMT against 433.4 LMT a year earlier, because market prices were above the MSP.
Quote (Box V.1): "On 13 May 2022, wheat flour exports were prohibited".
FLAG (definition): the Survey describes the 13 May 2022 measure as a prohibition of wheat flour exports, and para 5.11 speaks of "wheat products under HS Code 1101". A ban on exports of wheat grain is not stated as such (see Not found). Do not write "wheat export ban" without another source.

**ES22-21 — Rice, pulses and other food measures.** Ch 5, Box V.1, pp. 126-127 (st. 169-170); Ch 5 summary, p. 117 (st. 160).
A 20% export duty on rice (brown, semi-milled and wholly milled, except parboiled) from 9 September 2022; buffer stocks of pulses; zero import duty on masur (26 July 2021) and zero AIDC (12 February 2022); free import of tur and urad extended (30 March 2022); 1.5 million tonnes of chana to States at a discount of ₹8 per kg (1 September 2022). The Ch 5 summary also lists stock limits on edible oils and oilseeds, buffer stocks for onion and pulses, and lower duties on industrial inputs.
Quote (Box V.1): "imposed an export duty of 20 per cent on rice, brown rice".

**ES22-22 — Edible oils: 60% imported; sunflower oil from Ukraine and Russia.** Ch 5, para 5.14 and Figures V.8-V.10, pp. 125-126 (st. 168-169); Box V.1, p. 127 (st. 170).
India imports 60% of its edible-oil demand. Sunflower oil, 15% of edible-oil imports, comes mainly from Ukraine and Russia. Edible-oil inflation was high in 2021-22 and subdued in 2022-23 because of tariff cuts and stock limits. Measures: duty- and AIDC-free imports of 20 lakh tonnes each of crude soybean and sunflower oil (from 24 May 2022); stock limits extended to 31 December 2022; in July 2022 edible-oil associations directed to cut MRPs by ₹15 per litre.
Quote (para 5.14): "India meets 60 per cent of its edible oils demand through imports".
FLAG (vintage): ES 2023-24 gives "more than 50 per cent" imported (para 3.23, p. 96, st. 143).

**ES22-23 — Fuel: excise cuts and the crude price.** Ch 3, para 3.11, p. 46 (st. 89); Annexe 3, p. 75 (st. 118); Ch 5, Box V.2 and para 5.23, p. 133 (st. 176).
Excise on petrol and diesel was cut in two phases: ₹5 and ₹10 per litre from 4 November 2021, and ₹8 and ₹6 from 22 May 2022 (through the Road and Infrastructure Cess), followed by State VAT cuts. Annexe 3: the Indian crude basket passed US$110/bbl in May 2022, the rupee hovered around ₹77 per dollar, and retail petrol and diesel rose from ₹95.41 and ₹86.67 per litre (March 2022) to ₹105.41 and ₹96.67 (21 May 2022). Para 3.11: excise collections fell 20.9% year on year in April-November 2022 (the budget assumed −15%). Para 5.23: the Indian basket peaked at US$116/bbl in June 2022 and fell to US$78/bbl in December.
Quote (para 3.11): "reduced the excise duty in November 2021 and May 2022".
FLAG (date): the second cut is "from 22 May 2022" in Box V.2; Annexe 3 gives retail prices "on 21 May, 2022" before the cut. They are consistent; cite 22 May for the cut.

### Monetary policy

**ES22-24 — April 2022: the stance changes and the SDF arrives.** Ch 4, para 4.2, p. 80 (st. 123); Ch 4 summary, p. 78 (st. 121).
After cutting 115 bps in March-May 2020, the MPC held the repo rate from May 2020 to February 2022. With retail inflation above the band since January 2022, the April 2022 meeting introduced the Standing Deposit Facility at 3.75% (replacing the reverse repo as the floor of the LAF corridor, collateral-free) and changed the stance.
Quote (para 4.2): "Accommodative and focused on the withdrawal of accommodation, while supporting growth".
FLAG (internal): the Ch 4 summary says the RBI "initiated its monetary tightening cycle in April 2022"; the first repo hike was 4 May 2022 (Table V.3). April was the stance change and the SDF; May the first hike.

**ES22-25 — Hikes from May to December 2022: 4.0% → 6.25%.** Ch 4, para 4.3, p. 80 (st. 123); liquidity section, p. 82 (st. 125); para 4.9, p. 83 (st. 126); Ch 5, para 5.28 and Table V.3, p. 138 (st. 181).
An off-cycle meeting in May 2022 raised the repo, SDF and MSF by 40 bps each and the CRR by 50 bps (withdrawing about ₹87,000 crore of primary liquidity, p. 82). Table V.3: 8 April 2022 unchanged at 4%; 4 May 4.0 → 4.4; 8 June 4.4 → 4.9; 5 August 4.9 → 5.4; 30 September 5.4 → 5.9; 7 December 5.9 → 6.25 (35 bps). Cumulative 225 bps over five meetings. Transmission to December 2022: external-benchmark lending rates up 225 bps, 1-year MCLR up 115 bps; fresh-loan WALR up 135 bps (to November).
Quote (para 4.3): "the MPC convened an off-cycle meeting in May 2022".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2023-24 records a further hike to 6.5% in February 2023, cumulative 250 bps (ES23-6).

### The rupee, reserves and capital flows

**ES22-26 — The rupee: "one of the better-performing currencies".** Ch 1, para 1.12 and Figure I.12, pp. 10-11 (st. 53-54); Ch 1 summary, p. 1 (st. 44); Ch 11, para 11.63, p. 340 (st. 383).
The dollar appreciated against the rupee, but the rupee fared better than most; its modest depreciation may have added to inflation and the deficit. Figure I.12 (depreciation against the dollar, April-December 2022, rates as on 31 December): South Africa 16.4%, Brazil 11.5, Australia 10.0, Indonesia 8.8, India 8.8, China 8.8, UK 8.6, Japan 7.7, euro 4.1, South Korea 3.9, Mexico −2.0 (appreciation).
Quote (para 1.12): "the rupee has been one of the better-performing currencies worldwide".

**ES22-27 — "It is the US dollar that has strengthened".** Ch 11, paras 11.44-11.47, pp. 333-334 (st. 376-377).
April-December 2022 the rupee fell 8.3% against the dollar while the dollar index rose 4.4%; in calendar 2022 the rupee fell 10.8% and the dollar index rose 6.4%. The dollar's NEER rose 7.8% in 2022 while India's 64-currency NEER fell 4.8%. The rupee rose against sterling (6.7%), the yen (14.5%) and the euro (6.4%) on April-December averages. The 6- and 40-currency REER fell 3.4% and 4.0%. The paragraph adds that this is a fact whatever is said on social media.
Quote (para 11.44): "it is not that the INR has weakened".
FLAG (internal): April-December depreciation is 8.8% in Figure I.12 (end-December rate) and 8.3% in para 11.44 (December monthly average over April, footnote 20). Say which.

**ES22-28 — Reserves: depletion, mostly valuation.** Ch 11, paras 11.42-11.43, p. 330 (st. 373); para 11.49, p. 335 (st. 378); paras 11.38 and 11.40, pp. 328-329 (st. 371-372); chapter summary, pp. 311-312 (st. 354-355).
Reserves fell by US$25.8bn on a BoP basis in H1 2022-23 (against an accretion of US$63.1bn a year earlier); with valuation losses of US$48.9bn, the nominal fall was US$74.6bn. Reserves were US$532.7bn at end-September 2022 (8.8 months of imports) and US$562.7bn at end-December (9.3 months); India was the sixth-largest holder at end-November (IMF). Reserve assets fell 12.3% between end-March and end-September 2022. Net capital inflows fell to US$29.0bn in H1 from US$65.0bn, mainly because of an FPI outflow of US$14.6bn in Q1; FPIs turned positive in Q2, net −US$2.5bn for April-December. External debt was 19.2% of GDP at end-September 2022.
Quote (para 11.42): "huge valuation losses (US$ 48.9 billion) contributed to the net depletion".

### Current account and trade

**ES22-29 — The deficit at 4.4% of GDP in Q2 2022-23.** Ch 11, para 11.35, p. 326 (st. 369); chapter summary, pp. 311-312 (st. 354-355).
The current-account deficit was US$36.4bn (4.4% of GDP) in Q2 2022-23, against US$9.7bn (1.3%) a year earlier, on a merchandise trade deficit of US$83.5bn and higher net investment-income outgo. Petroleum crude and products imports rose 45.6% to US$163.9bn, the largest import item; fuel (coal and POL) rose to 37.1% of imports from 30.4%. The merchandise deficit was US$218.9bn against US$136.5bn.
Quote (para 11.14): "The merchandise trade deficit for April-December 2022 was estimated at US$ 218.9".

**ES22-31 — Services and remittances cushion the deficit.** Ch 11, paras 11.36-11.37, p. 327 (st. 370); Ch 10, para 10.7, pp. 295-296 (st. 338-339); para 11.62, p. 339 (st. 382).
Net services receipts rose from US$51.4bn (H1 2021-22) to US$65.5bn (H1 2022-23), on computer and business services; net private transfers from US$38.4bn to US$48.0bn, helped, the Survey suggests, by higher oil prices and a weaker rupee. ### Fiscal

**ES22-33 — The deficit path: 9.2 → 6.7 → 6.4% (budgeted).** Ch 3, paras 3.4-3.5, pp. 42-43 (st. 85-86); chapter summary, p. 41 (st. 84).
The Centre's deficit, 9.2% of GDP in 2020-21, fell to 6.7% in 2021-22 (Provisional Actuals) and was budgeted at 6.4% in 2022-23. The war, which the Budget had not foreseen, required more spending on food and fertiliser subsidies plus duty cuts, yet the deficit at end-November was 58.9% of the full-year target (five-year average for the period 104.6%). Gross tax revenue grew 15.5% in April-November 2022; gross GST 24.8%.
Quote (para 3.5): "not anticipating the geopolitical developments that unfolded during the year".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): the outcome was 6.4% (ES 2023-24 para 1.22 and SA Table 2.4; ES23-12).

Subsidies fell from 3.6% of GDP (2020-21) to 1.9% (2021-22 PA) and were budgeted at 1.2% for 2022-23. Because of the war's effect on food, fertiliser and fuel prices, 94.7% of the subsidy budget was used by November. The first supplementary demands sought ₹80,000 crore more for food subsidy and PMGKAY, and ₹1.09 lakh crore for fertiliser subsidy.
Quote (para 3.22): "there was a higher food and fertiliser subsidy requirement".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): major subsidies were 2.0% of GDP in 2022-23 (ES 2023-24 SA Table 2.4), against 1.2% budgeted.

**ES22-35 — Capex: ₹7.5 lakh crore, 2.9% of GDP budgeted.** Ch 3, paras 3.17-3.19, pp. 50-51 (st. 93-94); Ch 12, p. 345 (st. 388).
Central capex rose from a long-run average of 1.7% of GDP (FY09-FY20) to 2.5% (2021-22 PA), budgeted at 2.9% for 2022-23. The 2022-23 capex budget of ₹7.5 lakh crore (35.4% above ₹5.5 lakh crore in 2021-22, Ch 12) was 59.6% spent by November, with growth of more than 60% year on year (long-run average 13.5%). States were given interest-free capex loans and higher borrowing limits.
Quote (para 3.17): "It is further budgeted to increase to 2.9 per cent of GDP in FY23."
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2023-24 SA Table 2.4 gives central capex of 2.7% of GDP for 2022-23.

**ES22-36 — Free grain: PMGKAY to December 2022, then folded into NFSA.** Ch 8, para 8.33 and preceding text, pp. 256-257 (st. 299-300); Ch 5, para 5.11, p. 124 (st. 167).
PMGKAY (5 kg per person per month free, on top of NFSA rations) ran in phases from April 2020; Phase VII covered October-December 2022; about 1,118 LMT were allocated over all phases. From 1 January 2023 a new integrated scheme gives NFSA grain free (5 kg per person to priority households, 35 kg per AAY household) for a year.
Quote (para 5.11): "to provide free foodgrains to more than 80 crore beneficiaries".

## Economic Survey 2023-24 (July 2024)

### Growth, with the revised past

**ES23-1 — The Preface: 8.2% after 9.7% and 7.0%.** Preface, p. vii (st. 8).
The economy grew 8.2% in 2023-24 (NSO, May 2024). The two previous years grew 9.7% and 7.0%. Inflation is largely under control except for some food items; the trade deficit was lower than in 2022-23; the current-account deficit was about 0.7% of GDP, with a surplus in the last quarter. Repatriation of FDI was US$29.3bn in 2022-23 and US$44.5bn in 2023-24.
Quote: "came on the heels of growth rates of 9.7% and 7.0%".
FLAG (vintage): 2021-22 was 8.7% (PE) in ES 2022-23 and is 9.7% here; derived from SA Table 1.7 levels: 9.69% (2021-22) and 6.99% (2022-23, 1st RE).

**ES23-2 — 2023-24: 8.2%, a third year above 7%.** Ch 1, paras 1.11-1.13, pp. 9-10 (st. 56-57); SA Table 1.7, p. 21 (st. 552).
Real GVA grew 7.2%; net taxes 19.1% (strong tax growth and subsidy rationalisation), hence GDP growth above GVA growth. Manufacturing grew 9.9% after a weak 2022-23, helped by lower input prices (manufacturing deflator −1.7%); construction 9.9%. Foodgrain output fell 0.3%. SA Table 1.7 levels: ₹1,60,71,429 crore (2022-23, 1st RE) and ₹1,73,81,722 crore (2023-24, PE), implying 8.15% (derived).
Quote (para 1.11): "posting growth of over 7 per cent for a third consecutive year".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2022-23 projected 6.5% (range 6.0-6.8) for 2023-24 (ES22-15).

**ES23-3 — 2020-21 revised to −5.8%; 2023-24 output 20% above 2019-20.** Ch 1, para 1.52, p. 34 (st. 81); chapter summary, p. 1 (st. 48); Box I.1, pp. 14-16 (st. 61-63).
Real GDP in 2023-24 was 20% above 2019-20, a 4.6% annual average, despite a 5.8% fall in 2020-21. The decade to 2019-20 averaged 6.6%. Box I.1: GDP was only about 1% below its pre-pandemic trend in 2023-24; services GVA had not yet reached its trend (trade, hotels and transport).
Quote (para 1.52): "despite a 5.8 per cent decline in FY21 inflicted by the pandemic".
FLAG (vintage): 2020-21 −7.7 (AE) → −7.3 (PE) → −6.6 (1st RE, derived from ES 2022-23 SA) → −5.8 here (SA Table 1.7 levels give −5.78).

**ES23-4 — How 2022-23 began.** Ch 1, para 1.21, p. 16 (st. 63).
Spillovers from the war raised domestic prices and widened the current-account deficit through higher oil prices; central banks raised rates, creating uncertainty in advanced and emerging economies alike. Macroeconomic stability is presented as what secured growth through 2022-23 and 2023-24.
Quote: "Spillovers from the conflict in Europe were stoking domestic price pressures".

**ES23-5 — The private capex upcycle.** Ch 1, para 1.16, p. 11 (st. 58); para 1.31, p. 21 (st. 68); Preface, p. vii (st. 8).
GFCF by private non-financial corporations grew 19.8% in 2022-23 (National Accounts Statistics 2024); Axis Bank data on over 3,200 firms point to continued growth in 2023-24. But between 2018-19 and 2022-23 their share of total GFCF rose only from 34.1% to 34.9%, mostly in dwellings and structures; machinery and equipment grew strongly only from 2021-22. The Preface says private investment began in 2021-22 and must now take over from public investment.

### Fiscal

**ES23-6 — Deficit 6.4% (2022-23) → 5.6% (2023-24 PA).** Ch 1, para 1.22, p. 17 (st. 64); para 1.46, p. 29 (st. 76); SA Table 2.4, p. 63 (st. 594).
Buoyant direct and indirect taxes, a higher RBI dividend and restrained revenue spending brought the deficit down. A growing share of the deficit is capital outlay. The deficit is expected to fall to 4.5% of GDP or lower by 2025-26. SA Table 2.4 (% of GDP): fiscal deficit 9.2 (2020-21), 6.7 (2021-22), 6.4 (2022-23), 5.6 (2023-24 PA); capex 2.1, 2.5, 2.7, 3.2; major subsidies 3.6, 1.9, 2.0, 1.4.
Quote (para 1.22): "from 6.4 per cent of GDP in FY23 to 5.6 per cent".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): 2022-23 out-turn 6.4% equals the budget figure in ES 2022-23 (ES22-33). The 2023-24 target of 5.9% is not stated in either Survey; it is in the Budget Speech 2023-24, para 116 (`sources/text/docs/bs2023-24.txt`, line 1037), outside the scope of these notes.
FLAG (vintage): 2019-20 deficit 4.7% in this SA table, 4.6% in ES 2024-25 SA Table 2.4 (ES24-2).

**ES23-7 — Capex ₹9.5 lakh crore in 2023-24.** Ch 1, paras 1.29-1.30, p. 21 (st. 68); Table I.2, p. 21 (st. 68).
Capex in 2023-24 (PA) was ₹9.5 lakh crore, 2.8 times the 2019-20 level. Table I.2 shows road transport and highways up 28.1% and defence capital outlay up 7.9%. Total expenditure fell to 15.0% of GDP from 17.7% in 2020-21 (para 1.28).
Quote (para 1.29): "an increase of 28.2 per cent on a YoY basis".

**ES23-8 — Subsidies fall as fertiliser prices return to pre-war levels.** Ch 1, para 1.34, p. 22 (st. 69).
Major subsidies fell 22.1% in 2023-24: fertiliser 24.6% and food 22.4%. Fertiliser prices, up steeply in 2022-23 because of the war, had broadly returned to pre-conflict levels as supply chains adapted. Free grain went to 81.4 crore people (para 1.28).
Quote: "The prices of fertilisers had steeply increased in FY23 due to the Russia-Ukraine conflict".

### Inflation and monetary policy

**ES23-9 — Inflation: 6.7% (2022-23) → 5.4% (2023-24).** Ch 1, para 1.40, pp. 26-27 (st. 73-74); Ch 3, paras 3.1 and 3.6-3.9, pp. 85-88 (st. 132-135).
Measures credited: open-market sales, sales through specified outlets, timely imports, LPG and fuel price cuts (LPG ₹200 per cylinder in August 2023; petrol and diesel ₹2 per litre in March 2024), and the RBI's 250 bps of hikes between May 2022 and February 2023. CPI was 5.1% and core 3.1% in June 2024. India is called the only country among its peers on a high-growth, low-inflation path in 2021-22 to 2023-24 (para 1.40); in 2023 its inflation was within the 2-6% range and 1.4 points below the world average (para 3.6).
Quote (para 1.40): "After averaging 6.7 per cent in FY23, retail inflation declined".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): the RBI projected 6.8% for 2022-23 (ES 2022-23, ES22-17); the out-turn here is 6.7%.

**ES23-10 — Food inflation rises again: 6.6% → 7.5%.** Ch 3, paras 3.18-3.22, pp. 93-94 (st. 140-141).
CFPI food inflation rose from 3.8% (2021-22) to 6.6% (2022-23) and 7.5% (2023-24), attributed to heat waves, uneven monsoons, unseasonal rain and low reservoirs. Tomatoes (July 2023), onions, tur and urad, and milk are discussed.
Quote (para 3.18): "increased from 3.8 per cent in FY22 to 6.6 per cent in FY23".
FLAG (vintage): CFPI 2022-23 was 7.0% (April-December) in ES 2022-23 (ES22-19).

**ES23-11 — Export curbs extended to rice; Bharat Atta.** Ch 3, Box III.2, pp. 95-96 (st. 142-143); para 3.22, p. 94 (st. 141); para 3.24, p. 96 (st. 143).
Box III.2 lists measures in force in 2023-24: wheat flour, maida and semolina exports kept prohibited; stock limits on wheat; Bharat Atta at ₹27.50 per kg (November 2023); broken rice exports prohibited (September 2022) and non-basmati rice (July 2023); a floor price for basmati exports (October 2023); a 20% duty on parboiled rice to 31 March 2024; Bharat Rice at ₹29 per kg (February 2024); onion export restrictions (December 2023); sugar export restrictions (from June 2022, extended beyond 31 October 2023). PMGKAY free grain to more than 81 crore people was extended for five years from January 2024.
Quote (Box III.2): "placed the export of broken rice and non-basmati rice under the".
FLAG (vintage): ES 2022-23 records a 20% export duty on non-parboiled rice from 9 September 2022 (ES22-21); ES 2023-24 dates the broken-rice prohibition to September 2022. Both may be right (different products); give each with its source.

**ES23-12 — Repo at 6.5% since February 2023; 250 bps in all.** Ch 2, para 2.6, p. 39 (st. 86); Ch 3, para 3.8, p. 88 (st. 135); Ch 1, para 1.54, p. 34 (st. 81).
The MPC held the repo at 6.5% through 2023-24, focused on withdrawal of accommodation. Para 1.54: with core inflation around 3%, the RBI, watching both the withdrawal of accommodation and the Fed, had kept rates unchanged for some time.
Quote (para 2.6): "kept the policy repo rate unchanged at 6.5 per cent since February 2023".
FLAG (vintage): 225 bps to 6.25% at ES 2022-23's cut-off (ES22-25) → 250 bps to 6.5% here.

**ES23-13 — Transmission and liquidity, May 2022 to May 2024.** Ch 2, para 2.13 and Table II.1, p. 42 (st. 89); para 2.7, p. 39 (st. 86).
Over the tightening cycle the external-benchmark lending rate rose 250 bps and the 1-year median MCLR 175 bps. Factors in 2023-24 liquidity: the withdrawal of ₹2,000 notes (announced 19 May 2023; 97.87% returned by 28 June 2024), the HDFC merger (July 2023) and a temporary incremental CRR (August 2023).
Quote (para 2.13): "increased by 250 bps and 175 bps, respectively".

### External sector

**ES23-14 — The 2022-23 deficit settles at 2.0% of GDP.** Ch 4, paras 4.53-4.54, pp. 133-134 (st. 180-181); Ch 1, para 1.43, pp. 27-28 (st. 74-75).
The deficit narrowed to US$23.2bn (0.7% of GDP) in 2023-24 from US$67bn (2.0%) in 2022-23, with a surplus in Q4 2023-24. Net services receipts rose from US$143.3bn to US$162.8bn; net private transfers from US$101.8bn to US$106.6bn. Services exports reached a record US$341.1bn in 2023-24.
Quote (para 4.53): "from USD 67 billion (2 per cent of GDP) during the previous year".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2022-23 reported 4.4% in Q2 and 3.3% in H1 2022-23 (ES22-29); the full year came in at 2.0%.

**ES23-15 — The trade deficit narrows.** Ch 4, para 4.13, pp. 109-110 (st. 156-157); chapter summary, p. 103 (st. 150).
The merchandise trade deficit narrowed to US$238.3bn in 2023-24 from US$264.9bn, as imports fell more than exports with lower commodity prices; merchandise exports contracted in H2 2022-23 and H1 2023-24. The overall (goods and services) trade deficit fell from US$121.6bn to US$78.1bn; cheaper crude helped.
Quote (para 4.13): "the merchandise trade deficit narrowed to USD 238.3 billion in FY24".
FLAG (typo): para 4.13 says the EU grew 0.6% in 2023 "compared to 3.6 per cent growth in 2024"; the comparison year should be 2022. Do not use the 2024 figure.

**ES23-16 — Reserves rebuilt; the rupee "least volatile".** Ch 4, paras 4.72-4.75, pp. 145-147 (st. 192-194); chapter summary, p. 103 (st. 150); Ch 1, paras 1.44-1.45, p. 28 (st. 75).
Reserves rose US$68bn in 2023-24, the largest increase among major holders, to US$653.7bn on 21 June 2024 (more than 10 months of projected imports). Net FPI inflows were US$44.1bn in 2023-24 after net outflows in the two previous years; net FDI fell from US$42.0bn to US$26.5bn because of repatriation. The rupee traded within ₹82-83.5 per dollar and fell only 2.9% against the dollar in 2023-24, while the dollar gained on virtually every major peer. External debt was 18.7% of GDP at end-March 2024.
Quote (para 4.73): "INR was one of the least volatile major currencies".

### The world as ES 2023-24 saw it

**ES23-17 — World growth 3.2% in 2023; rates high, the ECB cuts first.** Ch 1, paras 1.1-1.3, pp. 2-4 (st. 49-51); paras 1.6-1.7, pp. 5-7 (st. 52-54); para 1.53, p. 34 (st. 81).
IMF (April 2024): world growth 3.2% in 2023, above the 2.8% projected in April 2023. The war hit Europe hardest (Germany, France); US mortgages and corporate debt on fixed rates limited the effect of rate rises. Inflation, after peaking in 2022, fell as supply pressures and the war's energy and food shocks faded, but core inflation stayed sticky. Many central banks held or raised rates in 2023; China eased. The ECB cut its deposit rate by a quarter point in June 2024, its first cut in nearly five years; the Fed was reluctant to cut.
Quote (para 1.7): "became the first major central bank to cut its policy rate".
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): ES 2022-23 cited the IMF's October 2022 projection of 2.7% for 2023 (ES22-7).

**ES23-18 — Fragmentation, the Red Sea and trade along geopolitical lines.** Ch 1, paras 1.5 and 1.9-1.10, pp. 5-8 (st. 52-55); Ch 4, paras 4.4-4.6, p. 105 (st. 152).
The Red Sea crisis from October 2023 raised shipping costs, but supply-chain pressure rose only briefly. Global export volumes grew 0.5% in 2023; trade is reallocating along geopolitical lines. Para 4.4: Mexico became the largest US goods partner in 2023; US imports from Vietnam rose from US$46bn (2017) to US$114bn (2023); EU pipeline gas from Russia fell from 150.2 bcm (2021) to 42.9 bcm (2023) while gas from the US rose from 18.9 to 56.2 bcm; the text names "decoupling", "derisking", "reshoring", "nearshoring" and "friend sharing". Para 4.6: world trade volume contracted 1.2% in 2023 after 3% growth in 2022.
Quote (para 1.9): "About 3,000 new restrictions on trade were introduced in 2023".
FLAG (typo): para 4.6 puts commercial-services trade at "USD 7.5 billion in 2023"; the order of magnitude must be trillion. Do not use the figure as printed.

**ES23-19 — "China plus one": invite Chinese FDI rather than imports.** Ch 4, Box IV.6, pp. 143-144 (st. 190-191); para 4.71, p. 143 (st. 190); Ch 5, para 5.19, p. 161 (st. 208).
Multinationals are "de-risking" from China; Apple assembled US$14bn of iPhones in India in 2023-24 (14% of global output). India's electronics trade with the US went from a deficit of US$0.6bn (2016-17) to a surplus of US$8.7bn (2023-24). Of two routes (joining China's supply chain or attracting Chinese FDI), the box prefers FDI, citing the growing trade deficit with China, and says India must balance importing goods and importing capital from China. Para 5.19 ("The Chinese conundrum") ties Chinese dominance of supply chains to the war in Ukraine.
Quote (Box IV.6): "focusing on FDI from China seems more promising".

**ES23-20 — Fastest-growing G20 economy; the G20 presidency.** Ch 5, para 5.19, p. 161 (st. 208); p. 172 (st. 219); Ch 6, p. 205 (st. 252); Ch 7, para 7.77, p. 247 (st. 294).
India is described as the fastest-growing G20 country, growing faster than China, though its economy is still a fraction of China's. The 2023 G20 presidency appears in passing: a report under it on multilateral banks (p. 172), climate-finance positions reflected in the Delhi Declaration (p. 205), and "women-led development" as one of six priorities (para 7.77). No chapter or box is given to the presidency.
Quote (para 5.19): "India is the fastest-growing G20 country".

## Problems and caveats

1. **Layout.** Both Surveys are largely two-column and the extracts interleave columns in charts, tables and boxes. Chart values (e.g. ES22 Figures I.1, I.5, I.13; ES23 Charts I.53, IV.21) cannot always be tied to their labels; check the PDF before using them.
2. **GDP vintages.** The revisions in ES 2023-24 are large: 2021-22 from 8.7 to 9.7, 2020-21 from −6.6 to −5.8. The 2022-23 figure stayed at 7.0. The derived rates (6.95, 6.99, 8.15, 9.69, −5.78) are calculations in these notes from SA Table 1.7 levels, not printed. Always give the vintage.
3. **Internal inconsistencies in ES 2022-23.** Fed hikes "since March 2022" (para 1.4) vs "since Jan 2022" (Figure I.5). Rupee depreciation April-December 2022: 8.8% (Figure I.12, end-period) vs 8.3% (para 11.44, monthly average). MSME credit growth 30.6% (summary) vs 30.5% (para 1.23). Tightening "initiated in April 2022" (Ch 4 summary), but the first hike was 4 May 2022.
4. **Typos in ES 2023-24.** EU growth "3.6 per cent growth in 2024" for 2022 (para 4.13); commercial-services trade "USD 7.5 billion" (para 4.6). The SA contents page is labelled 2022-23.
5. **Forecast vs outcome.** 2023-24 growth 6.5 → 8.2. World 2023 growth 2.7 → 3.2. 2022-23 CPI 6.8 (RBI) → 6.7. CAD 4.4% (Q2) / 3.3% (H1) → 2.0% for the year. Repo 6.25 → 6.5. Deficit 6.4 budgeted → 6.4. Capex 2.9 budgeted → 2.7. Subsidies 1.2 budgeted → 2.0.
6. **Definitions.** The 13 May 2022 measure is described as a ban on wheat flour exports (Box V.1), not wheat grain. Rice: a 20% export duty from 9 September 2022 (ES22) and a broken-rice export prohibition dated September 2022 (ES23). CPI peak 7.8% is headline CPI-C; CPI-C food (8.3% in April 2022, ES22 para 5.15) is a different series from CFPI.
7. **What the Surveys leave out.** Neither Survey discusses India's purchases of discounted Russian crude, Western sanctions (beyond "price caps" in ES22 Box V.3), or the 2023 bank failures. The rupee trade-settlement scheme (ES22 para 11.20) is not linked to Russia in the text. These gaps are themselves a finding about what the Survey chose to say at the time; do not fill them from memory.
8. **Retrospectives in ES 2024-25** are thin: it keeps the ES 2023-24 GDP and fiscal figures unchanged and adds little on 2022-23.
