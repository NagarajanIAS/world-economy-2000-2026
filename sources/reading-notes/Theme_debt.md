### DF-IMF-1. IMF, Fiscal Monitor October 2024, Chapter 1 "Putting a Lid on Public Debt", October 2024
Issue page: https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/FM/Issues/2024/10/23/fiscal-monitor-october-2024 (title "Fiscal Monitor October 2024:
Putting a Lid on Public Debt"). Chapter PDF: https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2024/october/english/ch1.pdf
(24 PDF pages; printed page = PDF page). Read: pp. 1–5 (introduction, debt-at-risk), p. 8 (global factors in yields), pp. 12–13 (required
adjustment), p. 16 (India mention), pp. 18–19 (conclusions); searched the rest.
- p. 1: global public debt is expected to exceed $100 trillion in 2024; after falling in 2021–22 it rose again in 2023 and is projected to
  approach 100 percent of GDP by 2030, with China and the United States largely driving the increase. Debt stabilises or falls by 2029 in
  about two-thirds of countries but stays above pre-pandemic levels.
- p. 1: upside risks: political discourse has tilted towards more spending over three decades; spending pressures (green transition,
  defence, industrial policy, ageing, SDGs) are not fully in projections. Projections show an "optimism bias": realised debt ratios three
  years ahead have been on average 6 points of GDP higher than projected, and errors are larger where debt was projected to fall.
- p. 1, fn 1: interest-growth differentials are projected to keep helping debt reduction on average, but primary deficits and stock-flow
  adjustments push debt up; for China and the US "sizable fiscal deficits are driving the increase in debt".
- p. 2 and p. 5: new "debt-at-risk" framework (debt in an extreme adverse scenario, 95th percentile). Global debt-at-risk is 115 percent
  of GDP three years ahead, about 20 points above the WEO projection for 2026; the 75th percentile is 103 percent (p. 5). Five years ahead
  it is 119 percent (fn 11). The distribution is as right-skewed as in the global financial crisis: the model's median is 85 percent for
  2009 against 97 percent for 2023, the 95th quantile 104 against 115 (fn 12).
- p. 2: high current debt amplifies the effect of weak growth and tight financial conditions on debt-at-risk; global factors correlated
  with US yield volatility and US fiscal and monetary policy uncertainty increasingly drive borrowing costs across countries. p. 8: the
  global-factor share of yield swings rises with the share of foreign and nonbank investors (by 15 percent for advanced economies moving
  from the 25th to 75th percentile of nonbank share).
- p. 2: "unidentified debt" (debt changes not explained by deficits, r–g and exchange rates) has averaged about 1.0–1.5 percent of GDP a
  year and up to 7 points of GDP after financial stress, from contingent liabilities, fiscal risks and arrears.
- p. 12: the median country's WEO baseline adjustment is about 1 percent of GDP cumulative over 2023–29, giving a 60 percent probability
  that debt stabilises or falls by 2029; raising that to 80 percent needs 3–3½ percent of GDP.
- pp. 12–13: past adjustments averaged 2½ percent of GDP. Countries whose debt is not projected to stabilise hold nearly 60 percent of
  global debt and need 4½ percent of GDP, almost twice past adjustments; delay adds about 0.2 point of GDP a year. Where debt is projected
  to stabilise the median need is 3.6 points. The US needs less adjustment than China because its 2029 deficit is closer to the
  debt-stabilising level (p. 12).
- p. 16: in some countries (Brazil, India, South Africa) adjustment needs reforms to budget rigidities to reallocate spending.
- p. 19 (conclusions): cumulative adjustment of 3.0–4.5 percent of GDP is needed on average over the medium term, about 20 percent of
  revenues in low-income developing countries and 13 percent elsewhere. With central banks easing, "Now is an opportune time." Debt is
  growing faster than pre-pandemic in the UK and US. Adjustment should be gradual but sustained, more front-loaded where market access is
  under pressure; well-designed packages mix spending and revenue measures.
- p. 18, fn 27: fiscal rules alone do not curb unidentified debt; budget transparency and compliance with rules do (p. 2).

### DF-IMF-2. IMF, Fiscal Monitor April 2026, Executive Summary and Chapter 1 "Fiscal Policy under Pressure: High Debt, Rising Risks", April 2026
Executive summary: https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2026/april/english/execsum.pdf (pp. xi–xii). Chapter 1:
https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/fiscal-monitor/2026/april/english/ch1.pdf (printed page = PDF page). URL note: these two
paths were GUESSED from the October 2024 pattern (the issue page could not be found: a guessed issue-page URL returned 404); the files
are genuine IMF PDFs with the running head "International Monetary Fund | April 2026". Read: executive summary in full; ch. 1 pp. 1–3
(introduction, Tables 1.1 and 1.2), pp. 10–16 (US, China, Europe, emerging markets, low-income countries), p. 28 (policy); searched the rest.
- p. xi / p. 1: global gross government debt rose to 93.9 percent of GDP in 2025 (nearly 2 points above 2024) and on current paths reaches
  100 percent by 2029, "a level previously reached only in the aftermath of World War II" (p. xi). The global deficit stayed at about
  5 percent of GDP in 2025 (p. 1).
- p. xi: the global "fiscal gap" (projected primary balances minus debt-stabilising ones) has shrunk from a cushion of more than 1 percent
  of GDP a decade ago to near zero; IMF calls this structural, reflecting permanent entitlement spending or revenue cuts in some of the
  largest economies.
- p. xi / p. 1: interest payments rose from 2 percent to nearly 3 percent of global GDP in four years as maturing debt is refinanced at
  higher rates; long-term yields in advanced economies are at multidecade highs (p. 1).
- p. xi: US general government deficit 7–8 percent of GDP "despite operating near full capacity", with no consolidation plan; gross debt
  projected at 142 percent of GDP by 2031. p. 10: US interest payments approach 5 percent of GDP by 2031; CBO estimates the One Big
  Beautiful Bill Act adds above half a percent of GDP a year to the primary deficit; such deficits would be unprecedented in peacetime with
  a closed output gap.
- p. xi / p. 11: China's deficit widened to nearly 8 percent of GDP; debt rises from 99.2 percent of GDP in 2025 to almost 127 percent by
  2031, nearly 11 points above the April 2025 projection for 2030, largely because of a methodological change in how local government
  financing vehicles are counted (fn 8).
- Table 1.2 (p. 3), gross debt, 2019 / 2025 / 2031: World 82.1 / 93.9 / 102.3; advanced economies 100.9 / 108.0 / 114.8; US 108.8 / 123.9
  / 142.1; Japan 206.3 / 206.5 / 192.8 (definition revised to consolidated face value); Italy 133.9 / 137.1 / 136.1; France 98.2 / 116.0 /
  120.7; UK 84.9 / 102.3 / 102.6; Germany 58.7 / 62.9 / 73.7; China 59.8 / 99.2 / 126.8; India 76.7 / 84.1 / 77.7; Brazil 87.1 / 93.3 /
  106.5; low-income developing countries 38.5 / 48.2 / 44.0. Peak year 2020: World 97.4, US 132.6, India 90.6.
- Table 1.1 (p. 3), overall balance 2025: World –5.0; US –6.8; China –7.9; India –7.4 (–13.2 in 2020; –6.6 projected for 2031).
- pp. xi, 13: in Europe 16 EU members invoked the national escape clause of the Stability and Growth Pact for security needs; balances
  revised down about 0.5 point; IMF says defence is one of the most rigid spending items, so the loosening "is unlikely to be transitory"
  (p. 13). Germany's debt rises to nearly 74 percent by 2031 for investment and defence (p. 12).
- p. 13: emerging markets excluding China: deficit widened to 4.7 percent of GDP in 2025, interest payments reached 3.8 percent of GDP;
  India, Mexico and Türkiye improved through restraint in primary spending; debt above 84 percent in India. p. 15: debt ratios projected to
  decline or stabilise in India and Argentina, helped by strong nominal growth and consolidation.
- p. 16: low-income developing countries: interest payments hit a historic high of 2.3 percent of GDP, nearly 15 percent of revenue, more
  than 5 points above 2015; tax revenue rose to 11.3 percent of GDP as aid fell.
- p. xi: global debt-at-risk three years ahead is near 117 percent of GDP, about 20 points above the median; a prolonged Middle East war
  could add 4 points, a 20 percent fall in US stocks after an AI valuation correction 2.4 points (p. xii).
- p. xii: as central banks shrink balance sheets, leveraged private investors (hedge funds) are the marginal buyers of government bonds;
  rising Treasury supply is compressing the US safety premium, and supply-driven rises in US yields spill over "almost one for one" to
  foreign bond markets. The US has shortened debt maturities to manage interest bills.
- p. xii (policy): for the US, stabilising debt needs revenue and spending action including major entitlement programmes; Europe must
  reconcile defence with ageing; China should keep near-term support until reflation is durable, then consolidate and restructure LGFV
  debt; central bank independence and fiscal frameworks must be safeguarded.

### DF-IMF-3. Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, "Growth Forecast Errors and Fiscal Multipliers", IMF Working Paper WP/13/1, January 2013 (the follow-up to WEO October 2012 Box 1.1)
https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/wp/2013/wp1301.pdf (printed page = PDF page – 1). The WEO October 2012 chapter files themselves
could not be fetched by curl (see Not obtained); the Box 1.1 findings are recorded in the existing note `IMF_2010-2014.md` (WEO October 2012
entry, Box 1.1 pp. 41–43, read earlier in the browser), summarised in the last bullet. Read: abstract, pp. 3–4 (introduction), pp. 19–20
(conclusions).
- Abstract: in advanced economies, stronger planned fiscal consolidation was associated with lower growth than forecast, especially early
  in the crisis; "fiscal multipliers were substantially higher than implicitly assumed by forecasters". The weaker relation later may
  reflect learning by forecasters and smaller multipliers.
- p. 3: method: regress the real GDP growth forecast error on the planned fiscal consolidation; under rational expectations and a correct
  model the coefficient should be zero; a negative one means multipliers were underestimated. The October 2012 box focused on European
  forecasts made in early 2010, when large multiyear consolidation plans were announced.
- pp. 3–4: reasons multipliers were likely large: the zero lower bound stopped central banks offsetting consolidation; weak income and
  impaired finance made spending depend on current income; studies find multipliers larger with slack (Auerbach and Gorodnichenko: near
  zero in normal times to about 2.5 in recessions, US data).
- p. 4: baseline (spring 2010 WEO forecasts, 26 European economies): a 1-point-of-GDP larger planned consolidation for 2010–11 went with
  a GDP loss of about 1 percent relative to forecast, so implicit multipliers were "on average, too low by about 1".
- p. 19: forecasters probably assumed multipliers of about 0.5 at the start of the crisis (pre-crisis WEO estimates averaged 0.5; the
  March 2009 G-20 staff note assumed 0.3–0.5 for revenue and 0.3–1.8 for spending), so actual multipliers were "substantially above 1"
  early in the crisis.
- p. 20: caveats: no single multiplier; multipliers should return to pre-crisis levels once economies exit the liquidity trap, but "for the
  time being" it is safe to assume higher ones. The results "do not imply that fiscal consolidation is undesirable".
- From `IMF_2010-2014.md` (WEO Oct 2012 Box 1.1, pp. 41–43, by Blanchard and Leigh): 28 economies; multipliers underlying forecasts too low
  by 0.4 to 1.2 across specifications; actual multipliers since the Great Recession in the 0.9 to 1.7 range.

### DF-IMF-6. IMF, World Economic Outlook April 2023, Chapter 3 "Coming Down to Earth: How to Tackle Soaring Public Debt", April 2023
Issue page: https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WEO/Issues/2023/04/11/world-economic-outlook-april-2023. Chapter PDF:
https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/weo/2023/april/english/ch3.pdf (22 PDF pages, pp. 69–90; printed = PDF + 68). Authors
(p. 69): Sakai Ando, Tamon Asonuma, Alexandre Balduino Sollaci, Giovanni Ganelli, Prachi Mishra (co-lead), Nikhil Patel, Adrian Peralta
Alva (co-lead), Andrea Presbitero. Read: pp. 69–72 (summary, introduction, decomposition), p. 76 (consolidation results), p. 78 (restructuring
decomposition); searched the rest.
- p. 69: global average debt approached 100 percent of GDP in 2020 and is expected to stay above pre-pandemic levels for about half the
  world; rising real interest rates, tighter financial conditions, weak growth and a strong dollar make this a growing challenge. Rising
  domestic-bank holdings of sovereign debt in emerging markets add sovereign–bank feedback risks.
- p. 69 fn 1: earlier work finds consolidations achieve their ex ante objectives with a probability of only 21–65 percent.
- p. 70: debt-reducing consolidations averaged about 0.4 point of GDP in the primary balance, lowering the debt ratio 0.7 point in the
  first year and up to 2.1 points after five years; suitably tailored consolidations drive about half of observed debt-ratio declines.
- p. 70 / p. 76: the chance of success rises from about 50 percent to more than 75 percent when (1) the economy and world are expanding
  with low risk aversion and volatility, (2) initial public debt is high and private credit low (more crowding out), (3) in advanced
  economies, consolidation relies more on spending cuts than on revenue.
- p. 70: but because those conditions often fail and consolidation slows growth, "the average fiscal consolidation has a negligible
  effect on debt ratios". Transfers to state-owned enterprises, contingent liabilities and depreciation can offset efforts; examples
  (p. 76): Mexico 2016 SOE transfers, Greek arrears clearance 2016, Italy contingent liabilities 2013.
- pp. 70–71: restructuring (mostly in emerging and low-income countries) cuts debt ratios on average 3.4 points in the first year and
  8 points after five years; effects are larger with face-value cuts and coordinated large-scale schemes such as HIPC and MDRI. It is
  "by no means a free lunch" (p. 71).
- p. 72: debt-reduction episodes last about five years and cut debt by on average 3, 5 and 10 points a year in advanced, emerging and
  low-income economies. Primary surpluses and then real growth drive reductions in advanced economies; nominal interest always adds to
  debt; growth and inflation matter more in emerging and low-income countries.
- p. 78: with restructuring, debt falls 13 points (EMs) and 18 points (LICs) in reduction episodes, against about 4 and 8 without.
- p. 71 (policy): gradual reducers should consolidate in good times with structural reforms and strong institutions; rapid reducers may
  need bold action "that do not preclude debt restructuring"; the G20 Common Framework needs more predictability, earlier engagement, a
  payment standstill and clarity on comparability of treatment. High inflation can reduce debt but is not a desirable tool, as it hurts
  bond-holders' balance sheets and central-bank credibility.

### DF-WB-1. M. Ayhan Kose, Peter Nagle, Franziska Ohnsorge and Naotaka Sugawara, Global Waves of Debt: Causes and Consequences, World Bank (first published 2020; copy read carries "© 2021")
Listing page: https://www.worldbank.org/en/research/publication/waves-of-debt. PDF (310 pp.):
https://openknowledge.worldbank.org/bitstreams/dfe3e974-564c-533a-b5f6-b416905eef92/download. Main text printed page = PDF – 17 (Foreword
on PDF pp. 13–14, printed xv). Read: Foreword, Overview pp. 1–5, ch. 1 p. 8. The book's data end in 2018, before COVID-19.
- Foreword p. xv: four major debt waves in EMDEs since 1970; the first three ended in crises (Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s, Asian
  crisis of the late 1990s, global financial crisis 2007–09). The fourth began in 2010 with debt reaching $55 trillion in 2018, "the
  largest, broadest, and fastest growing of the four".
- Foreword p. xv: much of the fourth wave takes riskier forms: low-income countries borrow increasingly outside the Paris Club, notably
  from China, some with nondisclosure clauses and collateral; public investment has been falling in many countries as debt rises;
  EMDE growth slower than pre-2007 even as debt grows faster.
- p. 1: global debt reached an all-time high of 230 percent of GDP in 2018; EMDE total debt rose 54 points since 2010 to about 170 percent
  of GDP; low-income-country debt rose from 48 percent of GDP (about $137 billion) in 2010 to 67 percent ($268 billion) in 2018 after a
  steep fall in 2000–10. Foreword p. xv: China accounted for the bulk of the EMDE increase (more than $20 trillion).
- pp. 1–2, the three earlier waves: (1) 1970s–80s, governments in Latin America and low-income Africa borrowing via syndicated loans at
  low real rates; ended in early-1980s crises, resolved by the Brady Plan (loans turned into collateralised bonds) and, for poor
  countries, HIPC and MDRI relief from the mid-1990s; (2) 1990 to early 2000s, East Asian banks and corporates and ECA governments
  borrowing in foreign currency after liberalisation, ending in the 1997–2001 crises; (3) private borrowing in Europe and Central Asia from
  EU "mega-banks", ended by the 2007–09 crisis.
- p. 2: all three began in periods of low real rates and financial innovation, and ended with crises coinciding with global recessions
  (1982, 1991, 2009) or downturns (1998, 2001), triggered by jumps in risk aversion or borrowing costs and sudden stops.
- pp. 2–3: the fourth wave: average EMDE debt rise of almost 7 points of GDP a year since 2010, larger than in earlier waves; debt rose
  in almost 80 percent of EMDEs and by 20 points or more in just over a third. Drivers: very low global rates and search for yield,
  regional banks, local-currency bond demand and nonbank investors.
- p. 3: since 1970, 519 national episodes of rapid debt accumulation in 100 EMDEs, typically +30 points of GDP government debt and +15
  private, lasting about eight years; about half came with financial crises; higher (especially short-term) external debt and lower
  reserves made crises likelier.
- p. 4: the fourth wave "could follow the historical pattern and culminate in financial crises"; risks: a sudden rise in interest rates
  or risk premiums; shift from concessional to market and non-Paris Club lenders raises transparency and collateral concerns.
- pp. 4–5 (policy): manage debt composition (maturity, currency, creditor), debt transparency; robust monetary, exchange-rate and fiscal
  frameworks (fiscal rules); proactive financial supervision; better use of borrowed funds and institutions.

### DF-WB-2. World Bank, International Debt Report 2026, Chapter 1 "Analysis of External Debt Stocks and Debt Flows as of End-2025", 2026
Listing page: https://www.worldbank.org/en/programs/debt-statistics/idr/products (lists IDR 2026, handle https://hdl.handle.net/10986/45762,

(flows, net transfers, debt service, interest), pp. 44–45 (debt reorganisation); searched the rest. External debt only (owed to
nonresidents), for 119 low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) plus Guyana (p. 4).
- p. 3: LMIC external debt reached a record $9.4 trillion at end-2025, up 5.5 percent from $8.9 trillion; it has grown about 60 percent in
  a decade (Table 1.1, p. 4: $5,915.7 billion in 2015, $9,445.0 billion in 2025). Net debt inflows were $423.5 billion, more than double
  2024, plus a valuation effect from dollar depreciation (the dollar's biggest fall in more than five decades in the first half of 2025).
  Private lending rebounded, showing "the procyclical nature of the private creditor lending".
- p. 6: China, nearly a quarter of LMIC debt, cut its external debt 4.3 percent to $2.3 trillion; low-income countries' debt rose 8.9
  percent to $262.4 billion. p. 10: IDA-eligible countries' debt rose 8.1 percent to $1.3 trillion; over 60 percent of low-income countries
  are at high risk of or already in debt distress. At least 28.2 percent of the 2025 rise in public and publicly guaranteed debt reflects
  dollar depreciation.
- p. 17: excluding China (four straight years of net debt outflows), LMICs' net debt inflows hit a record $527.6 billion in 2025.
- p. 22: long-term net transfers (disbursements minus debt service) were negative for three years from 2022, i.e. LMICs paid more in
  interest and principal than they received; in 2025 they turned positive, from an outflow of $118.4 billion in 2024 to an inflow of
  $45.4 billion; excluding China, from –$48.5 billion to +$200.5 billion.
- pp. 24–25: total debt service rose for the fifth year in a row (+1.9 percent); payments to bondholders rose 13.7 percent to $363.1
  billion (partly buybacks; net of $34.5 billion of buybacks and prepayments, total debt service fell 0.7 percent). Principal repayments
  $973.3 billion; interest payments on external debt about unchanged at $425.5 billion; China accounted for 30.0 percent of debt service.
- p. 26: average interest rate on new private-creditor commitments 5.7 percent in 2025, almost double the official-creditor rate (3.0
  percent); high-yield issuers were effectively shut out of markets in 2022–23 and returned in 2025.
- pp. 44–45: debt reorganisation fell from a record $90 billion in 2024; 2025 was dominated by voluntary bond buybacks and exchanges in
  middle-income countries and by implementing restructurings: G-20 Common Framework MoUs for Ghana (signed December 2023) and Zambia (June
  2024), and Sri Lanka outside the Common Framework (June 2024); all three concluded bondholder deals in 2024; an MoU was also signed with
  Ethiopia. No new Common Framework or Paris Club requests in 2025. The Common Framework was created in 2020 as the coordinating mechanism
  for IDA-eligible countries; 2025 reforms via the Global Sovereign Debt Roundtable included early publication of restructuring
  parameters (NPV reduction, debt-service cut in the programme period, duration change) to signal comparability of treatment.

### DF-OTH-1. Olivier J. Blanchard, "Public Debt and Low Interest Rates", AEA Presidential Address (American Economic Review 109(4), 2019); copy read: NBER Working Paper 25621, February 2019
AEA article page (abstract only; full text behind subscription): https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/aer.109.4.1197. The PIIE
working-paper copy returned HTTP 403 (see Not obtained). Text read: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w25621/w25621.pdf
(author's working-paper version dated February 25, 2019; printed page = PDF – 1). Read: abstract, introduction pp. 1–8, section 1 pp. 8–17.
- Abstract: safe rates expected to stay below growth rates is "more the historical norm than the exception"; if the future is like the
  past, debt rollovers (issuing debt without later raising taxes) may be feasible: "public debt may have no fiscal cost". Debt may still
  have welfare costs by crowding out capital, but these may be smaller than assumed because a safe rate below growth signals a low
  risk-adjusted return to capital.
- p. 2 (dated early 2019): US 10-year rate about 3 percent against forecast nominal growth about 4 percent; UK 1.3 vs about 3.6; euro 1.2
  vs about 3.2; Japan 0.1 vs about 1.4.
- p. 11: US 1950–2018: the 1-year rate averaged 4.7 percent, the 10-year 5.6 percent, nominal GDP growth 6.3 percent; the 1-year rate was
  below growth in every decade except the 1980s, the 10-year rate in 4 of 7 decades. p. 13: after tax and maturity adjustment the relevant
  rate averaged 3.8 percent against 6.3 percent growth (about 2.4 percent in 2018).
- p. 15: "if we assume that the future will be like the past (admittedly a big if), debt rollovers appear feasible"; debt may rise after
  bad shocks but eventually falls. p. 17: counterfactuals with a zero primary balance from each start year show the debt ratio would
  eventually have fallen; this is "at striking variance" with fiscal-space discussions premised on r > g.
- pp. 7–8: at the effective lower bound with a low neutral rate, the case for fiscal support to demand is strong and debt's costs may be
  lower than assumed. Counterarguments: low safe rates may reflect distortions (financial repression, regulation); the future may differ
  and safe rates rise; multiple equilibria (risk premia making debt self-fulfillingly risky), which he says do not pin down a right debt
  level.
- p. 8: "The purpose of the lecture is not to advocate for higher public debt" but to assess its costs.
- Hindsight check against DF-IMF-2: by 2025–26 interest costs were rising (global interest payments from 2 to nearly 3 percent of GDP in

## Synthesis: debt and fiscal policy, 2000–2026

1. **The level: a third wave of post-war public debt, now near 100 percent of world GDP.** World general government debt went from 82.1
   percent of GDP in 2019 to 97.4 in 2020 and, after a dip, 93.9 in 2025, with 100 percent projected for 2029 (DF-IMF-2 Table 1.2); in
   October 2024 the IMF had put 100 percent "by 2030" (DF-IMF-1 p. 1), so the projected date moved a year closer in 18 months. China and
   the US drive the rise (DF-IMF-1 fn 1; DF-IMF-2: US 123.9 to 142.1, China 99.2 to 126.8 by 2031). IMF projections have an optimism bias
   of about 6 points of GDP at three years (DF-IMF-1 p. 1), and debt-at-risk was 115 percent (2024) and about 117 percent (2026) three
   years ahead (DF-IMF-1 p. 5; DF-IMF-2 p. xi).
2. **Crisis responses: stimulus, then the multiplier lesson, then the pandemic.** In late 2008 the IMF called broad-based stimulus "likely
   warranted" where space existed (DF-IMF-5). The 2010–11 European consolidations then hurt growth more than forecast: multipliers were
   underestimated by about 1, actual ones well above 1 at the zero bound (DF-IMF-3). The pandemic brought $11.7 trillion of fiscal action by
   September 2020 and $16 trillion by March 2021 (DF-IMF-4). India's escape clause (0.5 point) was swamped as the centre's deficit hit 9.2
   percent of GDP (DF-IN-3 Annex 1; DF-IN-8 p. 257).
3. **r versus g: the central intellectual argument, and how it has shifted.** Blanchard (DF-OTH-1) argued that with safe rates below growth
   as the historical US norm, debt may have "no fiscal cost"; India's Economic Survey 2020-21 adopted this to argue for counter-cyclical
   spending, with India's negative r – g coming from high growth, not low rates (DF-IN-5 p. 43). The FRBM Review Committee had earlier
   warned that r and g tend to move together, so a growth surprise does not change the debt anchor (DF-IN-2 p. 9). By 2023–26 the IMF and
   BIS stress rising interest costs: global interest from 2 to nearly 3 percent of GDP in four years, US interest near 5 percent by 2031
   (DF-IMF-2 pp. xi, 10), r – g as the variable that narrows the "region of stability" and risks fiscal dominance (DF-OTH-2).
   **Disagreement:** DF-OTH-1/DF-IN-5 versus DF-IMF-2/DF-OTH-2 on how much weight low r deserves; Blanchard himself flagged the risk that
   "the future may be very different" (DF-OTH-1 pp. 7–8).
4. **What actually reduces debt ratios.** The IMF's WEO 2023 finds the average consolidation has a "negligible effect" on debt ratios, but
   well-timed (in expansions), spending-based (in advanced economies) consolidations raise the success chance from about 50 to over 75
   percent; growth and inflation matter more in emerging economies; restructuring cuts debt 3.4 points in a year and 8 in five (DF-IMF-6
   pp. 70–72). The FM 2024 says the needed adjustment (3.0–4.5 percent of GDP) exceeds past adjustments of 2½ percent (DF-IMF-1 pp. 12, 19).
   India's own record fits: debt fell from 83–84 percent in 2003-04 to about 68–70 percent by 2007-10 mostly through growth and revenue
   buoyancy (DF-IN-5 p. 60; DF-IN-2 p. 22; DF-IN-8 p. 260). The figures differ slightly (DF-IN-5: 83 to about 70 by 2009-10; DF-IN-8: 83.6 to
   67.9 by 2007-08) because the end years and definitions differ.
5. **Developing-country debt: from the "fourth wave" warning to restructuring and recovery of flows.** The World Bank warned in 2019–20
   that the fourth wave (from 2010) was the largest, fastest and broadest, with riskier non-Paris Club and collateralised lending
   (DF-WB-1). After the DSSI ($12.9 billion suspended, one private creditor; DF-WB-3), net transfers on long-term external debt were
   negative in 2022–24 and turned positive in 2025 as private lenders returned at about 5.7 percent; Ghana, Zambia (Common Framework) and Sri
   Lanka (outside it) finished restructurings (DF-WB-2 pp. 22, 26, 44–45). WEO 2023 called for a more predictable Common Framework
   (DF-IMF-6 p. 71). **Tension:** DF-WB-1 expected the wave might "culminate in financial crises"; by 2025 the outcome was concentrated
   distress (over 60 percent of LICs at high risk or in distress; LIC interest 15 percent of revenue) rather than an EM-wide crisis
   (DF-WB-2 p. 10; DF-IMF-2 p. 16).
6. **India's fiscal framework: from deficit rules to a debt anchor.** The 2003 FRBM rules (3 percent deficit, nil revenue deficit by 2009)
   were met on paper by 2007-08 but suspended in 2008-09 (DF-IN-2 pp. 21–22). The 2018 amendment set 60/40 debt targets for 2024-25 with a
   0.5-point escape clause (DF-IN-1). Those dates passed unmet (central debt 57.1 percent in 2024-25, DF-IN-3 para 13; 56.1 in 2025-26,
   DF-IN-4). From 2026-27 the operational goal is central debt of about 50±1 percent by 31 March 2031, framed as a shift from rigid "rules"
   to flexible "standards" (DF-IN-3 Annex 1; DF-IN-4 para 92). Quality of spending improved: capex about 3 percent of GDP, effective capex
   4 percent, revenue spending down (DF-IN-6 p. 37).
   **Disagreements:** (a) Path: the 16th Finance Commission's roadmap (Union deficit to 3.5 percent by 2030-31) gives Union debt of 47.6
   percent, below the government's 50±1 (DF-IN-8 p. 258); the IMF calls 50±1 achievable without new measures and wants it more ambitious,
   to include states, with annual paths and an independent fiscal body, which the authorities reject (DF-IN-7 PDF 16–18). The FRBM Review
   Committee had also recommended a Fiscal Council in 2017 (DF-IN-2 p. 9). (b) Level of general government debt: IMF 81.6 percent (FY25)
   and 84.1 percent (2025) in two IMF sources (DF-IN-7 PDF 4; DF-IMF-2 Table 1.2, which may use a different year basis); 80.5 percent for
   2023-24 in the 16th FC (DF-IN-8 Table 3.7, external debt at historical rates); the Survey stresses a 7.1-point fall since 2020 (DF-IN-6
   p. 73). IMF projections to 2030-31 (77 percent, DF-IN-7; 77.7 for 2031, DF-IMF-2) sit above the FC's 73.1 percent (DF-IN-8). (c) The
   2017 dissent (Subramanian) preferred a primary-balance target over a deficit target (DF-IN-2 PDF 189).
7. **States: the new pressure point.** States' deficits rose from 2.6 to 3.2 percent of GDP (FY22–FY25) and debt is about 28 percent of GDP;
   unconditional cash transfers worth about ₹1.7 lakh crore spread to many States, half of them in revenue deficit, and markets barely
   price state fiscal differences (DF-IN-6 pp. 64–65, 72). The 16th FC keeps the 3 percent of GSDP cap and notes States that amend their
   FRBM laws to shift targets (DF-IN-8 pp. 257, 260); the IMF wants state debt inside the anchor (DF-IN-7 PDF 17). India's interest bill
   (over 5 percent of GDP; more than a quarter of revenue) is high, though debt is long, fixed-rate, rupee and resident-held (DF-IN-7 PDF
   39, 60; DF-IN-6 p. 72).
8. **New fiscal pressures 2024–26: defence, tariffs, energy.** EU escape clauses for defence, Germany's debt rising to about 74 percent,
   defence spending judged "unlikely to be transitory" (DF-IMF-2 p. 13); a Middle East war adds energy and interest-rate risk (DF-IMF-2
   pp. xi–xii); US 50 percent tariffs on India in the IMF baseline (DF-IN-7). The October 2026 FM turns to "taxing better" because fiscal
   space for tax cuts is limited (DF-IMF-2b).
