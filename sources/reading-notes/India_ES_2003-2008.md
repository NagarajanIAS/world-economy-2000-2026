# India: Economic Surveys 2003-04 to 2007-08 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of the boom-years chapter (2003 to mid-2007). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book.

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2003-04, 2004-05, 2005-06, 2006-07 and 2007-08. These are the official English PDFs from indiabudget.gov.in (archive index pages are listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md`). The notes were read from the `pdftotext -layout` extracts in `Econ Subject/sources/text/es<YEAR>.txt`.

**How pages are cited.** "p." means the **printed** page number, taken from the running header or footer. "st." means the page of the stitched PDF, counted as form feeds + 1. The two are related as follows:

| Survey | Chapters used | Printed vs stitched |
|---|---|---|
| 2003-04 (interim Survey, published after the May 2004 election; data run to June 2004) | Ch 1 General Review (appended at the END of the stitched file, st. 613-629); Ch 3 Monetary and Banking Developments; Ch 6 External Sector | Ch 1: printed = st. − 612. Ch 3 (first copy): printed = st. − 15. Ch 6: printed = st. − 84. Each chapter appears twice in this file (once as the whole-chapter PDF, once as section PDFs); I cite the first copy. |
| 2004-05 | Ch 1 General Review; Ch 6 External Sector | Ch 1: printed = st. Ch 6: printed = st. + 3 |
| 2005-06 | Ch 1 General Review; Ch 6 External Sector | printed = st. throughout |
| 2006-07 | Ch 1 General Review; Ch 6 External Sector | Ch 1: printed = st. Ch 6: printed = st. − 1 |
| 2007-08 | Ch 1 State of the Economy; Ch 2 Challenges, Policy Response and Medium Term Prospects; Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management; Ch 6 External Sector | Ch 1 pp. 1-17: printed = st. From Ch 2 on: printed = st. − 1 (the Ch 1 tables, st. 18, carry no page number) |

The extracts have two columns side by side, so lines from the two columns are mixed together. Where a paragraph number had to be worked out from the column order, I say so. Quotes are copied exactly from the extract and are under 15 words each.

## Economic Survey 2003-04

**ES03-1 — Growth: 8.1% and a "resilient" economy.** Ch 1 General Review, para 1.1, p. 1 (st. 613).
Real GDP grew an estimated 8.1% in 2003-04 (CSO Advance Estimate, factor cost, 1993-94 prices). A footnote gives the Revised Estimate as 8.2%. The Survey calls the economy "resilient" on growth, inflation and the balance of payments. It also warns that India had grown faster than 8% in only three earlier years (1967-68, 1975-76, 1988-89), and each time the boom followed a bad monsoon year. Here 2002-03 grew 4.0%.
Quote: "resilient mode in terms of growth, inflation, and balance of payments".
Also para 1.3: GDP grew 10.4% in Q3 2003-04, "the highest in any quarter since at least 1997-98".

**ES03-2 — A benign world economy.** Ch 1, para 1.4, p. 1 (st. 613).
"A benign world economic environment" supported India. World output growth rose from 3.0% in 2002 to 3.9% in 2003. The US, China, Russia and a turnaround in Japan led the way. World trade volume grew 4.5%, against 3.1% the year before.

**ES03-3 — A weak dollar and a rising rupee.** Ch 1, para 1.9, pp. 3-4 (st. 615-616).
The rupee had been strengthening since June 2002. On a monthly-average basis it had risen 8.8% against the US dollar by March 2004. On an annual-average basis it appreciated 5.3% in 2003-04, after depreciating 1.5% in 2002-03. Against non-dollar currencies it fell, so the 5-country REER rose only about 2%.
Quote: "caused mainly by widening US deficits" (describing the weakening of the dollar).

**ES03-4 — Reserves: an "unprecedented" rise of US$36.9 billion.** Ch 1, para 1.10, p. 4 (st. 616).
Reserves rose US$21.3 billion in 2002-03, then "increased by an unprecedented US$36.9 billion in 2003-04". They stood at US$119.3 billion on May 31, 2004. Three sources are given: capital inflows, the current-account surplus, and valuation gains as the euro and sterling rose against the dollar.
**FLAG:** The text says reserves "crossed the US$100 billion mark on December 19, 2004". That date is after the Survey went to print (June 2004), so it is almost certainly a typo for December 19, 2003. Check it against RBI data before using it.

**ES03-5 — Monetary policy taken up by the inflow surge; sterilisation.** Ch 1, paras 1.11 and 1.14, pp. 4-5 (st. 616-617).
Para 1.11 says monetary policy in 2003-04 was focused "on dealing with this surge in reserves". The RBI absorbed the inflows (Rs. 124,169 crore in rupee terms) with open market sales of government securities and repo operations under the LAF. Outward investment rules were also eased, and the interest spreads on NRI deposits were cut.
Para 1.14: reserve-money growth roughly doubled, from 9.2% to 18.3%, "driven entirely by the increase in the net foreign exchange assets of the RBI". Net RBI credit to government was negative because of "open market sale of government securities to sterilise the foreign inflows". The RBI's dwindling stock of securities limited how far these operations could go. M3 grew 16.4%, against a 14.0% target.

**ES03-6 — Creating the Market Stabilisation Scheme.** Ch 3 Monetary and Banking Developments, paras 3.7-3.8 and Box 3.1, pp. 48-49 (st. 63-64).
Para 3.7: "Dealing with the surge in capital flows became an important part of monetary management." The RBI's holdings of marketable government securities fell from Rs. 1,16,444 crore (March 31, 2003) to Rs. 44,217 crore (March 31, 2004).
Para 3.8: these measures were "considered inadequate", so the Government and the RBI signed an MoU on March 25, 2004 to set up the MSS, effective April 2004.
Box 3.1: under the MSS the Government issues extra T-bills or dated securities and holds the cash proceeds with the RBI. The initial ceiling for 2004-05 was Rs. 60,000 crore. The RBI's foreign exchange assets (adjusted for revaluation) rose Rs. 1,41,428 crore in 2003-04, against Rs. 82,090 crore in 2002-03.

**ES03-7 — Saving and investment rising, with a current-account surplus.** Ch 1, paras 1.33 and 1.35, Table 1.4, pp. 8-9 (st. 620-621).
Gross domestic saving rose from 23.5% of GDP (2001-02) to 24.2% (2002-03, Quick Estimate). GDCF at 1993-94 prices was 25.8% of GDP, against 25.1%. The saving-investment gap in Table 1.4 was +0.9% of GDP in 2002-03, meaning saving exceeded investment. (This is the old 1993-94 series. Later Surveys revise these levels up a lot; see ES05-3 and ES06-4.)

**ES03-8 — The surplus will shrink as India moves into a higher growth phase.** Ch 1, para 1.41, p. 11 (st. 623). The paragraph starts in the left column and finishes at the top of the right column.
The current-account surplus has helped sustain aggregate demand. The Survey expects it to fall as investment picks up.
Quote: "transits to a sustained growth phase at a higher rate".

**ES03-9 — Global risks: US imbalances and the Iraq war.** Ch 1, paras 1.56-1.57, p. 13 (st. 625).
"Concerns about structural imbalances in the US economy" remain, namely the large current-account and fiscal deficits, along with the situation in Iraq. Even so, the global setting still provides "a benign backdrop". The first challenge listed in para 1.57 is to reach "annual average growth of 7-8 per cent in the next five years".

**ES03-10 — Oil, reserves and the policy mix.** Ch 1, para 1.64, p. 15 (st. 627).
The oil-price surge is unlikely to stop the recovery, but if it lasts it could slow growth and push up inflation. If reserves keep rising after the high M3 growth of 2003-04, that "will require a dexterous combination of policies". Examples are external liberalisation and sterilisation. The aim is to reconcile limited fiscal costs, orderly foreign exchange markets and low inflation.

**ES03-11 — World outlook: risk of a disorderly unwinding of US deficits.** Ch 6 External Sector, para 6.4, p. 100 (st. 184).
Structural US imbalances (large current-account and fiscal deficits) "remain serious concerns". The worry is their effect on financial and currency markets "in the event of their disorderly resolution". Rising global interest rates, especially in the US, could also shift portfolio money away from emerging markets.
Table 6.1, p. 99 (WEO April 2004): net private capital flows to emerging and developing economies were US$47.0bn (2002) and US$131.3bn (2003), with US$162.9bn projected for 2004.

**ES03-12 — Software and offshoring.** Ch 6, paras 6.11-6.12, pp. 102-103 (st. 186-187). The 6.11 number is inferred from column order.
Net software inflows were US$6.9bn (2001-02) and US$8.9bn (2002-03). In 2003-04 they passed US$9.0bn in the first three quarters alone. Para 6.12 credits the "high skill-intensity of the Indian workforce" and calls India "one of the most preferred destinations for offshoring of IT services". Para 6.80 (p. 130) criticises "recent protectionist measures by some countries against BPO".

**ES03-13 — Reserves: US$113.0bn, the sixth-largest holding.** Ch 6, paras 6.64-6.67 and Box 6.5, pp. 126-127 (st. 210-211).
Para 6.64: total reserves (including gold, SDRs and the IMF reserve position) were US$113.0 billion in 2003-04, after a US$36.9bn accretion. India was "the sixth largest reserve-holder in the world".
Para 6.66: an RBI study found capital flows were the largest source of reserve growth from 1991-92 to December 2003 (Table 6.14). Para 6.67: the MSS was introduced in April 2004.
Box 6.5 "Reserve adequacy": reserves were US$118.5bn at end-April 2004. The import cover was almost 14 months (2002-03). The Box warns of the cost of holding reserves, including the "consequences of prolonged sterilization on domestic money supply and price levels".
**Note on figures:** US$113.0bn at end-March 2004 (Ch 6), US$118.5bn at end-April (Box 6.5) and US$119.3bn on May 31 (Ch 1) are different dates, not contradictions.

**ES03-14 — Reserve management as a policy challenge.** Ch 6, paras 6.78-6.79, p. 130 (st. 214).
Para 6.78 sets out the exchange-rate policy: manage volatility, with "no fixed rate target". Para 6.79 says absorbing capital flows productively is the task.
Quote: "Prudent reserve management has emerged as an important macroeconomic policy challenge".

## Economic Survey 2004-05

**ES04-1 — Growth: 8.5% in 2003-04 (revised); 6.9% expected in 2004-05.** Ch 1 General Review, paras 1.1-1.2, p. 1.
Growth in 2003-04 is now put at 8.5%, "the highest ever except in 1975-76 and 1988-89". For 2004-05 the CSO Advance Estimate (February 7, 2005) is 6.9%, despite a 13% monsoon shortfall, dearer oil and steel, and the tsunami.

**ES04-2 — The trend rate of growth "ratcheting up".** Ch 1, para 1.57, p. 14.
2003-04 and 2004-05 suggest "a possible ratcheting up of the trend rate of growth", from about 6% to about 7% a year (Fig. 1.3 "India: Accelerating Growth"). The Survey accepts that a business-cycle reading also fits, but says "a trend acceleration may be underway". It points to double-digit growth in capital goods and consumer durables, and to rising saving and investment rates.
Para 1.58 (pp. 14-15) warns that the NCMP target of 7-8% growth needs more investment. "Even after its increase", the investment rate is "far below that in China and East Asia".

**ES04-3 — Record saving rate.** Ch 1, paras 1.39-1.40, p. 9, and Table 1.3, p. 10.
Gross domestic saving rose from 23.4% of GDP (2001-02) to 26.1% (2002-03) and 28.1% (2003-04). Investment rose from 22.6% to 24.8% and then 26.3%. "The savings rate in 2003-04 is the highest recorded so far". The investment rate was the highest since 1996-97. Para 1.40 says the gain came from smaller public dissaving plus higher household and corporate saving.
**FLAG:** the 2002-03 saving rate is 24.2% in ES 2003-04 and 26.1% here, because of data revision. Always say which Survey a figure comes from.

**ES04-4 — The rupee and the US twin deficits.** Ch 1, para 1.12, p. 4.
"For the last two financial years", the rupee had strengthened against the dollar "with widening fiscal and current account deficits in the United States". The trend briefly reversed in May-August 2004, as markets expected higher US interest rates and portfolio money flowed out. The rupee resumed strengthening from September 2004 (para 1.13, pp. 4-5).

**ES04-5 — Reserves larger than external debt; slower accretion welcomed.** Ch 1, para 1.15, p. 5.
Reserves were US$128.91 billion on February 4, 2005, more than total external debt of US$114 billion (end-September 2004). Accretion so far in the year was just over US$15.9bn, against more than US$31bn in the same period a year earlier. This slowdown is "not entirely an unwelcome development". Three reasons are given: a current-account deficit, lower NRI deposits, and smaller valuation gains.

**ES04-6 — Inflation spike and first tightening (CRR, reverse repo); MSS ceiling raised.** Ch 1, paras 1.7, 1.11 and 1.24, pp. 3-7.
Para 1.7: inflation peaked at 8.7% on August 28, 2004. It was driven by global oil prices and by "monetary overhang from accretion of foreign exchange reserves".
Para 1.11: the RBI raised the CRR in two stages (from September 18 and October 2, 2004) and the reverse-repo rate (October 27, 2004).
Para 1.24: a liquidity overhang of "over Rs.81,000 crore" carried over from 2003-04. The MSS limit was raised from Rs. 60,000 crore to Rs. 80,000 crore on August 26, 2004, and the CRR went up 50 basis points to 5%.

**ES04-7 — Current account turns to deficit; software exports still strong.** Ch 1, para 1.16, p. 5.
After three years of surplus, the current account showed a deficit of US$3.2 billion in April-September 2004. The causes were a large trade deficit (high oil prices plus non-oil imports) and slower growth of invisibles. Software services exports "remained buoyant with 28.7 per cent growth" in the first half of 2004-05.

**ES04-8 — Box 6.1: a declining dollar and the risk of disorderly adjustment.** Ch 6 External Sector, Box 6.1 "Impact of a declining US dollar and its consequences", p. 108 (st. 105).
The dollar's fall is traced to "deepening structural imbalances in the US economy". The US fiscal balance went from a 1.3% surplus (2000) to a 4.9% deficit (2004). The US current-account deficit widened from 4.2% to 5.4% of GDP. If confidence in the dollar broke, the US might have to raise interest rates. The Box also notes that China had not revalued, and that a later revaluation of the renminbi could trigger "unanticipated and jerky realignments in the currency markets" of emerging economies.

**ES04-9 — Global risks: interest rates, imbalances, misalignment.** Ch 6, para 6.4, pp. 108-109 (st. 105-106). The paragraph starts in the right column of p. 108.
High energy prices and "unanticipated inflationary pressures" could bring tighter monetary policy and "higher interest rates in advanced economies". That could shift portfolios and make capital flows to emerging markets volatile. The US current-account and fiscal deficits keep growing, "intensifying macroeconomic imbalances" and "perpetuating concerns over misalignments in global foreign exchange markets and over sudden adjustments".
Para 6.5, p. 109: reserves grew US$31.4bn in 2003-04 excluding valuation changes, and almost one-third came from the current-account surplus.

**ES04-10 — Outlook: services "eclipsing" merchandise.** Ch 6, paras 6.78-6.81, p. 139 (st. 136).
Para 6.78 lists the main global risks: "Global imbalances and their potential disruptive impact on currency markets", rising interest rates, volatile crude prices, and an uncertain soft landing in China.
Para 6.79 notes "subtle shifts in international comparative advantage" and expects capital flows to stay buoyant.
Quote: "software, business and other commercial services eclipsing the performance of merchandise exports".
Para 6.81 restates the exchange-rate policy and says further liberalisation would "counter some of the upward pressure on the exchange rate of the rupee".

## Economic Survey 2005-06

Note: national accounts moved to base year 1999-2000 in this Survey (Box 1.1). Growth and ratios differ from earlier Surveys.

**ES05-1 — Growth: 8.5%, 7.5%, 8.1%; "a new phase of cyclical upswing".** Ch 1 General Review, paras 1.1-1.4, p. 1.
Growth was 8.5% (2003-04) and 7.5% (2004-05), and 8.1% is projected for 2005-06. India has grown faster than 8% in only five recorded years, two of them in the last three years. Para 1.3: Tenth Plan growth averaged 7.0% over the first four years, or 8.0% if the first year is left out.
Quote (para 1.4): "the beginning of a new phase of cyclical upswing in the economy". The Survey dates it from 2003-04.

**ES05-2 — Investment-led growth; a virtuous cycle; East Asian comparison.** Ch 1, paras 1.64-1.66, pp. 17-18.
Investment rose from 23.0% of GDP (2001-02) to 30.1% (2004-05). With 8.1% expected in 2005-06, compound growth over three years should top 8.0%. Para 1.65: "The odds are loaded heavily in favour of a continuation" of the growth momentum. "A virtuous cycle of growth and savings" seems to be under way, helped by demographics.
Once the investment gap with East Asia closes, India "should start posting growth rates close to the rates observed then" in East Asia. Para 1.66 closes with the aim of a "sustained high-growth trajectory".

**ES05-3 — Saving 29.1% and investment 30.1% of GDP (new series).** Ch 1, paras 1.43-1.49 and Table 1.3, pp. 12-13.
The gross domestic saving rate rose from 26.5% (2002-03) to 28.9% (2003-04) and 29.1% (2004-05). The rise came from public and corporate saving, while household saving fell as a share of GDP. Corporate saving went from 3.6% of GDP (2001-02) to 4.8% (2004-05, Quick Estimate).
Investment rose from 27.2% (2003-04) to 30.1% (2004-05), mainly because private investment grew 19.7%. A current-account deficit of 0.8% of GDP "largely bridged the savings-investment gap" (para 1.48).

**ES05-4 — Sterilisation through MSS and LAF, then partly unwound.** Ch 1, paras 1.25-1.26, p. 8.
In 2004-05 part of the growth in net foreign assets "had to be sterilised" through the MSS and the LAF. Outstanding MSS plus LAF reverse repo was Rs. 83,500 crore (3.7% of M3) on March 31, 2005. In 2005-06 NFA growth almost stopped (Rs. 2,043 crore, against Rs. 75,930 crore the year before). The balances were partly unwound to Rs. 40,178 crore (1.6% of M3) by January 20, 2006. The India Millennium Deposit redemption (about Rs. 32,000 crore, December 29, 2005) briefly tightened liquidity.

**ES05-5 — Reserve accretion stalls in 2005-06.** Ch 1, paras 1.29-1.30, p. 9.
Reserves grew US$28.5 billion in 2004-05, reaching US$141.5 billion at end-March 2005. By February 10, 2006 they had fallen US$1.1bn. Three causes are given: US$7.1bn of IMD redemption, valuation losses as the dollar strengthened (US$5.0bn in the first half), and a wider current-account deficit. The rupee appreciated 2.2% against the dollar in 2004-05 and strengthened against all major currencies in the first ten months of 2005-06.

**ES05-6 — A larger current-account deficit.** Ch 1, paras 1.31-1.32, p. 9; also Ch 6 para 6.6, p. 101.
The current-account deficit was about US$13.0 billion in April-September 2005, more than twice the US$5.4bn for all of 2004-05.
Para 6.6: the current account swung from a US$14.1bn surplus (2003-04), or 2.3% of GDP, to a US$5.4bn deficit (2004-05), or 0.8% of GDP. The Survey reads this as reflecting "the rising investment demand in the economy".
**FLAG:** ES 2004-05 Table 6.2 gave the 2003-04 surplus as US$10,561 million, while ES 2005-06 Table 6.2 gives US$14,083 million. This is a BoP data revision.

**ES05-7 — Oil prices "spiraling".** Ch 1, para 1.16, p. 6.
"The sharp and spiraling increase in international oil prices from late 2003", with high volatility, was a serious challenge for macroeconomic stability. The average price of the Indian crude basket rose 44.5%, from US$37.3 a barrel in April-November (2004).

**ES05-8 — Global imbalances and interest-rate risk to the investment boom.** Ch 1, para 1.85, p. 22.
Volatile oil prices make the inflation outlook uncertain, and India "is no longer insulated from the rest of the world in price developments".
Quote: "unresolved global macroeconomic imbalances, casts its shadow on the interest rate scenario".
If global interest rates keep rising, they could dampen "the domestic investment boom".

**ES05-9 — Services exports including software.** Ch 1, para 1.35, p. 10.
Services exports (net non-factor services, including software and IT-enabled services) grew 71.3% in 2004-05 and 75.3% in the first half of 2005-06. Business and professional services are also now driving growth.

**ES05-10 — World outlook: oil and interest rates; China's exchange rate reform; US deficit over 6% of GDP.** Ch 6 External Sector, para 6.4 and Box 6.1, p. 100.
Para 6.4: if crude stays high, inflation expectations driven by energy prices "can also raise global interest rates, and affect global capital flows, particularly to emerging markets".
Box 6.1 "Exchange rate reform in China": the renminbi was delinked from the dollar on July 21, 2005, within a ±0.3% daily band. The pressure came from "widening trade imbalances between the US and developing Asia". The US current-account deficit was "estimated to cross 6 per cent of its GDP in 2005" (WEO September 2005).
Para 6.2 (pp. 99-100): global expansion was carried by the US and developing Asia, "China and India in particular".

**ES05-11 — Software exports and India's share of the global IT market.** Ch 6, para 6.10, pp. 103-104; para 6.39 and Box 6.7, pp. 116-117.
Para 6.10: net earnings from software services rose 34.1%, from US$12.3bn (2003-04) to US$16.5bn (2004-05).
Para 6.39: software service exports grew 34.4% to US$17.2bn in 2004-05. India's share of the world market for IT software and services (including BPO) rose from about 1.7% (2003-04) to 2.3% (2004-05), with 2.8% estimated for 2005-06.
Box 6.7: in 2004-05 India held "65 per cent of the global market in offshore IT services and 46 per cent of the global BPO market".
**FLAG:** US$16.5bn (para 6.10) is net BoP software earnings; US$17.2bn (para 6.39) is software exports. They measure different things, so say which one you use.

## Economic Survey 2006-07

**ES06-1 — Growth: 9.0% and 9.2%; "entrenchment" of higher growth.** Ch 1 General Review, paras 1.1-1.3, p. 1.
Growth was 9.0% in 2005-06 (Quick Estimate, revised up from an 8.1% Advance Estimate) and 9.2% in 2006-07 (Advance Estimate).
Quote: "Entrenchment of the higher growth trends, particularly in manufacturing".
Para 1.3: "The ratcheting up of growth" shows in the Eleventh Plan target of 9%, against 8% in the Tenth Plan. Tenth Plan growth averaged 7.6%, or 8.6% over its last four years. The Survey also notes "genuine concerns on the inflation front".

**ES06-2 — "Taken off": a new phase of high growth.** Ch 1, "Issues and priorities", paras 1.49-1.54, pp. 14-15.
Para 1.49: the economy "appears to have decidedly 'taken off'".
Quote: "moved from a phase of moderate growth to a new phase of high growth".
Para 1.50: "the current growth phase is sustainable". The reasons follow in paras 1.51-1.55: a "growth-savings-growth cycle", better capital efficiency, new service sectors beyond IT, and improving infrastructure.
Para 1.54 takes up overheating. Because India has open and disguised unemployment, the concern there is "capacity utilization and skill shortages", and import growth "appears to be within reasonable limits".

**ES06-3 — Investment surge.** Ch 1, para 1.6, p. 3.
"A notable feature of the current growth phase is the sharp rise in the rate of investment". GDCF for 2004-05 was revised from 30.1% to 31.5% of GDP.

**ES06-4 — Saving 32.4% and investment 33.8% of GDP; the corporate saving surge.** Ch 1, paras 1.32-1.37, Table 1.3, pp. 9-10.
Gross domestic saving was 29.7% (2003-04), 31.1% (2004-05) and 32.4% (2005-06, Quick Estimate). Investment was 28.0%, 31.5% and 33.8%. Private corporate saving was revised up from 4.8% to 7.1% of GDP for 2004-05, and reached 8.1% in 2005-06. Para 1.33 calls this "a dramatic element in the savings profile". Para 1.35 expects the demographic dividend to keep the saving rate high.
**FLAG:** para 1.37 says the saving-investment gap widened "to 1.4 per cent of GDP" in 2005-06, but Table 1.3 shows −1.3. Use the table, or say there is a discrepancy.

**ES06-5 — Inflation in 2006-07.** Ch 1, paras 1.9-1.11, p. 4.
WPI inflation was 6.7% on February 3, 2007, up from 3.98% at the start of the year, with primary goods (mainly food) in the lead. The 52-week average was 5%. The Indian crude basket went from about US$27-28 a barrel (2002-04) to US$75.2 on August 8, 2006, and retail fuel prices were raised on June 6, 2006.

**ES06-6 — Monetary tightening in 2006-07.** Ch 1, paras 1.13-1.16, p. 5.
M3 grew 21.1% year-on-year (January 19, 2007), and bank credit 32.0% (September 2006). In April 2006 the RBI raised provisioning requirements and risk weights (commercial real estate risk weight up from 125% to 150%). The reverse repo went up 25 bp three times in 2005-06 (to 5.50%) and again on June 9 and July 25, 2006. Only the repo rate was raised on October 31, 2006 and January 31, 2007, reaching 7.50%, 150 bp above the reverse repo. The CRR went to 5.25% (December 23, 2006) and 5.50% (January 6, 2007), with 25 bp more on February 17 and March 3, 2007.
Quote (para 1.15): "measures to stem inflationary expectations".

**ES06-7 — The MSS in 2006-07.** Ch 1, para 1.18, p. 6.
NFA drove reserve-money growth. MSS outstanding rose from Rs. 29,062 crore (1.0% of M3) on March 31, 2006 to a peak of Rs. 42,364 crore (1.5%) on August 25, 2006. It stood at Rs. 40,491 crore (1.3%) on January 19, 2007.

**ES06-8 — Reserves: US$151.6bn (end-March 2006) to US$185.1bn (February 2007).** Ch 1, para 1.20, p. 6; Ch 6 paras 6.70-6.71, pp. 130-131 (st. 131-132).
BoP reserve accretion was US$15.1bn in 2005-06 and US$8.6bn in April-September 2006. Reserves were "US$141.5 at end-March 2005" [sic, "billion" missing in the text], US$151.6 billion at end-March 2006, US$165.3 [billion] at end-September 2006 and US$185.1 billion on February 9, 2007.
Ch 6 para 6.71 gives US$180.0bn on February 2, 2007, after US$9.8bn of RBI purchases in April-December 2006.
**FLAG:** US$180.0bn (February 2) and US$185.1bn (February 9) are a week apart. The gap may be valuation, but check before quoting either.

**ES06-9 — Global risks "appear to be limited".** Ch 1, para 1.68, p. 17.
The Survey describes "a sense of optimism". Quote: "a rapid unravelling of global macroeconomic imbalances". This risk, together with volatile oil and delays in the Doha Round, remains, but "for the present, they appear to be limited".

**ES06-10 — Box 6.1 "Global imbalances": US deficit near US$1 trillion; Bretton Woods 2.** Ch 6 External Sector, para 6.3 and Box 6.1, p. 107 (st. 108).
Para 6.3: after five years at about 5% of GDP or more, the US current-account deficit "is expected to be close to US$1 trillion in 2007". This "led to apprehensions about an abrupt unwinding of the global macroeconomic imbalances" and sudden currency moves.
Box 6.1: the US deficit was 4.8% of GDP (2003), 5.7% (2004) and US$791.5bn, 6.4% of GDP (2005). East Asian surpluses were invested in US bonds, which is the "Bretton Woods 2" argument. The Box quotes the IMF (WEO 2005) on the risk of rapid dollar depreciation and higher US rates. It flags cooling US housing and rising bond yields as threats.
Quote: "the need to unwind the global imbalances in an orderly fashion".

**ES06-11 — Oil and overheating in China and India.** Ch 6, para 6.4, p. 107 (st. 108).
Brent averaged US$42.1 a barrel (2004-05) and US$57.9 (2005-06), hit an all-time high of US$78.7 on August 8, 2006, and fell to US$50.7 on January 17, 2007. The Survey notes concerns about whether high growth in "China and India" can last "without overheating and build up of inflationary pressures".
(The Ch 1 figure of US$75.2 is the Indian basket; this one is Brent.)

**ES06-12 — Software and business services drive invisibles.** Ch 1, para 1.24, p. 7; Ch 6, para 6.13, p. 111 (st. 112).
Invisible receipts reached 11.5% of GDP in 2005-06, up from 2.4% in 1990-91. Recent acceleration was "particularly in software and business services". In 2005-06 net receipts from software services grew 31.7% and from business services 209.8%.

**ES06-13 — Rupee: two-way movement, then appreciation.** Ch 6, paras 6.65-6.69, p. 130 (st. 131).
The annual-average rupee went from Rs. 48.40/US$ (2002-03) to Rs. 44.27/US$ (2005-06). In 2006-07 it fell until August 2006 and rose after that. The REER-5 (base 2000=100) was 106.79 in 2005-06, dipped to 100.65 in August 2006, and reached 109.04 in November 2006.

**ES06-14 — "Problems of plenty".** Ch 6, Outlook, paras 6.77-6.79, p. 135 (st. 136).
The external environment should stay supportive (WEO projection for 2007: 4.9%). Para 6.79 says downside risks remain from volatile crude and "large global imbalances". Even so, strong non-debt capital flows will easily cover the current-account deficit.
Quote: "'problems of plenty' with large increases in foreign exchange reserves". The Survey says these bring implications "for monetary management".

## Economic Survey 2007-08

**ES07-1 — "Moved decisively to a higher growth phase".** Ch 1 State of the Economy, para 1.1, p. 1.
Quote: "The economy has moved decisively to a higher growth phase." The extract splits it with a drop cap ("T he economy…").
GDP at market prices grew more than 8% in every year from 2003-04, and 8.7% is projected for 2007-08. The same paragraph names the costs of fast growth: "Inflation flared up in the last half of 2006-07". It also lists the global hardening of commodity prices, "an upsurge in capital inflows", "an appreciation of the rupee", and infrastructure constraints.

**ES07-2 — Per capita income doubling in a decade.** Ch 1, paras 1.3-1.4, p. 1.
Per capita GDP growth was 3.1% a year (1980-81 to 1991-92) and 3.7% (1992-93 to 2002-03). It then rose to 7.2% a year (2003-04 to 2007-08), so "average income would now double in a decade". Per capita consumption growth almost doubled to 5.1% a year.

**ES07-3 — Growth: 9.4% and 9.6%, then 8.7%.** Ch 1, para 1.7, p. 3; Ch 4, para 4.23, p. 70 (st. 71).
The 8.7% projected for 2007-08 is a slowdown "from the unexpectedly high growth of 9.4 per cent and 9.6 per cent" in 2005-06 and 2006-07. Para 4.23 confirms 2006-07: real GDP grew 9.6%, inflation 5.9%, M3 21.3% and non-food credit 28.4%. All were well above the RBI's April 2006 assumptions.
**FLAG:** 2005-06 growth appears as 9.0% in ES 2006-07 and 9.4% here (revised), and 2006-07 as 9.2% (Advance Estimate) in ES 2006-07 and 9.6% here.

**ES07-4 — Saving and investment over the Tenth Plan; the "Capex" boom.** Ch 1, paras 1.16-1.20, p. 6.
Over the five years to 2006-07, investment rose 13.1 percentage points of GDP and saving 11.3 points. Investment went from 25.2% of GDP (2002-03) to 35.9% (2006-07). Gross domestic saving went from 26.4% to 34.8%, averaging 31.4% over the Tenth Plan. Private corporate investment rose from 5.4% of GDP (2001-02) to 14.5% (2006-07), "visible even to the public as a 'Capex' boom".
Para 1.18 reads the negative saving-investment gap since 2004-05 as "a correction of the domestic supply-demand balance".

**ES07-5 — Inflation peaked in March 2007, then eased.** Ch 1, paras 1.31-1.32, p. 10.
WPI inflation was 4.4% (2005-06) and 5.4% (2006-07). The latest rise began from about 4% in February-March 2006 and peaked in March 2007. It fell below 4% in mid-August 2007 and was 4.1% on February 2, 2008. Primary-article inflation fell from 12.2% (April 7, 2007) to 3.8% (January 19, 2008).

**ES07-6 — RBI stance; tightening in 2006-07 and 2007-08.** Ch 1, para 1.35, p. 10; Ch 4 Prices and Monetary Management, paras 4.24-4.26 and Box 4.1, pp. 70-71 (st. 71-72).
Para 1.35: the policy stance aims at "managing the transition to a higher growth path" while containing inflation. After the October 2007 mid-term review it cited "unusual heightened global uncertainties".
Para 4.24: by March 31, 2007 the repo rate was 7.75%, and the CRR had been raised 25 bp on four occasions to 6% on March 3, 2007.
Box 4.1: the CRR went to 6.50% from April 28, 2007 and to 7.5% from November 10, 2007 (mid-term review). Repo and reverse repo were held at 7.75% and 6%.

**ES07-7 — Higher real interest rates.** Ch 1, para 1.38, p. 11.
Real 364-day T-bill yields more than doubled to an average of 3.2% in April-December 2007, from 1.7% a year earlier. This may have curbed credit demand, and it "led to a widening of the interest differential between domestic and global rates", which encourages inflows.

**ES07-8 — MSS limit raised four times to Rs. 2,50,000 crore.** Ch 1, para 1.40, p. 11.
NFA rose to 134.7% of reserve money by January 4, 2008, from 117.4% on March 31, 2006. With "the continuing surge in capital flows during 2007-08", MSS limits "were revised upward four times to a level of Rs. 2,50,000 crore". Absorption in April-December 28, 2007 was Rs. 96,742 crore, with Rs. 1,59,717 crore outstanding. Even so, the higher growth of M0 and M3 "generated higher liquidity in the system".

**ES07-9 — The longest global expansion since the 1970s; sub-prime risk.** Ch 1, para 1.41, pp. 11-12.
The WEO (October 2007) found the recent global expansion, averaging 5% growth, "was the longest since the early 1970s". The January 2008 update revised estimates using new PPP rates. Downside risk comes from the US housing downturn and "the sub-prime mortgage market crisis". Monetary action seems "to have contained its immediate impact", though "more surprises in the next six months cannot be ruled out".

**ES07-10 — Capital inflow surge and rupee appreciation; reserves US$290.8bn.** Ch 1, paras 1.42-1.43, p. 12.
Capital flows rose to 5.1% of GDP in 2006-07.
Quote: "managing a more open capital account with increasing inflows and exchange rate appreciation". The Survey says this problem "surfaced".
Reserve accumulation was US$15.1bn (2005-06) and US$36.6bn (2006-07), over 5% of GDP. Excess capital inflows rose to 7.7% of GDP in April-September 2007. Reserves rose US$91.6bn to US$290.8 billion on February 8, 2008.
**FLAG:** Ch 6 para 6.66 gives US$290.9 billion for the same date. Para 1.43 also says the rupee "depreciated by 2.2 per cent on an overall yearly average basis" in 2006-07, despite upward pressure in the second half.

**ES07-11 — The fiscal cost of sterilisation; the trilemma.** Ch 1, para 1.48, p. 13.
The RBI's portfolio returned 4.6% in 2006-07, "indicating that the effective fiscal cost of sterilization may be 3.2 per cent". The fiscal cost for 2007-08 is "placed at Rs. 8,200 crore". The search for a policy mix that balances "a relatively open capital account, monetary policy independence and flexible exchange rate continues".

**ES07-12 — Rupee appreciation 2007-08.** Ch 1, paras 1.61-1.62, p. 15.
The rupee rose 8.9% against the dollar between April 3, 2007 and February 6, 2008, and 13.2% year-on-year (December 2007 over December 2006). Against other currencies it rose less, and it fell slightly against the euro. The causes given are dollar weakness and "the surge in capital flows". The REER (6-currency) rose 7.8% in April-January 2007-08. Exporters with low import content were hurt, and the Government announced relief measures.

**ES07-13 — Integration: trade openness; services exports.** Ch 1, paras 1.53 and 1.59, pp. 13-14.
Merchandise trade rose from 22.5% of GDP (2000-01) to 34.8% (2006-07), and from 29.2% to 48% including services. Services exports grew 32.1% to US$76.2bn in 2006-07, led by software, business, financial and communication services. They then grew only 8.6% in April-September 2007, because "non-software services" fell.

**ES07-14 — "Best ever five year growth performance".** Ch 1, para 1.78, p. 17.
Quote: "perhaps, the best ever five year growth performance in the history of independent India". The Survey is describing fiscal 2003-08.

**ES07-15 — Ch 2: growth breaks; inflows "far in excess" of needs.** Ch 2 Challenges, Policy Response and Medium Term Prospects, paras 2.2 and 2.5-2.7, pp. 18-19 (st. 19-20).
Para 2.2 finds two statistically significant growth breaks (early 1980s, mid-1990s). Growth was 7.8% over the Tenth Plan, with the last three years averaging over 9%. At 9% for a decade, India would join about a dozen economies such as China, Japan and Korea.
Paras 2.5-2.6: capital inflows averaged about 4% of GDP, "far in excess of the current account financing requirements". That led to reserve build-up and price pressure. Growth draws capital in, and if investment opportunities do not keep pace the currency comes under pressure, giving either reserve accumulation (then inflation) or nominal appreciation, or both. "Particularly in fiscal 2007-08", India went through such a phase.
Para 2.7: the surge "will continue in the medium term".

**ES07-16 — Ch 2: US slowdown and sub-prime; excess inflows likely to persist.** Ch 2, paras 2.13-2.14 and Box 2.1 "US sub-prime crisis", pp. 21-22 (st. 22-23).
Para 2.13: the US is expected to slow in 2008 "consequent to the 'sub-prime crises'". Most projections show a moderate, not severe, world slowdown, with a modest effect on Indian exports as long as a severe US recession is avoided.
Para 2.14: liquidity expansion by developed-country central banks may raise flows to India, while risk aversion may reduce them.
Quote: "the situation of excess inflows is likely to remain". Pressure on reserves and the rupee should ease. In the long run the solution lies in productivity gains and in tackling the interest differential and expectations about the rupee.
Box 2.1 calls sub-prime "the major financial crisis of the new millennium".

**ES07-17 — Ch 6: inflows test absorptive capacity; EM capital flows at US$495bn.** Ch 6 External Sector, para 6.1 and Table 6.1, p. 109 (st. 110).
The 2007-08 inflow surge matched what "other emerging market economies" had seen. Problems of managing a more open capital account showed in "the economy approaching the limits of its absorptive capacity".
Table 6.1 (WEO October 2007): net private capital flows to emerging and developing countries were US$271.1bn (2005), US$220.9bn (2006) and US$495.4bn (2007), with US$291.3bn projected for 2008. Oil prices rose 41.3% (2005) and 20.5% (2006). Non-fuel commodities rose 28.4% in 2006.

**ES07-18 — Box 6.2 "Management of capital flows".** Ch 6, Box 6.2, p. 128 (st. 129).
The excess of net capital inflows over the current-account deficit raised the problem of "managing the contradictions in the trilogy of objectives". The response combined intervention, reserve build-up and sterilisation. Volatile flows (short-term credit and portfolio) were 76.3% of capital flows in 2003-04, 29.9% in 2006-07 and 47% in April-September 2007. With considerable rupee appreciation, "the pass through of capital flows to the monetary sector was partial". MSS limits were raised progressively, and the cost of sterilisation reached Rs. 8,200 crore for 2007-08. The Box says the move to fuller convertibility would stay "gradual, sequenced".

**ES07-19 — Reserves: US$199.2bn at end-March 2007.** Ch 6, paras 6.64-6.66, p. 129 (st. 130).
Reserves rose from US$151.6bn (end-March 2006) to US$199.2bn (end-March 2007), an increase of US$47.6bn that includes a US$10.9bn valuation gain. ECBs and foreign investment were the main sources. Reserves were US$290.9bn on February 8, 2008. The budget estimate for sterilisation costs in 2007-08 was Rs. 3,700 crore, later raised (to Rs. 8,200 crore, per Box 6.2).

**ES07-20 — Software as the main services driver.** Ch 6, paras 6.11-6.12, p. 113 (st. 114).
Software services (IT and ITES) were "the main driver" of the non-factor services surplus. The other six components added only 9% of the net services surplus in 2006-07. Software receipts grew at "an annual average of 32.9 per cent in the last five years". The 6.12/6.13 paragraph break is unclear in the extract; the software sentence follows 6.12 (travel) in the left column.

## Problems and caveats

1. **2003-04 Survey.** This was the interim, post-election Survey (data run to June 2004). Chapter 1 is at the end of the stitched PDF (st. 613-629, printed pp. 1-17), and the other chapters appear twice. Para 1.10 has the date "December 19, 2004" for crossing US$100bn, which is almost certainly a typo for 2003.
2. **Base-year change in 2005-06.** National accounts moved from base 1993-94 to base 1999-2000, so saving, investment and growth figures before ES 2005-06 cannot be compared directly with later ones. The private corporate saving revision in ES 2006-07 (4.8% to 7.1% of GDP for 2004-05) is large.
3. **Internal inconsistencies.** These are listed in the items above:
   - ES 2006-07: saving-investment gap 1.4 (para 1.37) vs −1.3 (Table 1.3); missing "billion" in para 1.20.
   - ES 2007-08: reserves 290.8 vs 290.9.
   - ES 2005-06: software 16.5bn (net earnings) vs 17.2bn (exports).
   - The 2003-04 current-account surplus was revised from US$10.6bn to US$14.1bn.
4. **Paragraph numbers.** A few were worked out from the two-column order: ES03 6.11, ES04 6.4, ES07 6.12. They should be checked against the PDF.
5. **Dollar-rupee figures.** Different paragraphs use annual averages, monthly averages and point-to-point changes. Quote the basis together with the number.
