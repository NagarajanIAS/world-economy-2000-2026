# India: Economic Surveys 2015-16 to 2019-20 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of the "Slow growth and slowbalisation" chapter (mid-2014 to 2019: the oil-price collapse, the 2015 China scare, Brexit and the US election of 2016, the 2018 tariff war and emerging-market sell-off, the 2019 global slowdown). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book. ES 2014-15 is covered in `India_ES_2010-2014.md` and is not repeated here. ES 2020-21 is used only for three retrospective items on 2019-20 (ES20-1 to ES20-3).

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2015-16, 2016-17, 2017-18, 2018-19 and 2019-20, with three items from 2020-21 (official English PDFs from indiabudget.gov.in; archive pages listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md`). Read from the extracts in `Econ Subject/sources/text/es<YEAR>.txt`. Every Survey in this period comes in two volumes, stitched into one file.

**Data cut-offs, as the texts show them.**
- ES 2015-16 (February 2016): CSO Advance Estimates for 2015-16 on the 2011-12 base; reserves to early February 2016; rupee to 12 February 2016.
- ES 2016-17 Vol 1 (January 2017): data to about end-December 2016; Vol 1 Box 3 uses the CSO Advance Estimate of 7.1%. Vol 2 (August 2017) is a mid-year volume with data to the repo cut of 2 August 2017 and the CSO provisional estimate of May 2017.
- ES 2017-18 (January 2018): CSO Advance Estimates (6.5%); reserves to 12 January 2018.
- ES 2018-19 (July 2019, post-election): full-year 2018-19 provisional estimates; reserves to 14 June 2019; repo cuts to 6 June 2019.
- ES 2019-20 (January 2020): First Advance Estimate for 2019-20 (5.0%); reserves to 10 January 2020; CPI to December 2019.
- ES 2020-21 (January 2021): retrospective only.

**GDP vintage.** All growth figures in these Surveys are on the 2011-12-base series (released 30 January 2015; ES19-20 Vol 1 Ch 10, Box 1). Each year's figure is revised several times between Surveys (AE → PE → 1st RE → 2nd RE). See the cross-Survey table and Problems item 2. State the vintage with every growth number used in the book.

## Economic Survey 2015-16

**ES15-1 — India as the "haven of stability" in a weak world.** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 1).
The Survey opens by setting India against a world of slowing growth and financial turbulence. India stands out for macro stability. In the same summary the failure to pass the GST is listed among the year's disappointments.
Quote: "haven of stability and an outpost of opportunity".

**ES15-2 — The 2015 China turbulence and a 1930s-style tail risk.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.21-1.26, p. 7 (st. 7); Box 1.2, p. 8 (st. 8).
The Survey lists the global financial crisis, the mini-crises of 2013 and the China-provoked turbulence of 2015 as a sequence of shocks coming at shorter intervals. It names vulnerabilities in China, Brazil and Saudi Arabia. The tail risk it stresses is a major currency readjustment in Asia following a Chinese devaluation. Box 1.2 notes that China holds more than US$3 trillion in reserves. It argues that a tail event in a systemically important emerging market would set off competitive devaluations, as in the 1930s.
Quote: "resemble more the events of the early 1930s".

**ES15-3 — Growth in 2015-16: 7.6% on the new series, with doubts about the numbers.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.29-1.31, pp. 10-11 (st. 10-11).
CSO Advance Estimates put real GDP growth at 7.6% in 2015-16, against 7.2% in 2014-15. Real GVA growth was 7.3% (7.1%). Manufacturing was put at 9.5% against 5.5%. Nominal GDP growth was only 8.6% (nominal GVA 6.8%), because the WPI deflator was negative. CPI inflation was about 5½%. WPI inflation had been negative since November 2014, mainly because of oil.
FLAG (vintage): these are the first AE numbers. ES 2017-18 V2 Table 0.1 gives 2014-15 at 7.5 and 2015-16 at 8.0 (see table below).

**ES15-4 — Fiscal 2015-16: target met, but nominal growth fell far short.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.34-1.35, pp. 12-13 (st. 12-13).
The Budget had assumed nominal GDP growth of 11.5%; the AE was 8.6%. The fiscal-deficit target of 3.9% of GDP was nevertheless expected to be met. On the IMF definition the deficit falls from 4.2% to 4.0%. Excise collections on petroleum products rose 90.5%, to about Rs 1.3 lakh crore from Rs 0.7 lakh crore, as the government raised duties while oil fell.

**ES15-5 — External position in early 2016.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.32, p. 12 (st. 12).
Reserves reached US$351.5bn in early February 2016. FDI rose from US$21.9bn to US$27.7bn (April-December, year on year). The Survey notes that the US Federal Reserve's December 2015 rate rise passed without disruption.

**ES15-6 — India's growth is now more tied to the world's.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.40-1.41, p. 15 (st. 15).
The correlation between India's growth and world growth rose from about 0.2 (1991-2002) to 0.42. With world growth stuck near 3%, the Survey judges that India can expect growth of 7-7½%. ES 2019-20 V2 para 1.2 repeats this observation.

**ES15-7 — The oil windfall shrinks.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.45, p. 16 (st. 16).
The Survey assumes the Indian crude basket averages about US$35/bbl in 2016-17, against about US$45 in 2015-16. That implies a gain of about 1% of GDP (an 18% price fall times net oil imports of about 6% of GDP), about half the previous year's gain.

**ES15-8 — Oil: price and import-bill figures in Vol 2.** V2 paras 1.53-1.54, pp. 18-19 (st. 180-181); para 4.12, p. 67 (st. 229); Box 5.1, p. 92 (st. 254).
The Indian basket averaged US$105.5/bbl in 2013-14 and US$84.2 in 2014-15 (para 4.12). Box 5.1 says it fell 46% in April-December 2015. Para 1.54 gives an oil-price fall of 20.2% in 2014-15. POL imports fell 41.4% in April-January 2015-16, to US$73.1bn from US$124.8bn.
FLAG: percentage falls are measured over different spans (calendar, fiscal, April-December). ES 2017-18 Table 1 gives −10.1% for 2014-15 and −46.2% for 2015-16 (ES17-6); ES 2019-20 dates the >50% fall to "2016-17" (ES19-16).

**ES15-9 — Corporate stress builds.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.46, p. 16 (st. 16).
Stressed companies (interest coverage below 1) accounted for 41% of corporate debt in December 2015, against 35% a year earlier.

**ES15-10 — Outlook for 2016-17: 7-7¾%.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.50, p. 16 (st. 16).
Growth in 2016-17 is projected at 7-7¾%.

**ES15-11 — The "twin balance sheet" problem is named.** V1 Ch 1, Box 1.6, pp. 19-20 (st. 19-20).
This is the first use in these Surveys of the term "twin balance sheet" (TBS). It means the joint stress of public-sector banks and over-leveraged large companies. The Box reviews the tools so far: Indradhanush (August 2015), the 5:25 refinancing scheme and Strategic Debt Restructuring (SDR). It proposes the "4 Rs": recognition, recapitalisation, resolution and reform. It notes that the RBI's equity ratio, about 32%, is double the international median (about 16%), which hints at a possible source of bank capital.
Quote: "impaired financial positions of the Public Sector Banks (PSBs) and some large corporate houses".

**ES15-12 — Inflation: within target, and a possible undershoot.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.51, 1.58 and 1.60, pp. 20-23 (st. 20-23).
Inflation stayed within the RBI's 4-6% range (para 1.51). The RBI's goal of 5% by March 2017 could be undershot (para 1.58). The Survey forecasts CPI inflation of 4½-5% in 2016-17 (para 1.60).

**ES15-13 — Rate cuts in 2015 and weak transmission.** V1 Ch 1, Box 1.7, p. 23 (st. 23); V2 para 1.46, p. 17 (st. 179); V2 Table 3.1, p. 49 (st. 211).
The RBI cut the repo rate four times in 2015, by 125 bps in all, including a 50 bps cut "at the October meeting" (Box wording). Lending rates fell only about 50 bps (Box 1.7). Vol 2 gives the dates: 8.00% to 7.75% (15 January 2015), 7.50% (4 March), 7.25% (2 June) and 6.75% (29 September 2015). The rate was left unchanged on 2 February 2016.
FLAG (wording): the Box says "October" for the 50 bps move; the V2 table dates it 29 September 2015. Use the table date.

**ES15-14 — The monetary-policy framework agreement of February 2015.** V2 para 3.2, p. 49 (st. 211); Box 3.4, p. 62 (st. 224).
The Government and the RBI signed an agreement on a monetary policy framework in February 2015. CPI inflation came in below the 6% target set for January 2016, ahead of time. Box 3.4 says the RBI Act is to be amended to give the framework statutory backing and to create a Monetary Policy Committee. The same Box records that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2015, was introduced in Parliament on 21 December 2015.
FLAG: ES 2018-19 (ES18-2) later says the MPC itself was constituted in February 2015. On ES 2015-16's own account, only the agreement dates from then; the MPC was still to be legislated.

**ES15-15 — Bank asset quality, September 2015.** V2 para 3.16, p. 57 (st. 219).
Gross NPAs of scheduled commercial banks rose from 4.6% of advances (March 2015) to 5.1% (September 2015). Stressed advances (NPAs plus restructured loans) rose from 11.1% to 11.3%; for public-sector banks they were 14.0%.

**ES15-16 — China's August 2015 devaluation and the rupee.** V2 para 4.41, p. 83 (st. 245); para 3.26, p. 59 (st. 221).
The rupee traded in a range of ₹62.16-64.17 per dollar until early August 2015. It weakened past ₹66 by end-August, after the renminbi devaluation of 11 August and the Chinese stock-market fall of 24 August. It touched ₹68.44 on 12 February 2016. The Sensex fell 8.5% (to 5 January 2016); para 3.26 names the August 2015 devaluation and the 4 January 2016 sell-off.

**ES15-17 — Balance of payments, 2014-15 and H1 2015-16.** V2 paras 1.59-1.60, p. 20 (st. 182).
The current-account deficit was US$26.8bn (1.3% of GDP) in 2014-15 and US$14.4bn (1.4%) in H1 2015-16. Net portfolio flows turned to an outflow of US$8.7bn in H1, against an inflow of US$22.2bn a year earlier. Net capital flows of US$88.2bn in 2014-15 were the second highest on record.

**ES15-18 — World trade slows faster than world output.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.83-1.84, p. 30 (st. 30); V2 paras 1.51-1.54, pp. 18-19 (st. 180-181).
Exports fell about 18% in the first three quarters of 2015-16, holding growth back by 1-1.2 points (V1). Vol 2 gives exports down 17.6% in April-January, to US$217.7bn, and imports down 15.5%, to US$324.5bn. The IMF's January 2016 update has world growth at 3.1% (2015), 3.4% (2016) and 3.6% (2017), and world trade volume at 2.6% (2015) and 3.4% (2016).
Quote (V1 para 1.84): "trade has sputtered".

**ES15-19 — Nairobi, mega-regionals and Chinese overcapacity.** V1 paras 1.89, 1.106-1.107, pp. 32-35 (st. 32-35); V2 para 1.57, p. 19 (st. 181).
The WTO's Nairobi ministerial (MC10, 15-19 December 2015) is described as a non-success, divided over the Doha agenda. The mega-regional agreements, the TPP above all, leave India out. A weakening Chinese currency threatens India's competitiveness. Chinese excess capacity in steel and aluminium is producing an import surge.

**ES15-20 — Fiscal path and a call to review the FRBM framework.** V1 paras 1.64, 1.76 and 1.80, pp. 26-30 (st. 26-30).
Consolidated general-government debt is about 67% of GDP. The Centre's primary deficit is below 1% of GDP in 2015-16, against nearly 3% in 2011-12. Para 1.80 says it is time to review the medium-term fiscal framework, listing risks: debt dynamics thrown off course, a faltering global recovery, and inflation turning out lower than expected.

**ES15-21 — Petroleum subsidy figures disagree.** V2 para 2.17, p. 41 (st. 203).
The 2015-16 Budget Estimate for petroleum subsidy is Rs 30,000 crore, against Rs 85,378 crore in 2013-14.
FLAG: for 2014-15 (provisional) the text gives Rs 57,769 crore but Table 2.5 gives Rs 60,270 crore.

**ES15-22 — Key indicators table, 2012-13 to 2015-16.** V2 Table 0.1, p. 2 (st. 164). Figures carried into the cross-Survey table below (GDP, CPI, WPI, CAB, reserves, rupee, fiscal deficit).

## Economic Survey 2016-17 (Vol 1, January 2017; Vol 2, August 2017)

**ES16-1 — Brexit and the US election as a turn against globalisation.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.2 and 1.21, pp. 2 and 6 (st. 18 and 22); paras 1.92-1.94, p. 25 (st. 41).
The Survey treats Brexit and the US election as a tectonic shift. It warns of a move towards isolationism and nativism and the end of the post-war consensus for globalisation. After 8 November the dollar rose 5.3%. The Survey sees a vacuum in trade leadership and suggests India seek FTAs with the UK and Europe. Para 1.22 says exports must grow 15-20% a year for growth of 8-10%.

**ES16-2 — Box 2: the West's tolerance of openness.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.25-1.27 and Box 2, pp. 6-9 (st. 22-25).
The Box asks whether the rich world's political tolerance for globalisation has fallen, and what that means for India. It notes that world trade has grown more slowly than world GDP. China, with its currency and its debt, is the country to watch.
Quote (Box title): "Political Carrying Capacity of the West for Openness and Impact on India".

**ES16-3 — Demonetisation, 8 November 2016.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.3, p. 2 (st. 18); Ch 3, para 3.1, p. 53 (st. 69).
Rs 500 and Rs 1000 notes, 86% of the cash in circulation, ceased to be legal tender on 8 November 2016. Old notes could be deposited until 30 December.

**ES16-4 — The cost of demonetisation.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.42-1.43 and 1.56-1.57, pp. 13 and 17 (st. 29 and 33); Box 3, p. 19 (st. 35).
In Q3 2016-17 cash fell 9.4% while demand deposits rose 43%. The MCLR fell about 90 bps. Against a baseline of about 7% real (11¼% nominal) growth, demonetisation would cut real growth by ¼-½ point and nominal growth by ¼-1 point in 2016-17. Box 3 notes that the CSO Advance Estimate of 7.1% largely predates the shock.
FLAG (forecast vs outcome): the May 2017 CSO estimate was also 7.1% (V2 para 1.166, ES16-12); Q4 GVA was 5.6% (V2 para 1.128).

**ES16-5 — The economy in H1 2016-17.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.29-1.34 and 1.36, pp. 9-11 (st. 25-27).
- Growth was 7.2% in H1 2016-17.
- CPI inflation averaged 4.9% in April-December and was 3.4% at end-December, below the RBI's 5% goal.
- WPI inflation went from a trough of −5.1% (August 2015) to 3.4% (December 2016) as oil recovered.
- The CAD was about 0.3% of GDP in H1.
- Reserves rose from about US$350bn (end-January 2016) to US$360bn (end-December 2016).
- FDI rose from 1.7% to 3.2% of GDP.
- Remittances fell US$4.5bn in H1, a lagged effect of low oil prices on the Gulf (para 1.36).

**ES16-6 — Oil: the windfall ends.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.23, 1.64, 1.68 and 1.71, pp. 6 and 20-21 (st. 22 and 36-37).
The fall in oil prices since their June 2014 peak had lifted incomes (para 1.23). Oil is now expected to be 10-15% higher in 2017, perhaps US$60-65/bbl. The windfall had raised the tax-to-GDP ratio by about 0.5 point in each of the last two years (para 1.71), and that support is now ending.

**ES16-7 — "The China scare of 2015" and the yuan.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.83, pp. 23-24 (st. 39-40).
The world export-to-GDP ratio has fallen since 2011. The yuan fell 11.6% between July 2015 and December 2016; the rupee gained about 6% against it.
Quote: "the China scare of 2015".

**ES16-8 — The twin balance sheet problem festers.** V1 Ch 4, paras 4.1-4.5, pp. 82-83 (st. 98-99); paras 4.46-4.47, p. 95 (st. 111).
After the RBI's Asset Quality Review (PSB results of February 2016), NPAs reached about 9% of advances in September 2016, about 12% at public-sector banks. About 40% of corporate debt is owed by firms with interest coverage below 1. India's NPA ratio is higher than that of any major emerging market except Russia. The chapter proposes a central asset-reconstruction agency (PARA, Box 1). It reviews SDR (June 2015), S4A and the Indradhanush capital plan of Rs 70,000 crore.
Quote (chapter title): "The Festering Twin Balance Sheet Problem".

**ES16-9 — Fiscal path; FRBM review.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.37-1.38 and 1.76, pp. 12 and 22 (st. 28 and 38); Ch 5, p. 105 (st. 121).
The Centre's deficit target for 2016-17 is 3.5% of GDP, after 4.5% (2013-14), 4.1% and 3.9%. States' deficits rose from 2.5% to 3.6% of GDP. Ch 5 refers to the FRBM Review Committee and to a global paradigm shift in fiscal and monetary policy since 2008, including negative interest rates. The Committee's recommendations are not summarised in the pages read (see Not found).

**ES16-10 — Outlook for 2017-18: 6¾-7½%.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.64-1.69, pp. 20-21 (st. 36-37).
Growth in 2017-18 is projected at 6¾-7½%. The risks are higher oil, trade tensions and the GST rollout.

**ES16-11 — GST and the shift to low inflation (Vol 2).** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.6-1.13, pp. 2-4 (st. 353-355); paras 1.28-1.38, pp. 6-9 (st. 357-360).
GST, launched on 1 July 2017, is called historic; it has six rates. Para 1.13 revises the likely number of taxpayers from 87 lakh to about 70 lakh. Paras 1.28-1.38 argue that India has moved to a low-inflation regime: about 9% for 23 years, about 4% in 2000-05, about 9% in 2006-14 and low since. Shale production caps the oil price at around US$50.
Quote (para 1.35): "has capped the upside risks to oil prices".

**ES16-12 — Growth slows: 7.1% in 2016-17.** V2 paras 1.128 and 1.166, pp. 32 and 39-40 (st. 383 and 390-391); para 1.80, p. 19 (st. 370).
The CSO (May 2017) puts 2016-17 growth at 7.1%, against 8.0% in 2015-16. Real GVA was 5.6% in Q4 2016-17. Cash remained about Rs 3.5 lakh crore (20%) below trend; the currency-to-GDP ratio fell from 11.3% to 9.7%.
FLAG (vintage): ES 2015-16 gave 2015-16 as 7.6% (AE); by August 2017 it is 8.0%.

**ES16-13 — Inflation targeting codified: 4% ±2.** V2 Ch 3, paras 3.1-3.2, p. 85 (st. 436); Table 1, p. 86 (st. 437); V1 p. 147 (st. 163).
The RBI Act was amended in May 2016. The inflation target is CPI 4% with a tolerance of ±2%, for 5 August 2016 to 31 March 2021. The MPC was notified on 29 September 2016. Its stance became neutral on 8 February 2017. The repo rate went from 6.75% to 6.50% (5 April 2016), 6.25% (4 October 2016) and 6.00% (2 August 2017). CPI inflation fell to 1.5% in June 2017, far below the 4% target (V2 paras 1.133-1.138).
Quote (V1 p. 147): "inflation target of 4 per cent with tolerance".

**ES16-14 — NPAs, March 2017.** V2 para 3.10, p. 90 (st. 441).
Gross NPAs of scheduled commercial banks rose from 9.2% (September 2016) to 9.5% (March 2017).
FLAG: ES 2017-18 gives 9.6% for March 2017 (ES17-13).

**ES16-15 — Brexit in the markets.** V2 p. 97 (st. 448); p. 146 (st. 497).
G-sec yields hardened before the Brexit referendum (23 June 2016) and softened after it. Brexit, the US election, demonetisation and the Fed's rate rise produced volatility. Banking-capital outflows of US$16.6bn reflected the redemption of FCNR(B) deposits.

**ES16-16 — World trade and anti-globalisation (Vol 2 Ch 6).** V2 Ch 6, summary and paras 6.1-6.2, p. 137 (st. 488); Box 1, p. 138 (st. 489); Table 2, p. 141 (st. 492).
The CAD fell to 0.7% of GDP in 2016-17 from 1.1%. World trade fell 13.2% in value in 2015 and 3.2% in 2016; volume growth was 2.3% in 2016 (2.6% in 2015). Box 1 links anti-globalisation to the US election and Brexit, and counts 42 new trade-restrictive measures by G20 members from mid-October 2016 to mid-May 2017. POL imports were US$86.9bn in 2016-17, after falls of 40.0% (2015-16) and a rise of 4.8% (2016-17).
Quote (Box 1 title): "Rising Anti-globalization and Trade Restrictive Measures".

## Economic Survey 2017-18

**ES17-1 — GST, IBC and recapitalisation.** V1 Ch 1, opening summary, p. 1 (st. 19); para 1.2, pp. 1-2 (st. 19-20).
GST was launched on 1 July 2017. The TBS problem was tackled by sending large stressed accounts to the new bankruptcy code and by a bank recapitalisation package of about 1.2% of GDP. The Survey forecasts 6¾% for 2017-18 and 7-7½% for 2018-19.
Quote: "launched at the stroke of midnight on July 1, 2017".

**ES17-2 — India "decoupled" in H1 2017.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.3-1.5, p. 2 (st. 20).
In H1 2017 India slowed while the world accelerated. The causes listed are demonetisation, GST, high real interest rates, the TBS and low food prices. India rose 30 places in the Ease of Doing Business ranking; FDI rose about 20%. India received its first sovereign ratings upgrade in 14 years. Fiscal deficit, current account and inflation were higher than expected. Oil is described as India's historic vulnerability.

**ES17-3 — Real appreciation and tight money.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.19, p. 6 (st. 24); paras 1.46-1.47, pp. 14-16 (st. 32-34).
The REER rose about 21% since January 2014. Real policy rates rose about 2.5 points in July-December 2016, while US real rates fell about 1 point. The rupee gained about 9% in real terms from early 2016 to November 2017.

**ES17-4 — The world in 2017: synchronised recovery.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.34-1.35 and 1.38, pp. 11-12 (st. 29-30).
World growth was 3.6% in 2017 (3.2% in 2016). World trade volume grew 4.7% in 2017, against 2.5% in 2016. Oil rose 16% to about US$61/bbl by end-2017. The listed risks are China's credit surge and trade tensions.
FLAG: ES 2018-19 gives world trade in 2017 as 4.6% (WTO) and ES 2019-20 as 5.7% (ES18-14, ES19-16).

**ES17-5 — World trade: a decade of slowing.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.58, p. 20 (st. 38).
World trade growth is still less than half what it was a decade earlier.
Quote: "remains less than half its level of a decade ago".

**ES17-6 — Oil price and its macro cost.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.52 and Table 1, p. 18 (st. 36).
The Indian crude basket changed −10.1% in 2014-15, −46.2% in 2015-16, −11.4% in 2016-17 and +15.9% in the first nine months of 2017-18. A US$10/bbl rise is estimated to cut growth by 0.2-0.3 point, raise WPI inflation by about 1.7 points and widen the CAD by about US$9-10bn.

**ES17-7 — Demonetisation one year on.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.56, p. 19 (st. 37).
The economy now holds about Rs 2.8 lakh crore less cash (1.8% of GDP) and about Rs 3.8 lakh crore less high-denomination notes (2.5% of GDP) than it otherwise would.

**ES17-8 — Growth in 2017-18: 6.5% AE.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.60, p. 20 (st. 38); V2 Ch 1 opening, p. 1 (st. 149).
The CSO's AE is 6.5%; the Survey expects about 6¾%. Growth was 6.0% in H1 and is expected at 7.5% in H2. The 2014-15 to 2017-18 average is 7.3%.
FLAG (vintage): by ES 2019-20, 2017-18 is 7.2% (1st RE; ES19-6).

**ES17-9 — Inflation undershoot ends.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.62, p. 21 (st. 39).
CPI inflation was below the 4% target for 13 months, by 130 bps on average. It crossed 4% in November and was 5.2% in December 2017.

**ES17-10 — External accounts.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.63-1.64, p. 21 (st. 39); V2 Ch 1 opening, p. 1 (st. 149).
The CAD is expected at 1.5-2% of GDP in 2017-18. Reserves were about US$432bn including forwards (end-December 2017); spot reserves about US$414bn on 12 January 2018. Vol 2 flags protectionist tendencies abroad as a risk to exports. The Indian basket was up 14% so far in 2017-18 (average US$56-57), and a further 10-15% rise is expected in 2018-19.

**ES17-11 — Fiscal slippage and bank capital.** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.66 and 1.69, pp. 23-24 (st. 41-42); V2 para 8.10, p. 123 (st. 271).
The fiscal deficit reached 112% of the Budget Estimate in eight months. Rs 80,000 crore (0.5% of GDP) of bank recapitalisation is scheduled for 2017-18. Vol 2 records the recapitalisation package of Rs 2.11 lakh crore.

**ES17-12 — Risks for 2018-19: oil and a "sudden stall".** V1 Ch 1, paras 1.75-1.80, pp. 27-28 (st. 45-46).
The IMF projects a further 12% rise in oil in 2018-19. A sharp correction in high stock prices could cause a sudden stall in capital flows.
Quote: "sudden stall".

**ES17-13 — Inflation-targeting framework and NPAs.** V2 Ch 3, para 3.1, p. 44 (st. 192); p. 48 (st. 196).
The framework is dated as effective from 5 August 2016; the repo was cut to 6.0% in August 2017. Gross NPAs rose from 9.6% (March 2017) to 10.2% (September 2017).
FLAG: ES 2016-17 gives 9.5% for March 2017 (ES16-14).

**ES17-14 — IBC in action.** V2 Box 3.1, p. 50 (st. 198).
The IBC was passed in May 2016. More than 525 insolvency cases had been admitted, with 10 resolution plans approved and 30 liquidation orders.

**ES17-15 — GDP history revised up.** V2 Table 0.1, p. 2 (st. 150); para 1.3, p. 3 (st. 151).
Real GDP growth was 7.5% (2014-15), 8.0% (2015-16), 7.1% (2016-17, PE) and 6.5% (2017-18, AE). Quarterly growth peaked at 9.1% in Q4 2015-16 and fell to 5.7% in Q1 2017-18, recovering to 6.3% in Q2. The low point of the series is put at 5.5% in 2012-13.
FLAG (vintage): ES 2015-16 had 5.6% for 2012-13, 7.2% for 2014-15 and 7.6% for 2015-16.

## Economic Survey 2018-19 (July 2019)

**ES18-1 — The US$5 trillion goal.** V1 Ch 1, opening, p. 1 (st. 13).
Becoming a US$5 trillion economy by 2024-25 requires sustained real GDP growth of 8%.
Quote: "India needs to sustain a real GDP growth rate of 8%".

**ES18-2 — The MPC dated to February 2015.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.3, p. 2 (st. 14).
The Survey says the MPC was constituted in February 2015 with a mandate to target headline inflation of 4%, and that it first met in April 2015.
FLAG (major): this conflicts with ES 2015-16 (only an agreement in February 2015; MPC still to be legislated; ES15-14) and ES 2016-17 (MPC notified 29 September 2016; ES16-13). Do not use this date.
Quote: "constituted in February, 2015".

**ES18-3 — Fiscal framework: the FRBM Act revived since 2016.** V1 Ch 1, para 1.4, p. 3 (st. 15); V2 Ch 2, paras 2.2-2.3, pp. 34-35 (st. 274-275).
The FRBM Act of 2003 is said to have been given new life since 2016 and to set the glide path for the fiscal deficit. The fiscal deficit fell from 4.5% of GDP (2013-14) to 3.4% (2018-19). The Budget of 2018-19 adopted a debt-and-deficit framework: 3% fiscal deficit by 2020-21 and central-government debt of 40% of GDP by 2024-25. The 2017-18 target was revised from 3.2% to 3.5%, and 2018-19 budgeted at 3.3%; the outcome was 3.4%, with central debt at 44.5%.

**ES18-4 — 2018-19: growth 6.8%, world slowing.** V2 Ch 1, opening, p. 1 (st. 241); paras 1.2-1.7, pp. 3-5 (st. 243-245).
World growth: 3.8% (2017), 3.6% (2018), 3.3% (2019). India: 6.8% in 2018-19, against 7.2% in 2017-18. Q4 2018-19 growth fell to 5.8%. The 2018 global slowdown followed the US-China trade tensions. NBFC stress hit consumer finance, and vehicle sales slowed. FDI rose 14.2%. The forecast for 2019-20 is 7%.
FLAG (vintage): ES 2017-18 had 2017-18 at 6.5% (AE); here it is 7.2%. FLAG (forecast vs outcome): the 7% forecast for 2019-20 compares with 5.0% (AE, ES 2019-20) and 4.2% (ES 2020-21; ES20-1).

**ES18-5 — Oil above US$80 and a wider CAD.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.9 and 1.12, pp. 5-7 (st. 245-247); st. 373 (Ch 6 trade).
CPI inflation was 3.4% in 2018-19 (3.6% in 2017-18). Crude rose above US$80/bbl in October 2018 and averaged about US$14/bbl more than the year before. The CAD widened from 1.9% to 2.6% of GDP (April-December 2018). The trade deficit rose from US$162.1bn to US$184bn. POL imports rose 29.7% in 2018-19; crude was 22.2% of imports.
FLAG: ES 2019-20 gives 2.1% for full-year 2018-19 (ES19-16).

**ES18-6 — The 2018 rupee fall.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.15-1.16, pp. 7-8 (st. 247-248); Ch 6, para 6.44, p. 150 (st. 390). (OCR text.)
The rupee fell 7.8% against the dollar over 2018-19 and hit ₹74.4 in October 2018, before recovering to about ₹69.2 at end-March 2019 (OCR reads "769.2"). The causes listed are high oil and US rate rises. The rupee did better than the peso, lira, real and ruble, each of which fell more than 10%. The NEER fell 5.6% and the REER 4.8%. Reserves fell US$11.6bn over the year, declining until October 2018, and were US$422.2bn on 14 June 2019. Para 6.44 adds the US-China trade war, sanctions on Iran and FPI outflows.
Quote (OCR, clean span): "Rupee depreciated by 7.8 per cent".

**ES18-7 — Investment and saving.** V2 Ch 1, para 1.27, p. 14 (st. 254); para 1.30, p. 15 (st. 255).
Fixed investment grew 8.3%, 9.3% and 10.0% in the last three years. Household saving fell from 23.6% (of GDP, 2011-12) to 17.2% (2017-18), almost all of it in physical saving; the household sector accounts for the whole fall in the saving rate.

**ES18-8 — Policy uncertainty and protectionism.** V1 Ch 6, p. 120 (st. 132); V2 para 1.35, p. 17 (st. 257); paras 1.53-1.55, p. 22 (st. 262).
Since mid-2018 India's economic policy uncertainty index has diverged from the rising global index, which the Survey links to US-China trade tensions and the uncertain outcome of Brexit. Export prospects depend on rising trade protectionism. Risks for 2019-20 include US-China tensions disrupting value chains, Brexit, China's slowdown and NBFC stress spilling into credit.

**ES18-9 — Monetary policy U-turn.** V2 Ch 3, summary and paras 3.1-3.4, p. 64 (st. 304); Table 1, p. 65 (st. 305).
The repo rate was raised 50 bps and then cut 75 bps. From 6.00% in April 2018, it rose to 6.25% (6 June 2018) and 6.50% (1 August 2018), citing oil and expected Fed tightening. The stance became "calibrated tightening" in October 2018. In February 2019 (7 February) the MPC returned to neutral and cut to 6.25%, citing the Fed's pause and hopes for US-China trade talks. Cuts in April (4 April 2019) and June (6 June 2019) took the repo to 5.75%; the stance became accommodative in June 2019. Liquidity had been tight since September 2018.
Quote: "Monetary policy witnessed a u-turn over the last year."

**ES18-10 — NPAs decline.** V2 Ch 3, para 3.19, p. 70 (st. 310).
Gross NPAs fell from 11.5% (March 2018) to 10.1% (December 2018); for PSBs from 15.5% to 13.9%.

**ES18-11 — IL&FS and the NBFC squeeze.** V2 Ch 3, paras 3.23-3.24, p. 72 (st. 312).
NBFCs ran into difficulties after the IL&FS downgrades and default. Public funds make up about 70% of NBFC liabilities. Mutual-fund deployment in NBFC paper fell 12% (April 2019). NBFC gross NPAs rose from 6.1% to 6.5%. The OCR text does not date the default; ES 2019-20 gives June-September 2018 (ES19-9).

**ES18-12 — IBC results.** V2 Ch 3, pp. 79-83 (st. 319-323); para 3.60, p. 82 (st. 322).
The RBI referred 12 large accounts (about 25% of gross NPAs) and then 28 more. By April 2019, Rs 1,83,519 crore had been recovered. The recovery rate was about 43%, against about 23% under earlier mechanisms.

**ES18-13 — TBS resolution.** V1 Ch 6, para 6.13, p. 122 (st. 134).
The Survey credits IBC and bank recapitalisation with resolving the twin balance sheet problem.

**ES18-14 — World trade slows in 2018.** V2 Ch 6, paras 6.1-6.2, p. 129 (st. 369); "at a glance", p. 158 (st. 398).
The WEO has world growth at 3.3% in 2019. The WTO puts world trade growth at 3% in 2018, against 4.6% in 2017, citing new and retaliatory tariffs. US-China trade tensions are named as the cause of the 2018 slowdown.

## Economic Survey 2019-20 (January 2020)

**ES19-1 — 2019: slowest world growth since 2009.** V2 Ch 1, opening, p. 1 (st. 317).
World growth was 2.9% in 2019, after 3.6% (2018) and 3.8% (2017). The Survey cites protectionist tendencies of China and the USA. India's growth fell to 4.8% in H1 2019-20, from 6.2% in H2 2018-19. The CAD fell from 2.1% to 1.5% of GDP. The repo rate was cut by 110 bps in 2019-20 (four consecutive MPC meetings; V2 Ch 4 summary). The AE for 2019-20 is 5%. CPI inflation was 7.4% in December 2019.
Quote: "slowest pace of 2.9 per cent since the global financial crisis of 2009".

**ES19-2 — H1 2019-20.** V2 Ch 1, opening, p. 1 (st. 317).
A sharp fall in real fixed investment, driven by slow consumption, explains the slowdown.
Quote: "GDP growth moderating to 4.8 per cent in H1 of 2019-20".

**ES19-3 — Trade tensions as the global backdrop.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.1-1.2 and 1.5, pp. 2-4 (st. 318-320).
China-USA trade tensions, rising trade barriers and weak manufacturing drag on world trade and demand. Para 1.2 repeats the finding that India's growth tracks world output.

**ES19-4 — Quarterly slowdown.** V2 Ch 1, Table 2, pp. 4-5 (st. 320-321).
Quarterly growth: 8.0%, 7.0%, 6.6%, 5.8% (Q1-Q4 2018-19), then 5.0% and 4.5% (Q1-Q2 2019-20).

**ES19-5 — Growth 5.0% AE.** V2 Ch 1, para 1.37, p. 16 (st. 332).
The AE puts 2019-20 at 5.0%, against 6.8% in 2018-19.

**ES19-6 — 2017-18 revised to 7.2%.** V2 Ch 1, Table 7, p. 16 (st. 332).
2017-18 is 7.2% (first revised estimate).
FLAG (vintage): 6.5% in ES 2017-18 (AE).

**ES19-7 — The financial sector as the drag.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.41-1.50, pp. 20-23 (st. 336-339).
The Survey links the investment slowdown to the earlier credit boom, and its firm-level analysis finds 2013 the pivotal year. It blames RBI's regulatory forbearance (from 27 August 2008) for deferring NPA recognition. Gross NPAs averaged 3.0% in 2009-14 and 8.3% in 2014-19. Bank credit growth fell from 16.7% a year (2009-14) to 10.5% (2014-19).

**ES19-8 — Housing and the outlook.** V2 Ch 1, paras 1.53 and 1.56-1.57, pp. 25-28 (st. 341-344).
Unsold housing stood at 9.43 lakh units (para 1.53). The relocation of investors from other countries amid trade tensions is presented as an opportunity for FDI. The National Infrastructure Pipeline is Rs 102 lakh crore.

**ES19-9 — IL&FS and DHFL.** V1 Ch 8, summary and paras 8.1-8.5, pp. 177-179 (st. 189-191).
Two IL&FS subsidiaries defaulted between June and September 2018; DHFL defaulted in June-August 2019. Panic followed in debt mutual funds: DSP's sale of commercial paper worth Rs 300 crore at a steep discount in September 2018 (DHFL paper, per the footnote) set it off, and September 2018 and June 2019 saw the largest net outflows from liquid and money-market funds. On 4 June 2019 the NAV of debt funds holding stressed NBFC paper fell 53% in one day. Equity prices of stressed NBFCs had fallen from May 2018 and dipped sharply in September 2018. Debt funds with such exposure lost about Rs 4,000 crore after recoveries.
FLAG (name): the chapter summary calls IL&FS "Infrastructure Leasing and Financing Services"; para 8.1 has "Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services". Use the latter.

**ES19-10 — GDP not overstated.** V1 Ch 10, pp. 231-232 (st. 243-244); Box 1.
The chapter rebuts claims (Subramanian 2019) that the 2011-12-base series overstates growth. Box 1 dates the series to 30 January 2015.
Quote (chapter title): "Is India's GDP Growth Rate Overstated? No!".

**ES19-11 — NPAs and IBC in 2019.** V2 Ch 4, summary, p. 110 (st. 426).
Gross NPAs were 9.3% from March to September 2019. The IBC recovery rate (realisation as a share of amounts involved) was 49.6% in 2017-18 and 42.5% in 2018-19. Proceedings take about 340 days on average, including litigation, against about 4.3 years under the previous regime.

**ES19-12 — Inflation framework: a third date.** V2 Ch 4, para 4.1, p. 110 (st. 426).
The framework is said to be effective from 27 June 2016.
FLAG: ES 2016-17 and ES 2017-18 give 5 August 2016 (ES16-13, ES17-13).

**ES19-13 — Repo cuts in 2019.** V2 Ch 4, Table 1, p. 111 (st. 427).
Repo: 6.00% (4 April 2019), 5.75% (6 June), 5.40% (7 August), 5.15% (4 October 2019); unchanged on 5 December.
FLAG: ES 2018-19 Table 1 (OCR) shows 4 April 2019 at 6.00% (consistent).

**ES19-14 — Reserves and the rupee.** V2 Ch 3, pp. 71-73 (st. 387-389).
Reserves: US$304.2bn (end 2013-14), US$412.9bn (March 2019), US$461.2bn (10 January 2020). Rupee: ₹60.50 to ₹69.92 per dollar.

**ES19-15 — CAD history.** V2 Ch 3, Table 1, pp. 71-73 (st. 387-389).
The CAD averaged 3.3% of GDP in 2009-14 and 1.4% in 2014-19; it was 2.1% in 2018-19 and 1.5% in H1 2019-20.

**ES19-16 — World trade and oil.** V2 Ch 3, paras 3.6-3.7, pp. 71-73 (st. 387-389); Table 9, p. 79 (st. 395).
World trade grew 1.0% in 2019, down from a 5.7% peak in 2017. The improvement in the trade balance over 2014-19 is put down mostly to a fall of more than fifty per cent in crude prices "in 2016-17" (sic). Table 9: the Indian basket averaged US$96.0/bbl in 2009-14, 60.8 in 2014-19, 69.9 in 2018-19 and 64.7 in H1 2019-20; POL's share of imports was 32.1%, 25.2%, 27.4% and 26.3%.
FLAG: ES 2017-18 puts the big fall in 2015-16 (−46.2%); 2017 world trade 5.7% here vs 4.7% (ES 2017-18) and 4.6% (ES 2018-19).

## Problems and caveats

1. **Layout and OCR.** All ten volumes are two-column, mixed line by line in the extracts. ES 2018-19 Vol 2 is OCR: ₹ comes out as "·", "<", "7" or "$" (₹69.2 reads "769.2"), and table cells are garbled; its numbers need checking against the PDF. ES 2016-17 Vol 2 renders ₹ as "$" in places.
2. **GDP vintages.** Every annual growth figure drifts between Surveys: 2014-15 7.2 → 7.5; 2015-16 7.6 → 8.0; 2017-18 6.5 → 7.2; 2019-20 5.0 → 4.2; 2012-13 5.6 → 5.5. ES 2019-20 Ch 10 defends the series against critics. Always cite the vintage.
3. **MPC date inconsistencies.** February 2015 (ES 2018-19, wrong on its predecessors' account); 29 September 2016 (MPC notified, ES 2016-17); framework effective 5 August 2016 (ES 2016-17, 2017-18) vs 27 June 2016 (ES 2019-20).
4. **Other inconsistencies.** GNPA March 2017: 9.5 vs 9.6. CAD 2018-19: 2.6 (Apr-Dec) vs 2.1 (full year). World trade 2017: 4.7 / 4.6 / 5.7. Oil-price falls given over different periods (ES15-8, ES19-16). Petroleum subsidy 2014-15: 57,769 vs 60,270. IL&FS name (ES19-9). The 50 bps cut of 2015: "October" (V1 Box) vs 29 September (V2 table).
5. **Forecast vs outcome.** 2016-17: 7-7¾ forecast → 7.1. 2017-18: 6¾-7½ (ES16) → 6.5 AE / 7.2 revised. 2018-19: 7-7½ (ES17) → 6.8. 2019-20: 7 (ES18) → 5.0 AE → 4.2.
