# Reading notes: IMF WEO 2003–2006 and GFSR 2006 (read 22 Nov 2026)

Method: imf.org refuses scripted downloads (HTTP 403). Each PDF was fetched inside an imf.org page in the
built-in browser and its text streams decoded there (no file saved). Page numbers come from the printed
running heads. Quotes are kept under 15 words; everything else is paraphrase.
Index pages: https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/weo/YYYY/0N/index.htm (WEO), .../gfsr/YYYY/0N/index.htm (GFSR).
PDF base: https://www.imf.org/-/media/websites/imf/imported-flagship-issues/external/pubs/ft/

## WEO April 2003 — *Growth and Institutions*  (Foreword: Kenneth Rogoff)
Files: weo/2003/01/pdf/_frontpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Ch. 1 p. 2, Table 1.1: world output 2001 2.3, 2002 3.0; projections 2003 3.2, 2004 4.1 (2003 cut 0.5 pp from Sep 2002). US 2002 2.4; China 2002 8.0; India 2002 4.9, proj. 2003 5.1, 2004 5.9.
- Foreword pp. x–xii: theme is structural reform and institutions. Ch. 2 essays: corporate leverage still weighs on investment; aftermath of the 1990s asset bubble.
- Foreword p. xii: house prices in some industrial countries have reached a "boom"; 40 percent of housing booms end in busts, with price falls averaging 25–30 percent and lost output averaging 8 percent of GDP over two to three years.
- Ch. 1 p. 19: public debt accumulation, including off-balance-sheet liabilities, a rising concern in parts of emerging Asia, "including India and China".

## WEO September 2003 — *Public Debt in Emerging Markets*  (Foreword: Rogoff)
Files: weo/2003/02/pdf/_frontpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Ch. 1 p. 1: April 2003 had expected recovery in H2 provided the Iraq war was short; hostilities ended quickly; SARS hit Asia in Q2; signs of a pickup, notably in the US, Japan and emerging Asia.
- Ch. 1 p. 2, Table 1.1: world 2002 3.0, proj. 2003 3.2, 2004 4.1; India 2002 4.7, proj. 2003 5.6, 2004 5.9.
- Foreword pp. x–xi: emerging-market public debt now matches or exceeds many industrial countries' as a share of GDP; the benign financing window should be used to reduce debt.
- Foreword p. x: Asian reserve accumulation to end-2001 explainable by fundamentals; the run-up over the past 18 months "much harder to rationalize". Industrial-country interest rates touched 40- and 50-year lows in 2003. Many Asian countries should consider more exchange-rate flexibility; burden of closing the US current account deficit should be shared.
- Ch. 1 p. 17: per capita growth robust in China and, to a lesser extent, India; below 1 percent in sub-Saharan Africa.

## WEO April 2004 — *Advancing Structural Reforms*  (Foreword: Raghuram Rajan, his first)
Files: weo/2004/01/pdf/_frontpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Foreword p. xi: outlook "among the rosiest we have seen for a decade". Expansionary US and East Asian policy carried the world past 9/11 and SARS, but US "policy bullets are now largely spent"; urges faster US fiscal consolidation.
- Foreword p. xi–xii: Ch. IV on credit booms: those that become booms invariably end in financial crises with a large fall in output; resilience requires sound banks and right incentives for bankers and supervisors.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: global growth 2004 and 2005 projected at about 4½ percent; risks include orderly resolution of global imbalances and the transition to higher interest rates.
- Ch. 1 p. 3, Table 1.1: world 2003 3.9, proj. 2004 4.6, 2005 4.4; US 2003 3.1; China 2003 9.1; India 2003 7.4, proj. 2004 6.8, 2005 6.0.
- Ch. 1 p. 7: India's growth exceeded expectations, "aided by favorable rainfall and low domestic interest rates".

## WEO September 2004 — *The Global Demographic Transition*  (Foreword: Rajan)
Files: weo/2004/02/pdf/_frontmpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Ch. 1 p. 1: mid-2003 to mid-2004 global growth averaged 5 percent, well above the 4 percent historical trend; a "soft patch" after Q1 2004.
- Ch. 1 p. 3, Table 1.1: world 2003 3.9, proj. 2004 5.0, 2005 4.3; India 2003 7.2, proj. 2004 6.4, 2005 6.7.
- Foreword p. xi: oil prices shot up on demand, little spare capacity and geopolitical threats. Long-run trends become short-run concerns: growth of China "and, to a lesser extent, India" would weigh on energy; "that future is now upon us" (quote: 6 words).
- Foreword p. xi: about 40 percent of house-price movements across industrial countries explained by global factors; booms likely to unwind together.
- Ch. 1 p. 7: emerging Asia growth 7¼ percent in 2004; India supported by global expansion and supportive monetary conditions despite adverse weather.
- Ch. 1 p. 12: China's rise will reorganise global production, more so "if it is joined by India".

## WEO April 2005 — *Globalization and External Imbalances*  (Foreword: Rajan)
Files: weo/2005/01/pdf/_frontmpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Ch. 1 p. 1: growth averaged about 6 percent in late 2003 and early 2004, then moderated; Box 1.1 on the December 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (growth impact modest).
- Ch. 1 p. 3, Table 1.1: world 2003 4.0, 2004 5.1, proj. 2005 4.3, 2006 4.4; China 2004 9.5; India 2004 7.3, proj. 2005 6.7, 2006 6.4.
- Foreword p. xi: imbalances rooted in two investment booms and busts (East Asia before 1997–98; US IT boom); after the IT bust the US deficit widened because of fiscal expansion and very accommodative Fed policy that kept house prices rising.
- Ch. 1 p. 6: China's investment "unsustainably high"; growth in India "quite robust". India tsunami deaths 16,500 (Box 1.1).

## WEO September 2005 — *Building Institutions*  (Foreword: Rajan)
Files: weo/2005/02/pdf/_frontmpdf.pdf, _chapter1pdf.pdf
- Foreword p. xi: world economy "tremendously resilient"; Katrina, disease and oil caused only blips.
- Foreword p. xi: the pattern of rich countries consuming while emerging markets supply and finance them is "a temporary and effective response to crises, which now needs to be reversed"; two transitions needed (consumption to investment; demand from deficit to surplus countries).
- Foreword p. xi–xii: easy finance means traditional signals (inflation, rates, exchange rates) are not flashing; risk that they change abruptly.
- Ch. 1 p. 2, Table 1.1: world 2004 5.1, proj. 2005 4.3, 2006 4.3; India 2004 7.3, proj. 2005 7.1, 2006 6.3.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: 2005 projections cut in most regions, with the exceptions of Japan and India.

## WEO April 2006 — *Globalization and Inflation*  (Foreword: Rajan)
Files: weo/2006/01/pdf/_frontmpdf.pdf, _c1pdf.pdf
- Foreword p. xi: world in "an extraordinary purple patch", a third year of above-trend growth; sub-Saharan Africa heading for best growth in over 30 years.
- Foreword p. xi: globalization cut advanced-economy inflation by about ¼ pp a year through non-oil import prices (½ pp in the US); G-7 corporate excess saving 2003–04 of $1.3 trillion, more than twice EM current account surpluses.
- Foreword p. xii: soft landing is the central case, but consumption could fall "as the froth comes out of house prices"; risks weighted to the downside. Imbalances "unsustainable at their current level".
- Foreword p. xiii: "These are the best of times but they are also the most dangerous of times" (14 words). Multilateralism "in retreat everywhere".
- Ch. 1 p. 1: growth keeps beating expectations, aided by benign financial conditions and accommodative policies; global imbalances continue to increase.
- Ch. 1 p. 2, Table 1.1: world 2004 5.3, 2005 4.8, proj. 2006 4.9, 2007 4.7; US 2005 3.5; China 2005 9.9; India 2005 8.3, proj. 2006 7.3, 2007 7.0.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: China, India and Russia together accounted for two-thirds of the upward revision to 2005 global growth.
- Ch. 1 p. 8: growth in China and India "continued to surprise on the upside".

## WEO September 2006 — *Financial Systems and Economic Cycles*  (Foreword: Rajan)
Files: weo/2006/02/pdf/_frontmpdf.pdf
- Foreword p. xi: third year of significantly above-trend growth; US slowing, euro area picking up.
- Foreword p. xi: in both China and India, TFP growth exceeds the contribution of physical or human capital; India's gains came through services as well as manufacturing.
- Foreword p. xi–xii: metals prices likely to fall as supply responds; commodity exporters should avoid hard-to-reverse spending.

## GFSR April 2006 — Ch. II *The Influence of Credit Derivative and Structured Credit Markets on Financial Stability*
File: gfsr/2006/01/pdf/_chp2pdf.pdf (Ch. I: _chp1pdf.pdf)
- Ch. II p. 51: "growing recognition" that dispersing credit risk from banks to a broader group of investors has made the banking and financial system more resilient (paraphrase; key phrase "more resilient"). Caveats on the same page: markets grew fast in a benign period, untested by a severe downturn; secondary-market liquidity lacking in some segments.
- Ch. I p. 1: risk now sits more with less regulated nonbank institutions; "potential for unpleasant surprises"; reliance on liquidity could amplify corrections.

## GFSR September 2006 — Executive Summary
File: gfsr/2006/02/pdf/_summarypdf.pdf
- p. ix: May–June 2006 sell-off called modest in scale; baseline of solid growth and contained inflation; risks include a sharper US slowdown with rapid weakening of housing, and illiquidity in complex instruments such as structured credit.

## Additional passages (same files)
- WEO Sep 2005, Ch. 1 p. 3: China's exchange reform of 21 July 2005: a 2.1 percent revaluation, a reference basket of currencies, a narrow daily band; renminbi broadly unchanged against the dollar since. Ch. 1 (US section): Congressional bill for across-the-board tariffs on China absent revaluation gathered wide support.
- WEO Apr 2006, Ch. 1 p. 7: a more abrupt slowdown in the housing market the most significant US risk; p. 11: greatest risks in the household sector where housing markets are elevated; p. 17 (United States section, by page sequence): the housing market is "a key uncertainty"; house prices "more richly valued"; a weaker housing market could trigger an abrupt withdrawal of consumer demand.

## Federal Reserve
- Greenspan, Monetary Policy Report testimony, US Senate, 16 February 2005 (https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/hh/2005/february/testimony.htm): long-term rates trended lower even as the Fed raised the funds rate target by 150 basis points; integration of China and India into global trade cited but "glacially increasing globalization" cannot explain it; the behaviour of world bond markets "remains a conundrum". Also: personal saving rate averaged only 1 percent over 2004; "people experiencing long periods of relative stability are prone to excess".
