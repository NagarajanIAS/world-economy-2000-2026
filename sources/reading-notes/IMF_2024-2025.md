## WEO April 2024 — *Steady but Slow: Resilience amid Divergence* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, April 2024: Steady but Slow: Resilience amid Divergence")
Read: Foreword "Global Economy Remains Resilient despite Uneven Growth; Challenges Lie Ahead" (pp. xiii–xv), Executive Summary (pp. xvi–xvii), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (ch1.pdf; pp. 1–24 main text and boxes; Table 1.1 pp. 10–11; Commodity Special Feature opening pp. 29–30; Annex Tables pp. 35–37).
- Foreword p. xiii (Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, Economic Counsellor): the world avoided a recession, banks proved largely resilient and big emerging markets had no sudden stops; the inflation surge did not set off wage-price spirals. Year-on-year global growth bottomed at 2.3% at end-2022, shortly after median headline inflation peaked at 9.4%. Growth to hold at about 3.2% in 2024 and 2025; median headline inflation 2.8% at end-2024 and 2.4% at end-2025.
- Foreword p. xiii: "Most indicators point to a soft landing."
- Foreword p. xiii: equity valuations soared as markets anticipated central banks exiting tight policy; less pandemic scarring than feared; "Astonishingly, the US economy has already surged past its prepandemic trend."
- Foreword p. xiii: US performance reflects strong demand, including a fiscal stance out of line with long-term sustainability, which risks pushing up global funding costs: "Something will have to give."
- Foreword p. xiv: China is affected by the enduring property downturn; "Credit booms and busts never resolve themselves quickly". With depressed domestic demand, external surpluses could rise and worsen trade tensions; other large emerging markets benefit from the reconfiguration of supply chains and China–US tensions.
- Foreword p. xiv: euro area growth picking up from very low levels; the ECB must calibrate its pivot to easing to avoid an excessive slowdown and an inflation undershoot.
- Foreword p. xiv: medium-term growth historically weak, mainly lower total factor productivity growth; "Much hope rests on artificial intelligence (AI) delivering strong productivity gains", with high potential for disruption in labour and financial markets. Geoeconomic fragmentation and the surge in trade-restrictive and industrial-policy measures since 2019 also harm prospects.
- Exec. Summary p. xvi: global growth estimated at 3.2% in 2023, projected at the same pace in 2024 and 2025; 2024 revised up 0.1 point from January 2024 and 0.3 point from October 2023. Global headline inflation from 6.8% (2023) to 5.9% (2024) and 4.5% (2025). Five-year-ahead growth of 3.1% is at its lowest in decades.
- Exec. Summary p. xvi: "Risks to the global outlook are now broadly balanced." Downside: price spikes from the war in Ukraine and the conflict in Gaza and Israel, a faltering China without a comprehensive property response, intensifying fragmentation. Upside: AI and structural reforms could spur productivity.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: in Q4 2023 AE headline inflation was 2.3% (quarter-on-quarter annualised), down from a 9.5% peak in Q2 2022; EMDEs 9.9%, down from 13.7% (median EMDE 3.9%). Activity grew steadily, "defying warnings of stagflation and global recession"; world real GDP rose a cumulative 6.7% in 2022–23, 0.8 point more than forecast in October 2022. Extra US budgetary support relative to October 2022 forecasts estimated at 2% of GDP (euro area 0.2%); China's fiscal stance was tighter than expected and its inflation fell unexpectedly.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: shipping costs rose after attacks on commercial shipping in the Red Sea, "through which 11 percent of global trade flows", with cargo rerouted to the Cape of Good Hope.
- Ch. 1 p. 6: the ECB and the Fed raised nominal rates only after inflation expectations started rising, so real rates initially fell; the Bank of Japan kept rates near zero; Brazil and Chile raised early and have already cut substantially.
- Ch. 1 pp. 8–9 (monetary assumptions): policy rates in major AEs expected to start declining in the second half of 2024; by Q4 2024 the Fed's rate from about 5.4% to 4.6%, the Bank of England's from about 5.3% to 4.8%, the ECB's short-term rate from about 4.0% to 3.3%. Japan's rates projected to rise gradually.
- Ch. 1 p. 9: euro area growth to recover from 0.4% (2023), which reflected high exposure to the war in Ukraine, to 0.8% (2024) and 1.5% (2025). p. 11: Germany revised down 0.3 point for both years amid persistently weak consumer sentiment.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 10 (2023 / 2024 / 2025): world 3.2/3.2/3.2; AEs 1.6/1.7/1.8; US 2.5/2.7/1.9; euro area 0.4/0.8/1.5; Germany –0.3/0.2/1.3; Japan 1.9/0.9/1.0; UK 0.1/0.5/1.5; EMDEs 4.3/4.2/4.2; China 5.2/4.6/4.1; **India 7.8/6.8/6.5**; Russia 3.6/3.2/1.8. World consumer prices 6.8/5.9/4.5 (AEs 4.6/2.6/2.0; EMDEs 8.3/8.3/6.2). Footnote 3 (p. 10): India on a fiscal-year basis.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 11 (footnotes): oil averaged $80.59 a barrel in 2023, assumed $78.61 in 2024 and $73.68 in 2025; assumed inflation for 2024/2025 of 2.4%/2.1% (euro area), 2.2%/2.1% (Japan), 2.9%/2.0% (US).
- Ch. 1 Annex Tables pp. 35–37 (consumer prices 2023/2024/2025): euro area 5.4/2.4/2.1; Germany 6.0/2.4/2.0; UK 7.3/2.5/2.0 (p. 35); Japan 3.3/2.2/2.1; China 0.2/1.0/2.0; India 5.4/4.6/4.2 (p. 36); US 4.1/2.9/2.0 (p. 37).
- Ch. 1 p. 12: China to slow from 5.2% to 4.6% and 4.1% as one-off factors fade and property weakness persists. India to remain strong at 6.8% (2024) and 6.5% (2025), reflecting "continuing strength in domestic demand and a rising working-age population".
- Ch. 1 p. 17 (risks): without a comprehensive restructuring package for China's property sector, a deeper real-estate decline could intensify disinflation into "sustained low inflation or deflation" (Box 1.2 scenario), with net negative spillovers to trading partners.
- Ch. 1 p. 19 (upside risk): generative AI marks a leap in technology's ability to outperform humans in several cognitive areas, but its impact and timing remain highly uncertain; in AEs "artificial intelligence could affect about 60 percent of workers", about half gaining and half seeing lower demand for their labour.
- Ch. 1 Box 1.1 p. 24 (*Fragmentation Is Already Affecting International Trade*): bilateral trade data before and after February 2022 confirm that "fragmentation is already underway": trade between politically distant blocs slowed more than within blocs; China–US links weakening since the 2017 trade tensions, with Mexico and Vietnam gaining.
- Ch. 1 p. 30 (Commodity Special Feature): geopolitical tensions and expected easing raised gold prices by 5.5% (August 2023–February 2024).

## WEO Update July 2024 — *The Global Economy in a Sticky Spot* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook Update, July 2024: The Global Economy in a Sticky Spot")
Read: whole Update (pp. 1–6, including Table 1 on p. 5 and Box 1, Global Financial Stability Update, p. 6). PDF p. 1 is the cover; printed p. 1 is PDF p. 2.
- p. 1: global growth 3.2% (2024) and 3.3% (2025), in line with April. "Services price inflation is holding up progress on disinflation", raising the prospect of higher-for-even-longer rates amid escalating trade tensions and policy uncertainty.
- p. 1: Q1 growth surprised on the upside in many countries but not in the US (moderating consumption) or Japan (a car-plant shutdown); the uptick in US sequential inflation delayed policy normalisation, putting the euro area and Canada ahead of the US in the easing cycle.
- p. 2: major central banks still expected to cut in the second half of 2024. US growth revised down to 2.6% (2024), slowing to 1.9% (2025); euro area 0.9% (2024), 1.5% (2025); continued manufacturing weakness means a slower recovery in Germany.
- p. 3: China revised up to 5% for 2024 (rebounding consumption, strong Q1 exports), 4.5% in 2025 and 3.3% by 2029. India revised up to 7.0% for 2024 on carryover from 2023 and better prospects for private (especially rural) consumption.
- p. 3: world trade to recover to about 3¼% a year in 2024–25 after quasi-stagnation in 2023; cross-border trade restrictions have surged, harming trade between geopolitically distant blocs.
- p. 4: elections this year raise the risk of big policy swings; tariffs plus scaled-up industrial policies could trigger retaliation, "resulting in a costly race to the bottom".

World consumer prices 6.7/5.9/4.4 (AEs 4.6/2.7/2.1; EMDEs 8.3/8.2/6.0). Footnote 4/: India on a fiscal-year basis (FY2022/23 in the 2022 column); on a calendar-year basis 7.3% (2024) and 6.5% (2025). - Box 1 p. 6 (Global Financial Stability Update): markets expect fewer 2024 cuts than in April; the figure note records that the ECB announced a policy-rate cut on 5 June 2024. The yen saw sustained depreciation pressure and official intervention.

## WEO October 2024 — *Policy Pivot, Rising Threats* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, October 2024: Policy Pivot, Rising Threats")
Read: Foreword "The Global Battle against Inflation Is Almost Won; A Policy Triple Pivot Is Now Needed" (pp. xii–xiii), Executive Summary (p. xiv), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (ch1.pdf; pp. 1–27 main text, Box 1.1 on electric vehicles, Box 1.2 risk scenarios; Table 1.1 pp. 10–11; Commodity Special Feature pp. 28–29; Annex Tables pp. 33–35).
- Foreword p. xii: "The global battle against inflation has largely been won", even though price pressures persist in some countries. Headline inflation peaked at 9.4% (year on year) in Q3 2022 and is projected at 3.5% by end-2025, below the 2000–19 average of 3.6%. Growth steady at 3.2% in 2024 and 2025.
- Foreword p. xii: disinflation came mainly from the unwinding of shocks and better labour supply (often immigration), but monetary policy kept expectations anchored and avoided a repeat of the 1970s.
- Foreword p. xii: the return of inflation near target "paves the way for a much-needed policy triple pivot"; the first, on monetary policy, has started: since June major AE central banks have been cutting rates toward neutral. Services inflation is still almost twice its pre-pandemic level.
- Foreword p. xiii: second pivot, fiscal: for the US and China "debt dynamics are not stabilized under current fiscal plans". Third, structural reforms: medium-term growth only 3.1%; the Draghi report assesses Europe's diminished prospects; industrial and trade-protection measures often lead to retaliation.
- Exec. Summary p. xiv: growth 3.2% in 2024 and 2025, virtually unchanged; US upgrades offset downgrades for the largest European economies; emerging Asia upgraded on semiconductor and electronics demand "driven by significant investments in artificial intelligence". Global headline inflation from 6.7% (2023) to 5.8% (2024) and 4.3% (2025). Risks tilted to the downside, including the early-August market turbulence, China's property sector and protectionism.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: "about half of the world population has gone or will go to the polls" in 2024; new governments could shift trade and fiscal policy (Box 1.2).
- Ch. 1 p. 5: in the first week of August, weak US jobs data stoked recession fears; together with the Bank of Japan's rate hike this caused "a rapid unwinding of Japanese-yen-funded carry trades", amplifying the equity correction.
- Ch. 1 p. 8 (monetary assumptions): euro area cuts of 100 bp in 2024 and 50 bp in 2025, taking the rate to 2.5% by June 2025; "the Federal Reserve pivoted to cutting rates in September", starting with 50 bp, and the federal funds rate is projected at its long-run 2.9% by Q3 2026; Japan's projected path revised up after the Bank of Japan's July hike, toward about 1.5%.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 10 (2023 / 2024 / 2025): world 3.3/3.2/3.2; AEs 1.7/1.8/1.8; US 2.9/2.8/2.2; euro area 0.4/0.8/1.2; Germany –0.3/0.0/0.8; Japan 1.7/0.3/1.1; UK 0.3/1.1/1.5; EMDEs 4.4/4.2/4.2; China 5.2/4.8/4.5; **India 8.2/7.0/6.5**; Russia 3.6/3.6/1.3. World consumer prices 6.7/5.8/4.3 (AEs 4.6/2.6/2.0; EMDEs 8.1/7.9/5.9). Footnote 3: India on a fiscal-year basis. Oil $80.59 in 2023, assumed $81.29 (2024) and $72.84 (2025).
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 11 (footnote 7): assumed inflation 2.4%/2.0% (euro area), 2.2%/2.0% (Japan), 3.0%/1.9% (US) for 2024/2025.
- Ch. 1 Annex Tables pp. 33–35 (consumer prices 2023/2024/2025): euro area 5.4/2.4/2.0; Germany 6.0/2.4/2.0 (p. 33); China 0.2/0.4/1.7; India 5.4/4.4/4.1 (p. 34); US 4.1/3.0/1.9 (p. 35).
- Ch. 1 p. 12: Germany strained by fiscal consolidation and a sharp fall in real-estate prices; manufacturing weakness weighs on Germany and Italy. India to moderate from 8.2% (2023) to 7% (2024) and 6.5% (2025) because "pent-up demand accumulated during the pandemic has been exhausted". China slows only to 4.8% in 2024 despite property weakness and low confidence, thanks to better-than-expected net exports.
- Ch. 1 p. 7: manufacturing production increasingly shifting toward EMDEs, in particular China and India, as AEs lose competitiveness.
- Ch. 1 Box 1.1 pp. 22–23 (*The Global Automotive Industry and the Shift to Electric Vehicles*): China's share of global car production and exports has risen dramatically over 15 years; it keeps a relative cost advantage in building EVs.
- Ch. 1 Box 1.2 pp. 25–27 (scenario A): a 10% tariff among the US, euro area and China and between the US and the rest of the world from mid-2025, affecting about a quarter of goods trade (close to 6% of world GDP), plus higher policy uncertainty and other layers; US GDP falls 0.4% (2025) and 0.6% (2026) from tariffs alone (p. 26); the combined scenario lowers world GDP about 0.8% by 2025 and 1.3% by 2026 (p. 27).
- Ch. 1 p. 29 (Commodity Special Feature): "Gold prices surged by 21.9 percent to record highs against the US dollar" (February–August 2024), on geopolitics and expected US rate cuts.

- p. 4: risks: near term tilted up for the US, down elsewhere; a new wave of tariffs could disrupt supply chains; looser US fiscal policy could later require disruptive adjustment and weaken US Treasuries' role as the global safe asset.

## WEO April 2025 — *A Critical Juncture amid Policy Shifts* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, April 2025: A Critical Juncture amid Policy Shifts")
Read: Foreword (pp. xii–xiv), Executive Summary (pp. xv–xvi), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (ch1.pdf; pp. 1–24 main text, Box 1.1 risk scenarios, Box 1.2 trade-policy models; Table 1.1 pp. 12–13; Commodity Special Feature "Market Developments and the Impact of AI on Energy Demand" pp. 35–37; Annex Tables pp. 41–43).
- Foreword p. xii (Gourinchas): the 2 April Rose Garden announcement forced IMF staff to discard near-final projections and compress a two-month cycle into under 10 days. After multiple waves of US tariffs ending in nearly universal tariffs on 2 April, the combination of measures and countermeasures "has hiked US and global tariff rates to centennial highs" (many increases on hold for now).
- Foreword p. xii: the central scenario is a *reference forecast*; "tariffs are a negative supply shock for the economy imposing them", and mostly a negative demand shock for trading partners.
- Foreword p. xiii: world trade growth cut by 1½ points this year; the dollar may appreciate or, given uncertainty and lower US growth prospects, weaken as it did right after the announcements; risks firmly to the downside; de-escalation could brighten the outlook at once.
- Foreword p. xiii: fiscal support for those hit by tariffs should be targeted and carry sunset clauses: "it is easier to open the tap of fiscal support than to close it". Some countries, especially in Europe, face permanent rises in defence spending. Generative AI offers promise for productivity.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: US tariffs and countermeasures since January ended in near-universal US tariffs on 2 April, "bringing effective tariff rates to levels not seen in a century". The report gives a reference forecast with information as of 4 April 2025, plus a range of forecasts.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: reference forecast: global growth 2.8% (2025) and 3% (2026), down from 3.3% for both years in January (cumulative 0.8 point). AEs 1.4% (2025); US 1.8% (0.9 point lower); euro area 0.8%; EMDEs 3.7% and 3.9%, with big downgrades for countries most affected, such as China. Global headline inflation 4.3% (2025) and 3.6% (2026).
- Exec. Summary p. xv (Figure ES.1 note): the 20 January–1 April tariffs include 20% on China, 25% on steel and aluminium, 25% on Mexico and Canada (USMCA carve-out assumed to halve the increase) and 10% on Canadian energy; the 9 April step raises tariffs on China to 145% and cuts other country-specific tariffs to 10%, with electronics exemptions announced on 11 April.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: the 2 April tariffs triggered historic drops in major equity indices and spikes in bond yields, then a partial recovery after the pause announced on 9 April; "price-to-earnings ratios in the United States remain at elevated levels" historically.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: China: prolonged real-estate weakness, including for local government finances; consumer confidence "plunged in early 2022 and has not recovered"; rebalancing toward consumption has paused amid continuing deflationary pressure (sentence continues on p. 4).
- Ch. 1 pp. 10–11 (monetary and fiscal assumptions): federal funds rate projected at 4% at end-2025 and its long-term 2.9% at end-2028; euro area 100 bp of cuts in 2025 (three cuts already made this year), to 2% by mid-year; Japan's rates rising toward about 1.5%. Germany's primary deficit to widen by about 1% of GDP by 2030 relative to 2024, driven mainly by higher defence and public investment spending.
- Ch. 1 p. 11: EMDE public debt rising from 70% of GDP to a projected 83% in 2030; China's structural fiscal balance to deteriorate by 1.2 points of GDP in 2025.
- Ch. 1 p. 11 (trade policy assumptions, tariff chronology): executive orders of 1 February imposed tariffs on Canada, China and Mexico; an extra 10% on China from 4 February and another 10% on 4 March; China retaliated with 10–15% tariffs on selected US farm goods, energy and farm equipment (10 February, 10 March); 25% on non-USMCA Canadian and Mexican goods from 4 March (10% on Canadian energy); Canada retaliated with 25% on about 40% of its US imports; steel and aluminium tariffs extended and aluminium raised to 25% from 12 March; 25% on cars from 3 April (parts postponed to 3 May). On 2 April the US Fair and Reciprocal Plan set a 10% minimum tariff (in force 5 April) and country rates up to 50% for about 60 countries (due 9 April), with exemptions for pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, energy and some minerals; China announced 34% tariffs on 4 April.
- Ch. 1 p. 13: alternative: under the pre–2 April forecast global growth would have been 3.2% in both 2025 and 2026.
- Ch. 1 p. 14 (post–9 April forecast): the US announced a 90-day pause on the higher rates but kept the 10% minimum while raising tariffs on China; the EU's 25% countermeasures were also paused; electronics exempted on 11 April. As of 14 April "the US effective tariff rate on the world stood at about 25 percent", up from under 3% in January 2025; the US rate on Chinese goods was 115% and China's on US goods 146%. Taken alone and as permanent, these changes would give global growth of about 2.8% (2025) and 2.9% (2026).
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 12 (2024 / 2025 / 2026, reference forecast): world 3.3/2.8/3.0; AEs 1.8/1.4/1.5; US 2.8/1.8/1.7; euro area 0.9/0.8/1.2; Germany –0.2/0.0/0.9; Japan 0.1/0.6/0.6; UK 1.1/1.1/1.4; EMDEs 4.3/3.7/3.9; China 5.0/4.0/4.0; **India 6.5/6.2/6.3**; Russia 4.1/1.5/0.9. World consumer prices 5.7/4.3/3.6 (AEs 2.6/2.5/2.2; EMDEs 7.7/5.5/4.6). Footnote 3: India on a fiscal-year basis. Oil $79.17 in 2024, assumed $66.94 (2025). Footnote 7: assumed inflation 2.1%/1.9% (euro area), 2.4%/1.7% (Japan), 3.0%/2.5% (US) for 2025/2026.
- Ch. 1 Annex Tables pp. 41–43 (consumer prices 2024/2025/2026): euro area 2.4/2.1/1.9; Germany 2.5/2.1/1.9 (p. 41); China 0.2/0.0/0.6; India 4.7/4.2/4.1 (p. 42); US 3.0/3.0/2.5 (p. 43).
- Ch. 1 pp. 14–15: US growth 1.8% in 2025, 1 point below 2024; euro area pickup in 2026 helped by projected fiscal easing in Germany after major changes to its fiscal rule (the debt brake). China cut to 4.0% for 2025 (from 4.6%) and 2026 (from 4.5%) on the tariffs. India "relatively more stable at 6.2 percent in 2025", supported by rural consumption but 0.3 point below January on trade tensions and global uncertainty.
