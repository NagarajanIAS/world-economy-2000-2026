## WEO April 2020 — *The Great Lockdown* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, April 2020: The Great Lockdown")
Read: Foreword (pp. v–vi), Executive Summary (pp. vii–ix), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (pp. 1–19, main text, Scenario Box, Commodity Special Feature).
Note: the first page of both the Foreword and the Executive Summary carries a stray running head from an older edition
("WORLD ECONOMIC OUTLOOK: TENSIONS FROM THE TWO-SPEED RECOVERY"); later pages read "THE GREAT LOCKDOWN". Do not cite the stray head.
- Foreword p. v (Gita Gopinath, Economic Counsellor): this year the global economy will very likely experience "its worst recession since the Great Depression", surpassing the global financial crisis.
- Foreword p. v: the name for the episode: "The Great Lockdown, as one might call it".
- Foreword p. v: "This crisis is like no other." Three reasons given: the shock is large; severe uncertainty about its duration and intensity; and, since the crisis largely results from needed containment, stimulating activity is harder and, for the most affected sectors, undesirable.
- Foreword p. vi: a global effort must ensure that rich and poor nations alike have immediate access to therapies and vaccines once developed. Contrast with the 1930s, when the absence of a multilateral lender of last resort pushed countries into mercantilist policies.
- Exec. Summary p. vii (same text opens Ch. 1, p. 1): global economy projected to contract by 3% in 2020, "much worse than during the 2008–09 financial crisis"; baseline (pandemic fades in H2 2020) has 5.8% growth in 2021.
- Exec. Summary p. vii: "There is extreme uncertainty around the global growth forecast." Many countries face a multi-layered crisis: health shock, domestic disruption, plummeting external demand, capital-flow reversals, collapsing commodity prices. Risks of a worse outcome predominate.
- Exec. Summary p. vii: fiscal response "swift and sizable" in many advanced economies (Australia, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, Spain, UK, US); China, Indonesia and South Africa also providing significant fiscal support. Policies should also limit lasting scarring from the unavoidable slowdown.
- Ch. 1 p. 2: lockdowns and distancing curtail mobility, hitting travel, hospitality, entertainment and tourism hardest: "There is a de facto shutdown of a significant portion of the economy."
- Ch. 1 p. 2: US initial jobless claims in the fourth week of March exceeded 6.6 million, against about 280,000 two weeks earlier.
- Ch. 1 p. 2: from mid-January to end-March base metals fell about 15%, natural gas 38%, crude oil about 65% (about $40 a barrel), after the breakdown of the OPEC+ agreement (Fig. 1.1: on 6 March).
- Ch. 1 p. 3: equities sold off; high-yield and EM sovereign spreads widened; portfolio flows to EM funds reversed (especially hard-currency bonds and equities); signs of dollar funding shortages amid a rebalancing toward cash and safe assets. US dollar up some 8½% in real effective terms as of 3 April.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: rate cuts, liquidity support and large asset purchases by the Fed, ECB, BoE, BoJ, Bank of Canada, RBA and EM central banks including Brazil, China, India, Malaysia, Mexico, the Philippines, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Thailand and Turkey. Footnote 1: coordinated swap-line action on 15 March 2020; Fed swap lines with nine more central banks on 19 March; Fed FIMA repo facility on 31 March.
- Ch. 1 p. 5: assumptions: countries with severe epidemics lose about 8% of working days in 2020, others about 5%; oil (APSP) $35.60 a barrel in 2020 and $37.90 in 2021, against $61.40 in 2019.
- Ch. 1 p. 5: 2020 global growth of –3.0% is "an outcome far worse than during the 2009 global financial crisis"; the forecast was cut more than 6 points from October 2019/January 2020, an extraordinary revision in so short a time.
- Ch. 1 p. 5: advanced economies –6.1% in 2020 (US –5.9, Japan –5.2, UK –6.5, Germany –7.0, France –7.2, Italy –9.1, Spain –8.0). EMDEs –1.0% (excluding China –2.2%). Emerging Asia the only region with positive growth (1.0%).
- Ch. 1 p. 5: China's Q1 2020 contraction could have been about 8% year on year; growth projected at 1.2% in 2020. India projected at 1.9%.
- Ch. 1 p. 6: 2021 rebound: world 5.8%, AEs 4.5%, EMDEs 6.6% (2010 rebound was 5.4% after –0.1% in 2009). GDP at end-2021 still below the pre-virus (January 2020) baseline in both groups. A much larger share of countries than in 2009 expected to have negative per-capita income growth.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1, p. 7: India 4.2% (2019), 1.9% (2020), 7.4% (2021); China 6.1 / 1.2 / 9.2; world trade volume –11.0% in 2020.
- Ch. 1 p. 11: India announced new in-kind (food and cooking gas) and cash transfers to poorer households.
- Ch. 1 p. 13: the IMF has "$1 trillion in available resources" to support vulnerable countries; access limits to emergency financing doubled.
- Ch. 1 Scenario Box p. 15: longer containment in 2020 puts global output 3% below baseline in 2020; a second outbreak in 2021 puts it almost 5% below baseline in 2021; both together almost 8% below baseline in 2021. EM scarring assumed 50% larger than in AEs because of limited fiscal space.
- Ch. 1 Commodity Special Feature pp. 17–19: OPEC+ deal broke down on 6 March 2020; as of 27 March, futures implied Brent rising toward $45 over five years. (Negative WTI is not mentioned: the report predates it; see October 2020.)

## WEO Update June 2020 — *A Crisis Like No Other, An Uncertain Recovery*
Read: whole Update (running heads present, pp. 1–20).
- p. 1: global growth –4.9% in 2020, 1.9 points below April; 5.4% in 2021, leaving 2021 GDP "some 6½ percentage points lower than in the pre-COVID-19 projections of January 2020".
- p. 1: slower recovery reflects persistent distancing, greater scarring (damage to supply potential) and a productivity hit from workplace safety practices. The rebound in financial-market sentiment appears disconnected from underlying economic prospects.
- p. 2: lockdowns most intense and widespread from about mid-March to mid-May. ILO: Q1 2020 work-hour decline equivalent to 130 million full-time jobs; Q2 likely more than 300 million.
- p. 2: Q1 GDP generally worse than expected (exceptions include Chile, China, India, Malaysia, Thailand); Q2 contraction more severe except in China, where most of the country had reopened by early April. Unusually, consumption and services fell markedly.
- p. 3: fiscal measures of about $11 trillion announced worldwide (citing the June 2020 Fiscal Monitor database).
- Table 1: AEs –8.0% (2020) / 4.8% (2021); EMDEs –3.0 / 5.9; China 1.0 / 8.2; India –4.5 / 6.0 (down 6.4 points for 2020 from April).

## WEO October 2020 — *A Long and Difficult Ascent*
Read: Foreword (pp. xiii–xiv), Executive Summary (pp. xv–xvii), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (selected pages incl. Commodity Special Feature).
- Foreword p. xiii (Gopinath): many economies recovering faster than expected after reopening from the Great Lockdown; policy actions have so far prevented a repeat of the 2008–09 financial catastrophe; "the ascent will likely be long, uneven, and uncertain".
- Exec. Summary p. xv: "The global economy is climbing out from the depths" of the Great Lockdown in April; China's recovery faster than expected; partial lockdowns being reinstated.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: global growth –4.4% in 2020 (less severe than June because Q2 outturns were better, mostly in AEs); 5.2% in 2021; 2021 global GDP only 0.6% above 2019. Medium-term growth about 3.5%.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: the pandemic will reverse progress since the 1990s in reducing poverty: "Close to 90 million people could fall below the $1.90 a day" threshold this year.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: baseline assumes distancing continues into 2021 and fades as vaccine coverage expands; local transmission low everywhere by end-2022. Scarring assumed (bankruptcies, reallocation costs, discouraged workers).
- Exec. Summary p. xv: "The uncertainty surrounding the baseline projection is unusually large."
- Exec. Summary p. xvi: downside risk if access to treatments and vaccines "remains unequal"; priority: fund advance purchase commitments for vaccines (CEPI, Gavi). IMF has funded about 80 countries since the crisis began.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: confirmed infections exceeded 33 million with over a million deaths. India named among cases where Q2 domestic demand plunged (sharp fall in consumption, collapse in investment).
- Ch. 1 p. 4: "Massive policy support has prevented worse outcomes." AE discretionary revenue and spending measures more than 9% of GDP, plus another 11% in liquidity support (equity injections, asset purchases, loans, guarantees).
- Ch. 1 p. 5: EMDE response about 3.5% of GDP in discretionary measures and more than 2% in liquidity support. EU €750 billion recovery fund; Fed moved to flexible average inflation targeting; many EM central banks used asset purchases for the first time.
- Ch. 1 p. 5: easier financial conditions imply a continuing disconnect between markets and the real economy, partly reflecting unprecedented policy support.
- Ch. 1 p. 8: baseline fiscal settings reflect the $6 trillion of direct tax and spending measures worldwide (citing the October 2020 Fiscal Monitor). Advanced economies –5.8% in 2020, 3.9% in 2021, leaving 2021 GDP some 2% below 2019; US –4.3% then 3.1%.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1, p. 9: world –4.4 / 5.2; AEs –5.8 / 3.9; EMDEs –3.3 / 6.0; China 1.9 / 8.2; India –10.3 / 8.8 (India's 2020 figure cut 12.2 points from April and 5.8 from June).
- Ch. 1 p. 11: China projected to grow about 10% over 2020–21 (1.9% and 8.2%) after reopening in early April, strong policy support and resilient exports. India: GDP contracted much more severely than expected in Q2, so projected –10.3% in 2020 and 8.8% in 2021. EMDEs excluding China –5.7% in 2020.
- Ch. 1 p. 13 (scarring): potential GDP in the 10 largest AEs expected to stay about 3.5% below the pre-pandemic (January 2020) projection over the medium term; in the 10 largest EMs about 5.5% below.
- Ch. 1 p. 21: the global economy is in the grip of the worst public-health crisis and its "worst recession in decades".
- Ch. 1 Commodity Special Feature p. 43: oil prices fell 60% between February and April 2020 as demand collapsed and storage worries grew; OPEC+ cut output by 9.7 million barrels a day in May–June; the front-month WTI futures price "briefly went to –$37 in April". Shale shut-ins and bankruptcies followed.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 note, p. 10: APSP oil $61.39 a barrel in 2019; assumed $41.69 in 2020, $46.70 in 2021.

## WEO Update January 2021 — *Policy Support and Vaccines Expected to Lift Activity*
Read: whole Update. The PDF has no "International Monetary Fund |" running heads; pages 2–11 carry bare page numbers, page 1 is unnumbered.
- p. 1 (unnumbered): "Amid exceptional uncertainty", world growth projected at 5.5% in 2021 (up 0.3 point) and 4.2% in 2022; 2020 contraction estimated at –3.5% (0.9 point better than October).
- p. 1: the 2020 collapse hit women, youth, the poor, informal workers and contact-intensive sectors hardest; recovery strength will vary with access to medical interventions and policy support. Calls for more COVAX funding and debt treatment under the G20 Common Framework.
- p. 2: Q3 2020 outturns mostly surprised on the upside (incl. India); investment slow except in China.
- p. 3: baseline assumes broad vaccine availability in AEs and some EMs in summer 2021 and in most countries by the second half of 2022.
- Table 1, p. 4: AEs –4.9 (2020) / 4.3 (2021); EMDEs –2.4 / 6.3; China 2.3 / 8.1; India –8.0 / 11.5 / 6.8 (2022).

## WEO Update July 2021 — *Fault Lines Widen in the Global Recovery*
Read: whole Update (running heads present).
- p. 1: "Vaccine access has emerged as the principal fault line" splitting the recovery into two blocs: almost all AEs, which can expect normalisation later in 2021, and the rest, still facing resurgent infections.
- p. 1: world growth 6.0% in 2021 (unchanged from April, with offsetting revisions) and 4.9% in 2022 (up 0.5 point, mainly the US, on expected extra fiscal support).
- p. 1: recent price pressures mostly reflect "transitory supply-demand mismatches"; inflation expected back in pre-pandemic ranges in 2022; central banks should generally look through, but may need preemptive action if pressures persist.
- p. 1: $50 billion IMF staff vaccine proposal (with WHO, WTO, World Bank); proposed $650 billion SDR allocation.
- p. 2: close to 40% of the AE population fully vaccinated, less than half that in EMs, a tiny fraction in low-income countries.
- p. 3: recovery set back severely where renewed waves hit, "notably India"; Delta delayed the UK's final reopening step.
- p. 7: EMDE 2021 forecast cut 0.4 point from April, mainly emerging Asia; India downgraded after the severe second wave in March–May; China's 2021 forecast cut 0.3 point on scaled-back public investment and fiscal support.
- Table 1: world –3.2 / 6.0 / 4.9; AEs –4.6 / 5.6 / 4.4; EMDEs –2.1 / 6.3 / 5.2; China 2.3 / 8.1 / 5.7; India –7.3 / 9.5 / 8.5 (India 2021 cut 3.0 points from April).

## WEO October 2021 — *Recovery During a Pandemic* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, October 2021: Recovery During A Pandemic"; running head: "RECOVERY DURING A PANDEMIC")
Read: Executive Summary (pp. xv–xvi), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (pp. 1–12 and Table 1.1).
- Exec. Summary p. xv: the recovery continues as the pandemic resurges; COVID fault lines look more persistent. "Vaccine access and early policy support are the principal drivers of the gaps."
- Exec. Summary p. xv: world growth 5.9% in 2021 and 4.9% in 2022 (2021 down 0.1 point from July), reflecting an AE downgrade partly due to supply disruptions and an LIDC downgrade on worsening pandemic dynamics. Medium-term growth about 3.3%.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: AE output forecast to exceed pre-pandemic medium-term projections (largely on expected US policy support); EMDEs face persistent output losses because of slower vaccine rollouts and less policy support.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: headline inflation up rapidly in the US and some EMDEs, mostly from pandemic-related supply-demand mismatches and higher commodity prices; expected to subside in 2022 for the most part. "Inflation risks are skewed to the upside" if mismatches last longer.
- Exec. Summary pp. xv–xvi: IMF proposal to vaccinate at least 40% of every country's population by end-2021 and 70% by mid-2022; the $650 billion SDR general allocation provided international liquidity.
- Exec. Summary p. xvi: central banks can generally look through transitory inflation pressures but should be ready to act quickly; where expectations risk de-anchoring, tightening may be needed even if it delays the employment recovery.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: about 58% of the AE population fully vaccinated, against about 36% in EMs and less than 5% in low-income developing countries.
- Ch. 1 p. 4: US downgrade linked to inventory drawdowns (partly supply disruptions); Germany to input shortages in manufacturing; Japan to the fourth State of Emergency. US baseline includes the infrastructure bill and expected social-spending legislation worth about $4 trillion over 10 years.
- Ch. 1 p. 7: China's 2021 prospects marked down slightly on stronger-than-expected scaling back of public investment. LIDCs need close to $200 billion to fight the pandemic and $250 billion to regain pre-pandemic convergence paths; 65–75 million more people in extreme poverty in 2021 than projected pre-pandemic. ILO: 2020 hours lost equivalent to 255 million full-time jobs.
- Ch. 1 p. 8: all AEs expected to regain pre-COVID output by end-2022, but only two-thirds their pre-pandemic employment.
- Ch. 1 p. 9: supply bottlenecks: microchip production still short of demand; shipping containers stranded off their usual routes; Suez Canal closure, Pearl River Delta port restrictions and Los Angeles/Long Beach congestion lengthened delivery times. The Baltic Dry Index rise was mostly supply-driven (Fig. 1.10). Oil prices expected close to 60% above their low 2020 base in 2021; non-oil commodities almost 30% higher.
- Ch. 1 p. 11: baseline has inflation back in its pre-pandemic range in most economies in 2022, but the outlook is highly uncertain; more persistent supply disruptions or broad wage and price increases are the risk.
- Ch. 1 p. 12 (scarring): activity generally below its pre-pandemic path through 2023; AE output back to its pre-pandemic trend by 2022 and slightly above it thereafter (mainly US policy support); other income groups below their pre-pandemic paths throughout the forecast horizon.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1, p. 5: world –3.1 (2020) / 5.9 / 4.9; AEs –4.5 / 5.2 / 4.5; US –3.4 / 6.0 / 5.2; EMDEs –2.1 / 6.4 / 5.1; China 2.3 / 8.0 / 5.6; India –7.3 / 9.5 / 8.5; LIDCs 0.1 / 3.0 / 5.3.
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 note, p. 6: APSP oil $41.29 a barrel in 2020; assumed $65.68 in 2021, $64.52 in 2022.

## Fiscal Monitor April 2020 — *Policies to Support People During the COVID-19 Pandemic* (Ch. 1 title; issue page title "Fiscal Monitor - April 2020")
Read: Ch. 1 Executive Summary (p. 1) and Chapter 1 (pp. 1–8).
- Exec. Summary p. 1: the pandemic has raised the need for fiscal action "to an unprecedented level". Direct fiscal costs of health and support measures estimated at $3.3 trillion globally; public loans and equity injections $1.8 trillion; guarantees and other contingent liabilities $2.7 trillion. Global public debt was 83% of GDP in 2019; revenue 2.5% of world GDP below the October 2019 projection for 2020.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: spending and revenue measures total $3.3 trillion; loans, equity injections and guarantees $4.5 trillion.
- Ch. 1 p. 4: global public debt projected to rise 13 points to 96.4% of GDP in 2020 (Table 1.2).
- Ch. 1 p. 8: the US CARES Act includes about $2 trillion, almost 10% of GDP, in tax, spending and liquidity measures.

## Fiscal Monitor October 2020 — *Policies for the Recovery*
Read: Executive Summary (pp. xi–xiii); full text searched for India.
- Exec. Summary p. xi: fiscal actions amounted to $11.7 trillion, close to 12% of global GDP, as of 11 September 2020; half additional spending or forgone revenue, half liquidity support (loans, guarantees, capital injections).
- Exec. Summary p. xi: 2020 deficits to rise by an average of 9% of GDP; "global public debt is projected to approach 100 percent of GDP, a record high".
- Exec. Summary p. xi: central bank liquidity and asset purchases eased fiscal expansion in AEs and some EMs; in many EMs and especially LIDCs financing constraints were binding. 100–110 million people expected to enter extreme poverty (80–90 million after extra social assistance).
- Exec. Summary p. xii: as lockdowns ease, governments should not withdraw lifelines too rapidly.
- Ch. 1 p. 7: India among countries that raised fuel excise taxes and tapped extrabudgetary funds.

## Fiscal Monitor April 2021 — *A Fair Shot* (issue page title "Fiscal Monitor, April 2021")
Read: Executive Summary (pp. xi–xii), Chapter 1 (pp. 1, 9).
- Exec. Summary p. xi: AEs implementing measures of 6% of GDP on average in 2021; support in EMs and LIDCs smaller and front-loaded. 2020 deficits averaged 11.7% of GDP (AEs), 9.8% (EMs), 5.5% (LIDCs).
- Exec. Summary p. xi: "Average public debt worldwide reached an unprecedented 97 percent of GDP in 2020", projected to stabilise around 99% in 2021.
- Exec. Summary p. xii: in an upside scenario global vaccination would yield more than $1 trillion in extra AE tax revenue by 2025: vaccination "will, thus, more than pay for itself". School closures cost about a quarter of a school year in AEs, half in EMDEs.
- Ch. 1 pp. 1, 9: $16 trillion in global pandemic-related fiscal actions since the start of the pandemic (data cut-off 17 March 2021).

## GFSR April 2020 — *Markets in the Time of COVID-19* (Executive Summary pp. x–xi; Ch. 1 "Global Financial Stability Overview")
- Exec. Summary p. x: the outbreak dealt "an unprecedented blow to global financial markets"; risk assets plummeted and borrowing costs soared; emerging and frontier markets saw the sharpest portfolio-flow reversal on record.
- Exec. Summary p. x: equities fell sharply from mid-February; credit spreads skyrocketed and issuance of high-yield bonds, leveraged loans and private debt essentially halted; the failed OPEC+ talks added to the slide; flight to quality lowered safe-haven yields. Strains in short-term dollar funding markets were reminiscent of the 2008 crisis; liquidity deteriorated even in very deep markets; leveraged investors were forced to close positions to meet margin calls.
- Exec. Summary p. x: "Emerging and frontier market economies are facing the perfect storm." The outflows were the sharpest on record both in dollars and as a share of GDP.
- Exec. Summary p. xi: central banks eased, bought assets and provided liquidity; EMs should use exchange-rate flexibility; capital flow measures cannot substitute for macroeconomic adjustment; the IMF has $1 trillion available.
- Ch. 1 pp. 3–6: precautionary demand for cash drove US corporate bond issuance; prime money market funds cut commercial-paper holdings to raise cash, so CP spreads widened sharply; leveraged basis trades in US Treasuries unwound and Treasury-market liquidity deteriorated sharply, prompting Fed action. (The phrase "dash for cash" does not occur in Ch. 1.)
- Ch. 1 p. 9: nonresident portfolio outflows from emerging markets reached a record in dollar terms, more than $100 billion since 21 January, and the highest ever relative to their GDP in Q1 2020.
