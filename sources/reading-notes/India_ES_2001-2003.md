# India: Economic Surveys 2001-02 and 2002-03 — reading notes

Reading notes for the India section of "The bust and the turn" chapter (2000-2002: the dot-com bust and the Nasdaq fall from March 2000, the 2001 US recession, 9/11, Argentina's 2001-02 default, China's WTO entry in December 2001, very low interest rates). They record what each Survey said at the time. They are not prose for the book.

**Sources.** Government of India, Ministry of Finance, *Economic Survey* 2001-02 and 2002-03 (official English files from indiabudget.gov.in; archive pages listed in `Econ Subject/sources/INDEX.md`). Read from the extracts in `Econ Subject/sources/text/`: `es2001-02.txt` (the PDF, Chapters 2-7), `es2001-02_ch1_general_review_html.txt` (Chapter 1 of 2001-02, published only as HTML) and `es2002-03.txt` (Chapter 1 appended at st. 416-436). Up to three retrospective items come from ES 2003-04 Chapter 1 (`es2003-04.txt`); they are numbered ES03-R1 and so on to keep them apart from the ES03-n items in `India_ES_2003-2008.md`, which I checked to avoid repeating.

**No ES 2000-01.** No text of the 2000-01 Survey (February 2001) is available, so the Survey's view of 2000-01 as it happened (the oil shock, the Nasdaq peak and fall, the India Millennium Deposits) is not recorded at first hand. Everything on 2000-01 here is the look back in ES 2001-02 and ES 2002-03.

**Data cut-offs, as the texts show them.** ES 2001-02 (presented February 2002) uses the CSO Quick Estimates for 2000-01 of 31 January 2002 and the Advance Estimates for 2001-02; its latest data are for January and early February 2002 (Sensex on 8 February 2002). ES 2002-03 (February 2003) uses CSO data of 31 January 2003 (Quick Estimates for 2001-02) and the Advance Estimates of 7 February 2003 for 2002-03, with data to end-January 2003. All GDP figures are at factor cost in 1993-94 prices unless marked.

**How pages are cited.** "p." is the **printed** page (running head or footer). "st." is the page of the stitched file (form feeds + 1). Where no printed number exists, I cite st. only and say so.

## Economic Survey 2001-02

**ES01-1 — Growth: 4.0% in 2000-01, 5.4% expected in 2001-02.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.1, 1.13 and 1.17 (no page numbers).
The opening paragraph says the economy is going through a difficult phase, for domestic reasons (two poor farm years) and external ones (a world slowdown to 2.4% growth, made worse by the 9/11 attacks). The CSO Advance Estimate puts 2001-02 growth at 5.4%, a recovery from 4.0% in 2000-01. Para 1.13 gives the 2000-01 figure as the Quick Estimate of 31 January 2002 and says growth fell from 6.1% in 1999-2000. The Survey calls 5.4% one of the highest growth rates in the world that year.
Quote: "The Indian economy is passing through a difficult phase".
See ES02-1: both 2000-01 and 2001-02 were later revised up.

**ES01-2 — Sector detail: farm rebound, industry and services slow.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.3-1.6, 1.14-1.16 and 1.41.
2001-02 (AE): agriculture 5.7%, industry 3.3%, services 6.5%. The acceleration is mainly farm output, up from −0.2% in 2000-01. Manufacturing slows from 6.7% to 3.3%, construction from 6.8% to 2.9%. In 2000-01 services grew only 4.8%; from 1993-94 to 1999-2000 they had grown 7.1-10.5% every year (para 1.41). financial, real estate and business services fell from 10.6% to 2.9% growth, and trade, hotels and restaurants from 7.3% to 3.8%. Para 1.6: transport, tourism and business services were hurt in 2001-02 by weak domestic and external demand. Foodgrains were expected at 209 million tonnes in 2001-02, against 196 mt in 2000-01.

**ES01-3 — Industry: the lowest growth in ten years, and the explanations offered.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.4, 1.34-1.36.
IIP growth in April-December 2001 was 2.3%, against 5.8% a year earlier. Manufacturing grew 2.4% (6.0% a year before). Capital goods fell 4.8%; only consumer durables grew at double digits (12.5%). The explanations listed are business and investment cycles, corporate restructuring lags, weak domestic and external demand, continued high real interest rates, infrastructure bottlenecks in power and transport, and (para 1.34) the lack of land and labour market reform.
Quote: "the lowest recorded during the last ten years".

**ES01-4 — Investment not yet recovering.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.18-1.22.
Real GDCF growth fell from 15.7% (1999-2000) to 2.0% (2000-01); real GFCF from 8.6% to 4.7%. Gross domestic saving was 23.4% of GDP in 2000-01, with public saving at −1.7%. For 2001-02 the leading indicators are mixed: capital-goods output and imports down, AIFI sanctions down sharply (−32.1% in April-December 2001, para 1.76). Foreign investment inflows improved, but the Survey thinks much of it had gone into reserves rather than new investment, so it sees no significant recovery of investment in 2001-02.

**ES01-5 — Resilience, and insulation through a small external sector.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.2 and 1.87; Ch 6 Outlook, paras 6.62-6.63, st. 189.
Ninth Plan (1997-2002) growth is now put at 5.4% a year, against a 6.5% target. The Survey lists the external shocks absorbed: the East Asian crisis of 1997-98, the oil price rise of 2000-01 and the current world slowdown. It also lists domestic ones: an adverse security environment, the Orissa cyclone, the Gujarat earthquake and two poor farm years.
Para 1.87: the slowdown has worsened the outlook for emerging markets through smaller capital inflows and restricted access to international markets. China and India should stay relatively insulated because the external sector is a small part of their GDP. Para 6.62 says the same of India alone, and adds that a recovery in advanced economies would lift demand for Indian exports. Para 6.63 warns that a more open economy will not stay insulated from external shocks.
Quote: "China and India are expected to remain relatively insulated from the global slowdown".

**ES01-6 — The world in 2001: a formally declared US recession.** Ch 6 External Sector, paras 6.1-6.2 and Table 6.1, st. 153 (no printed page); Ch 1 (HTML), para 1.86.
Table 6.1 (IMF WEO, December 2001): world output 4.7% in 2000, 2.4% in 2001 and 2.4% projected for 2002. US 4.1%, 1.0%, 0.7%; Japan 2.2%, −0.4%, −1.0%; China 8.0%, 7.3%, 6.8%; India 6.0%, 4.4%, 5.2%. World trade volume 12.4% in 2000, 1.0% in 2001. Oil prices +56.9% in 2000, −14.0% in 2001. Net private capital flows to emerging markets were US$8.9bn in 2000 and US$20.1bn in 2001. Para 6.2 calls the slowdown synchronized and self-reinforcing, the most marked in two decades; Japan faces its fourth recession of the decade.
Quote: "The downturn in the US economy has been formally declared as a recession."
**FLAG (revision):** ES 2002-03 Table 6.1 (ES02-5) revises 2001 to world 2.2%, US 0.3% and world trade −0.1%.

**ES01-7 — Box 6.1: what 9/11 did to the world economy, and to India.** Ch 6, Box 6.1 and para 6.3, st. 154; para 6.13, st. 159; Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.6 and 1.87.
Box 6.1 says the attacks deepened the existing slowdown and forced down growth projections for almost every region. It puts the direct cost to the US at US$21.4bn (about 0.25% of GDP), and names airlines, insurance, hotels, tourism, travel and civil aircraft as the worst-hit industries. For developing countries it lists lower risk appetite, a sharp fall in private capital flows, lower tourism earnings and higher trade costs. Para 6.3 adds the US retaliation in Afghanistan. For India, tourism receipts grew only 4.3% in 2000-01 (to US$3.17bn), and net tourism receipts fell US$0.6bn (para 6.13); para 1.6 says tourism and transport were hit in 2001-02.
Quote: "plunged the world into a deeper economic crisis".

**ES01-8 — The stock market and FII money: a 36% fall to April 2001, then 9/11.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.83 and 1.85; Ch 4 Capital and Money Markets, paras 4.1-4.2, st. 65, and para 4.14, st. 71.
The Sensex fell to 3184 on 12 April 2001, 36.3% below 5001 at end-March 2000. Para 4.2 blames fears of a global slowdown and reports of stock market irregularities (the March 2001 misconduct episode). After 9/11 the Sensex fell to 2600 on 21 September 2001, more than 1,000 points below 3604 at the start of the fiscal year. It recovered to 3443 by 10 December, lost about 180 points after the 13 December attack on Parliament, and closed at 3494 on 8 February 2002. Nifty fell 37.1% from 1 March to 21 September 2001 (para 4.14). Fig. 4.2 plots the Sensex against the Nasdaq Composite and Nikkei 225, but the text does not discuss the Nasdaq.
Net FII investment was positive in every month of April 2001-January 2002 except September 2001. It totalled US$1,295 million over those ten months, against US$1,379 million a year earlier. In September 2001 FII sales exceeded purchases by about 16% and net investment was −US$113 million.
Quote: "led to further squeezing of the stock indices at home".

**ES01-9 — The rupee and the RBI's September 2001 response.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.96-1.97; Ch 6, para 6.21, st. 163.
In 2000-01 the rupee came under pressure from mid-May 2000, falling 6.7% between end-April and end-October 2000 (Rs 43.655 to Rs 46.775 per dollar), and 5.15% over the year, to Rs 45.68. The RBI took measures on 15-25 September 2001 after the rupee fell 1.3% in 10-20 September. At end-January 2002 the rate was Rs 48.58, a 4.0% depreciation from Rs 46.64 at end-March 2001. Para 6.21 compares the cumulative depreciation since March 2000 (9.2% to November 2001) with the Indonesian rupiah (29.4%), Philippine peso (22.3%), baht (14.7%), won (13.0%) and Singapore dollar (11.4%); the ringgit and the yuan were stable.
Quote: "The world economy experienced one of its worst shocks in recent times".

**ES01-10 — Exports stall at 0.6% growth; competition and anti-dumping.** Ch 6, paras 6.15-6.21 and Tables 6.4-6.5, st. 160-163; Ch 1 (HTML), para 1.10.
DGCI&S exports grew 21.0% in dollars in 2000-01 (19.6% on a BOP basis), helped by world demand and trade recovering after the Asian crisis. In April-December 2001 they grew only 0.6%, against 20.8% a year earlier. Causes named: slower world output and trade, a slower domestic economy, and the real appreciation of the rupee. Measures: lower export credit rates, higher duty drawback on over 300 products, DEPB value caps removed on over 400 items, and the Medium Term Export Strategy of 30 January 2002.
World merchandise exports fell 0.3% in the first half of 2001, and imports into industrial countries fell 1.9%; US imports fell 3.4% in 2001 (data to September). Para 6.20 expects fiercer competition from India's main competitors and more non-tariff barriers. It cites WTO data showing 134 anti-dumping investigations in the first half of 2001 (93 a year earlier), with China, Korea, Taiwan and India the most targeted countries. Para 6.21 says the rupee's real appreciation in 2001-02 is hurting exports, given larger falls in East Asian currencies.
Quote: "Slackening of global demand, global downturn in IT and softening of commodity prices".

**ES01-11 — Software exports: US$6.3bn in 2000-01, up 57%; the US slowdown.** Ch 1 (HTML), para 1.46; Ch 6, para 6.12, st. 159; Ch 7, paras 7.52-7.59, st. 141-143.
On the BOP basis, software service exports rose from US$4.02bn (1999-2000) to US$6.3bn (2000-01), 57% growth, after about 52.5% a year in the five years to 1999-2000; they were the second-largest invisible receipt. In rupees (Ch 7), software exports rose from Rs 17,150 crore to Rs 28,350 crore (65%), and were expected to reach Rs 40,000 crore in 2001-02. Para 7.54 gives a turnover of US$8.3bn and exports of US$6.2bn for 2000-01, and says more than 260 of the Fortune 1,000 outsource software to India. Para 6.10 expects continued buoyancy of software service exports in 2001-02.
Of the Rs 28,350 crore of 2000-01 software exports, almost 62% went to North America, 24% to Europe, 4% to Japan and 10% elsewhere. With the US slowing, Indian firms were expanding in Europe and forming alliances in Asia; the Survey says US companies' interest in Indian software had in fact grown in recent months, and European and Japanese outsourcing to India was rising. IT-enabled services employed 70,000 people and earned 10.6% of IT software and services revenue (para 7.56). Offshore work rose to about 44% of exports, against 56% on-site. The text does not mention the dot-com collapse or the Nasdaq fall as such.
Quote: "Software and IT enabled services have emerged as a niche sector for India".
**FLAG (internal):** 2000-01 software exports are US$6.3bn in Ch 1 and Ch 6 (BOP software services) but US$6.2bn in Ch 7 (industry data). **FLAG (forecast vs outcome):** the Rs 40,000 crore forecast for 2001-02 became Rs 36,500 crore in ES 2002-03 Table 7.20 (ES02-8).

**ES01-12 — BOP 2000-01: oil, US rates and the India Millennium Deposits.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.88-1.92; Ch 6, paras 6.5-6.9 and Table 6.2, st. 156-158.
The first half of 2000-01 saw BOP pressure from higher oil prices, the sharp fall in international equity prices and successive interest rate rises in the US and Europe. The India Millennium Deposits (US$5.51bn, October-November 2000) reversed the fall in reserves. The current-account deficit narrowed from 1.1% of GDP (US$4.70bn) in 1999-2000 to about 0.5% in 2000-01. BOP exports rose 19.6%, imports 7.0% (oil +24.1%, non-oil +2.0%). Net capital inflows (excluding IMF) were US$9.02bn, against US$10.44bn. Reserves rose US$5.83bn, the fifth straight year of accumulation.
Quote: "the situation eased with the mobilisation of funds under the India Millennium Deposits".
**FLAG (internal):** the 2000-01 current-account gap is US$2.58bn in Ch 1 para 1.90 but US$2.68bn in Ch 6 para 6.7. Table 6.2 gives −2,579 (US$ million), so 2.68 is a slip.

**ES01-13 — BOP 2001-02: deficit expected to widen, within 1% of GDP.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.10 and 1.93-1.94; Ch 6, para 6.10, st. 158.
An RBI-Finance Ministry assessment expected the 2001-02 current-account deficit to widen somewhat because of poor exports, but to stay within 1% of GDP. Lower oil prices after September 2001 eased the trade account, and net invisibles were expected at about last year's level. Net capital inflows were expected to more than cover the deficit. Table 6.2 shows a current-account deficit of only US$938m in April-September 2001.
**FLAG (revision):** ES 2002-03 Table 6.2 revises April-September 2001 to a deficit of US$1,461m.
Quote: "it is expected to remain within 1 per cent of GDP".
**FLAG (forecast vs outcome):** ES 2002-03 reports a current-account **surplus** of 0.3% of GDP (US$1.35bn) for 2001-02 (ES02-6). The deficit the Survey expected did not happen.

**ES01-14 — Reserves near US$50bn; less indebted for the first time.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.11-1.12, 1.95 and 1.105; Ch 6, paras 6.53 and 6.57-6.60, Tables 6.12-6.13, st. 185-188.
Foreign currency assets rose US$7.01bn to US$46.56bn (end-January 2002); total reserves were US$49.48bn, about 10 months of imports. External debt was US$100.38bn at end-September 2001 (US$99.61bn at end-March 2001), 21% of GDP; the debt service ratio fell from 35.3% (1990-91) to 16.3% (2000-01); short-term debt was 2.8% of the total. The World Bank's GDF 2001 table (1999 data) ranks India tenth among debtors, with Argentina sixth and Turkey eighth, and classes India as less indebted.
Quote: "for the first time, the World Bank has classified India as a less-indebted country".
**FLAG (internal):** Ch 1 para 1.12 measures the fall in debt/GDP from 28.7% (end-March 1991), para 1.105 from 38.7% (end-March 1992). Both are in Table 6.12; the 1992 figure is the peak. The debt service ratio for 2000-01 is 16.3% in the text but 17.1% in Table 6.3.

**ES01-15 — FDI improving against a poor emerging-market backdrop.** Ch 6, paras 6.47-6.48 and Table 6.10, st. 182.
Total foreign investment was US$5.10bn in 2000-01 (US$5.18bn in 1999-2000, US$2.40bn in 1998-99 after the Asian crisis). In April-November 2001 it rose about 47% to US$3.68bn, mostly because FDI rose 61% to US$2.37bn. FDI was US$2.34bn in 2000-01, below the 1997-98 peak of US$3.56bn. Mauritius and the US were the main sources; computer hardware and software was the leading destination sector.
Quote: "augur well when seen against the background of private capital flows".
**FLAG (definitions):** FDI for 2000-01 is US$2.34bn in Table 6.10 (gross inflows) but US$1.83bn net in BOP Table 6.2. State the basis.

**ES01-16 — Oil: Brent from $10 to $33, then a slump.** Ch 6, paras 6.24 and 6.53, st. 164-165 and 185; Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.7 and 1.69-1.70.
Brent rose from around $10/bbl (February 1999) to around $33/bbl (November 2000) on OPEC cuts and tight supply; oil imports rose from US$6.4bn (1998-99) to US$15.6bn (2000-01), 31% of imports. The RBI sold foreign exchange in the first seven months of 2000-01 to meet the extra demand. After September 2001 crude slumped, to about $19-20/bbl against more than $27 in February 2001. WPI inflation rose from 3.3% (1999-2000) to 7.1% (2000-01) because administered petroleum prices were raised (twice in 2000-01); fuel-group inflation fell from 31.0% to 3.2% by January 2002.
Quote: "near tripling of international oil prices within a year or so".
**FLAG (internal):** last year's fuel-group inflation is 31.0% in Ch 1 para 1.70 but 30.1% in Ch 5 (st. 95).

**ES01-17 — Inflation at a two-decade low; Bank Rate 6.5%, lowest since 1973; CRR 5.5%.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.7-1.8, 1.68-1.71 and 1.74; Ch 3 Monetary and Banking Developments, para 3.9 and Box 3.1, st. 43-44.
Point-to-point WPI inflation was 1.3% in the week ended 19 January 2002. The 52-week average fell from 7.0% at the start of 2001-02 to 4.7%. Manufactured-product prices barely rose, reflecting weak demand. CPI-IW inflation stayed below 4% until July 2001, then ran at about 5% (5.2% in December 2001).
The RBI's April 2001 policy aimed to support credit for growth and keep interest rates stable, preferring softer rates. It assumed GDP growth of 6.0-6.5% and inflation within 5%. Box 3.1: CRR cut from 8.0% to 7.5% (19 May 2001); Bank Rate cut from 7.0% to 6.5% (23 October 2001); CRR cut 200 bps to 5.5% (by 29 December 2001). Para 1.74: the Bank Rate is the lowest since May 1973. The 91-day T-bill yield fell 125 bps to 7.25% (April-December 2001); 10-12 year gilt yields fell from 10.05-10.41% to 8.15-8.35%; major banks' PLRs fell to 11.00-12.00%.
Para 1.133 adds that high real interest rates persisted, leaving a debt overhang for firms that invested in 1994-97.
Quote: "preference for softer interest rates as warranted by the emerging economic and financial conditions".

**ES01-18 — Fiscal deficit: target missed on revenue shortfalls.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.9, 1.56, 1.64 and 1.66.
The Centre's gross fiscal deficit for 2000-01 was 5.1% of GDP in the revised estimates but 5.5% on provisional unaudited figures; the revenue deficit was 3.9%. For 2001-02 the budgeted 4.7% (revenue deficit 3.2%) becomes 5.1% (3.4%) on revised GDP estimates. In April-December 2001 tax revenue (net to Centre) fell about 7%, and excise plus customs collections fell 3.6%. Disinvestment receipts were Rs 280 crore against a full-year target of Rs 12,000 crore. The Survey expects the deficit to exceed the target.
Quote: "is likely to exceed the budgeted target".
See ES02-11: the 2001-02 outcome was 5.9%.

**ES01-19 — The general government deficit at about 10% of GDP; the FRBM Bill.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.108 and 1.112-1.113.
Despite efforts, the Centre's deficit could not be brought below 5% of GDP. With the States, the total is around 10%; combined debt reached 85% of GDP in 2001. The combined deficit was 9.6% in 2000-01. Government borrowing has raised real interest rates for everyone else. The FRBM Bill, introduced in December 2000, would require the Centre to cut its fiscal and revenue deficits to sustainable levels over five years.
Quote: "a situation not dissimilar to that prevailing in the early 1990s".

**ES01-20 — Last QRs removed in April 2001: no import surge.** Ch 1 (HTML), para 1.99; Ch 6, para 6.27 and Table 6.6, st. 166.
The EXIM Policy of March 2001 removed the remaining BOP-related quantitative restrictions on 715 items (342 textiles, 147 farm products, 226 other manufactures including cars) from 1 April 2001. Of the 714 items freed on 31 March 2000, 151 saw no imports at all. Imports of 300 monitored sensitive items rose only 2.1% in April-December 2001. Safeguards included state trading, health and SPS rules, and a 105% duty on used cars.
Quote: "these apprehensions are not borne out by actual import growth".

**ES01-21 — Doha, November 2001.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.100-1.101; Ch 6, paras 6.39 ff and Box 6.6, st. 178-180.
At the Fourth WTO Ministerial (Doha, 9-14 November 2001), India opposed adding investment, competition, government procurement, trade facilitation, environment and labour to the agenda, and pressed for settling implementation issues first. It says it secured an agenda that emphasised development, plus clarity on TRIPS and public health.
Quote: "India was opposed to overburdening of the multilateral trading system".
Not found: China's WTO accession (December 2001) is not mentioned in ES 2001-02, in Ch 1 or Ch 6 (searched "accession", "acceded", "China").

**ES01-22 — China in ES 2001-02.** Ch 6, para 6.4, st. 154-155; Table 6.4, st. 162; paras 6.21 and 6.33, st. 163 and 171; Box 6.5, st. 176; Ch 7, para 7.51, st. 141.
China is the one Asian economy to buck the downturn in 2001, on private consumption and public investment (para 6.4). China's exports grew 27.7% in 2000 and 9.2% in the first half of 2001 (Table 6.4). India-China trade grew about 26% in 2000-01 and the first seven months of 2001-02; exports to China rose 53.9% in 2000-01. The EXIM Policy declared China, Russia and 14 other countries non-market economies, for anti-dumping cost assessment. Ch 7 says foreign investment in electronics hardware is going to Taiwan, China, Brazil and Malaysia rather than India.
Quote: "have bucked the overall depressing trend in developing Asia".

**ES01-23 — Outlook: recovery expected in 2002.** Ch 1 (HTML), paras 1.153-1.154.
The Survey closes by expecting a world recovery in 2002, a revival of Indian exports, and an end to the deflation in commodity and manufactured goods prices, which would restore manufacturing profits. Fiscal imbalance at Centre and States is named as the urgent issue.
Quote: "The year 2001 has been a difficult year for almost all economies".

## Economic Survey 2002-03

**ES02-1 — Growth revised up: 4.4% (2000-01), 5.6% (2001-02); 4.4% expected for 2002-03.** Ch 1 General Review, paras 1.1-1.2, p. 1 (st. 416); Table 1.1, p. 2 (st. 417); Table 1.6, p. 11 (st. 426).
GDP grew 5.6% in 2001-02 (CSO, 31 January 2003), against 5.4% projected a year earlier. 2000-01 is revised to 4.4% from 4.0%. Quarterly GDP grew 6.0% and 5.8% in the first two quarters of 2002-03 (3.5% and 5.3% a year earlier). The monsoon failure cut agriculture by 3.1% (AE, 7 February 2003), so 2002-03 growth is likely to be only 4.4%. Industry is expected to accelerate from 3.3% to 6.1% and services from 6.8% to 7.1%.
Quote: "was stronger than what had been initially anticipated".
**FLAG (revisions):** 2000-01 GDP 4.0 → 4.4; 2001-02 5.4 → 5.6; 2000-01 manufacturing 6.7 → 7.3; 2001-02 services 6.5 → 6.8. ES 2003-04 then revises 2001-02 to 5.8 and 2002-03 to 4.0 (ES03-R1).
**FLAG (internal):** agriculture and allied growth in 2000-01 is −0.4% in Table 1.1 but +0.4% in Table 1.6 (ES 2001-02 had −0.2%).

**ES02-2 — Downside risks: Latin America (Argentina), Turkey, Iraq, the monsoon.** Ch 1, para 1.3, p. 1 (st. 416); Ch 6, para 6.3, st. 104.
The recovery in growth continued despite risks at home and abroad. The world recovery and world trade stayed subdued; international financial flows suffered from unsettled conditions in Latin America and Turkey; the stand-off in Iraq made geopolitics volatile; and the monsoon deficiency was the worst in two decades.
The outlook for Latin America is serious because of the financial crisis in Argentina, its partial spread to Uruguay, Paraguay and Bolivia, and the sharp loss of market confidence in Brazil. Developing Asia, by contrast, recovered faster and more strongly, with domestic demand reviving in China, India and Korea. The Survey does not call it a default (searched).
Argentina also appears in Tables 6.15-6.16 (st. 139-140) as the third-largest debtor by present value of debt in 2000 (US$155bn, severely indebted), while India is tenth and less indebted.
Quote: "partial spillover of the Argentinean crisis to neighbouring economies".

**ES02-3 — The 2002 drought: rainfall 19% below normal, foodgrains down 13.6%, inflation low.** Ch 1, paras 1.4, 1.47-1.48 and 1.58, pp. 1-2, 13, 15 (st. 416-418, 428, 430); Ch 5, Box 5.1 "Drought and inflation", st. 72 (no printed page).
Seasonal rainfall fell 19% short of normal, the most deficient monsoon since 1987-88. Seventeen states had moderate to severe drought. Kharif foodgrain output fell 19%; oilseeds were worst hit. Foodgrain output was projected at 183.2 million tonnes, against 212 mt the year before. Public stocks were more than enough to cover the shortfall, so primary prices showed no inflationary undercurrent. Offtake under welfare schemes rose to 5.48 mt in April-October 2002 (3.15 mt a year earlier).
Past droughts brought high inflation: 25.2% in 1974-75 (with the oil shock) and 17.1% in 1979-80. In 2002-03, with rainfall 19% below normal, WPI inflation was 2.6% (52-week average, week ended 18 January 2003). The Box credits surplus foodgrain stocks and irrigation: 55% of foodgrains now come from irrigated land, against 33% in 1987-88. Para 1.4 notes no shortages; fuel-group inflation, after the move to market pricing of petroleum products, was about 5% for much of the year, reaching 6.4% in mid-January 2003 on Gulf tensions. CPI-IW inflation fell from 4.7% to 3.2% (December 2002).
Quote: "the most deficient monsoon after 1987-88".
**FLAG (revision):** ES 2003-04 puts 2002-03 foodgrains at 174.2 mt (−18.2%) and agriculture at −5.2% (ES03-R1).
**FLAG (definitions):** Table 1.1 gives WPI inflation of 1.6% for 2001-02 and 4.4% for 2002-03 (to 18 January 2003), both end-period changes, not the 52-week average. ES 2003-04 Table 1.1 gives 6.5% for 2002-03 (end-March 2003).

**ES02-4 — Industry revives in 2002-03.** Ch 1, paras 1.34-1.38, pp. 11-12 (st. 426-427).
IIP growth was 2.7% in 2001-02 and 5.3% in April-November 2002, with capital goods up 9.9% and consumer non-durables 12.7%. Steel output grew 19.8% in 2001-02 and 24.5% in April-November 2002, helped by world steel prices rising from December 2001, highway building under NHAI and housing. DIPP data show FDI inflows of Rs 21,286 crore in 2002, against Rs 19,265 crore in 2001.
Quote: "Industry has experienced a significant revival".

**ES02-5 — The world in 2002: a recovery that faded.** Ch 6 External Sector, paras 6.1-6.2 and 6.4, Table 6.1, st. 103-104 (no printed page).
Recovery gathered pace in early 2002 but did not last. Table 6.1 (IMF WEO, September 2002): world output 4.7% (2000), 2.2% (2001), 2.8% (2002), 3.7% (2003). US 3.8%, 0.3%, 2.2%, 2.6%; Japan 2.4%, −0.3%, −0.5%, 1.1%; China 8.0%, 7.3%, 7.5%, 7.2%; India 5.4%, 4.1%, 5.0%, 5.7%. World trade volume 12.6%, −0.1%, 2.1%, 6.1%. The 2003 forecast was cut 0.3 points. Concerns: rising unemployment and fiscal deterioration in advanced economies. Capital flows to emerging markets were still nowhere near pre-East Asian crisis levels.
Quote: "developing Asia remains a rare centre of resurgence in the subdued global economy".
**FLAG (revision):** compare ES01-6 (world 2.4, US 1.0, world trade 1.0 for 2001). The IMF figures for India also differ from the CSO's fiscal-year figures; do not mix them.

**ES02-6 — A current-account surplus for the first time since 1977-78.** Ch 1, paras 1.7 and 1.31, pp. 3 and 10 (st. 418, 425); Ch 6, paras 6.5-6.9, Tables 6.2-6.3, st. 105-107.
In 2001-02 the current account showed a surplus of US$1.35bn, 0.3% of GDP. BOP exports were flat (0.0%), imports fell 2.8% (mainly oil), and the trade deficit fell to US$12.70bn (2.6% of GDP). Net invisibles were US$14.05bn (2.9% of GDP), the highest in a decade. Para 6.7 warns that a surplus built on stagnant exports and falling imports may not last. In April-September 2002 the surplus continued (US$1.67bn) on remittances and software, and four quarters in a row were in surplus. A surplus was expected for 2002-03 as well.
Quote: "may be a temporary feature for the economy".
**FLAG (internal):** Ch 1 (paras 1.7, 1.31) says the surplus came after twenty-three years; Ch 6 para 6.5 says after a gap of 24 years and names 1977-78 as the last surplus. **FLAG (forecast vs outcome):** ES 2001-02 had expected a deficit within 1% of GDP (ES01-13).

**ES02-7 — Exports: flat in 2001-02, then a surge.** Ch 1, paras 1.7 and 1.31, pp. 3 and 10; Ch 6, para 6.14, st. 109; Table 1.1, p. 2 (st. 417).
World merchandise trade volume shrank 1.5% in 2001; the dollar value of world exports fell 3.5%. The Survey blames the slowdown in industrial and East Asian markets, the slump in the technology sector and the 9/11 attacks. India's share of world exports rose slightly to 0.7% in 2001, against 4.3% for China and 2.4% for Korea. In April-December 2002, DGCI&S exports grew 20.4% in dollars (34.3% year-on-year in December 2002), despite the slow world recovery and a slightly stronger rupee; gems and jewellery, textiles, engineering goods, chemicals and ores led.
Quote: "with a major reason being the slump in the technology sector".
**FLAG (revision/definitions):** ES 2001-02 had 2001-02 exports up 0.6% (April-December, DGCI&S). ES 2002-03 gives the full year as −1.6% (DGCI&S, Table 1.1) and 0.0% (BOP, Table 6.3).

**ES02-8 — Software exports: up 13.1% in dollars in 2001-02, Rs 36,500 crore.** Ch 6, para 6.11, st. 108; Ch 7 Industry, paras 7.44-7.45 and Tables 7.19-7.20, st. 161-162; Ch 1, para 1.44, p. 13 (st. 428).
Net non-factor services rose almost 70% to US$4.2bn in 2001-02. Miscellaneous services (professional services, software, agency charges) earned a net US$3.77bn, against US$2.99bn (+26.2%). Software exports alone grew 13.1%, though IT trade had slowed sharply world-wide. Net travel receipts rose to US$628m (US$294m in 2000-01) although tourist arrivals fell 14.5%.
Software exports grew at a compound rate of over 50% a year for five years. Table 7.20: computer software exports Rs 17,150 crore (1999-2000), Rs 28,350 crore (2000-01), Rs 36,500 crore (2001-02); electronics hardware exports Rs 5,871 crore in 2001-02 (+22.6%). Global IT firms were starting to use India in their production chains and for research and development. Para 7.44 credits public investment in human capital, outward orientation and a competitive private industry.
Quote: "marked slowdown in IT trade world over".
**FLAG (cross-Survey growth rates):** software export growth falls from 57% (US$, 2000-01, ES 2001-02) to 13.1% (2001-02). This is the clearest trace in the Surveys of the US tech bust reaching India, but the Survey does not name the bust.
**FLAG (forecast vs outcome):** ES 2001-02 expected Rs 40,000 crore for 2001-02 (ES01-11). **FLAG (definitions):** Table 7.20 implies about 29% growth in rupees (28,350 to 36,500), against 13.1% in dollars in the BOP. The two come from different sources (Department of IT vs RBI BOP) and the rupee fell in the year, so do not set them side by side without saying so (my note; the Survey does not reconcile them).

**ES02-9 — Beyond software: call centres and back offices; tourism after 9/11.** Ch 1, paras 1.8 and 1.40, pp. 3-4 and 12 (st. 418-419, 427).
India's share of world commercial services trade is larger than its share of merchandise trade. Besides software, India is now used for financial accounting, call centres, insurance claims processing and medical transcription. The Survey sees large potential. ("BPO" and "IT-enabled services" as terms do not appear in Ch 1.)
Tourist arrivals fell sharply after 9/11 and other unnamed developments. Many governments issued travel advisories against India, causing group cancellations and few new bookings. There were signs of revival from October 2002.
Quote: "an important venue for many tasks in services".

**ES02-10 — Reserves: US$54.1bn (March 2002) to US$73.6bn (January 2003).** Ch 1, paras 1.6 and 1.9, pp. 3-4 (st. 418-419); Ch 6, para 6.65, st. 136.
Foreign currency assets rose US$11.5bn in 2001-02 to US$51.05bn, US$9.10bn of it in the second half. Total reserves were US$54.11bn at end-March 2002 (US$42.28bn a year earlier) and US$73.58bn on 31 January 2003. An RBI study attributes the accretion to the current-account surplus, non-debt capital and valuation gains. Despite a 3-4 point interest differential, there was no evidence of large debt-capital arbitrage; two-thirds of the rise came from non-debt flows. Net foreign assets reached 100.7% of reserve money on 24 January 2003 (9.1% in March 1991).
Quote: "which is close to a currency board situation".

**ES02-11 — Fiscal: the Centre's deficit rises to 5.9% of GDP; combined 10.0%; crowding out.** Ch 1, paras 1.18-1.19 and 1.74-1.75, pp. 5-6 and 20 (st. 420-421, 435); Ch 2 Public Finance, para 2.8, st. 5.
The Centre's fiscal deficit rose steadily from 4.1% of GDP (1996-97) to 5.6% (2000-01) and an estimated 5.9% in 2001-02, against 4.7% budgeted (5.1% on revised GDP). The primary deficit was 1.4% of GDP in 2001-02. States' combined deficit rose from 2.7% (1996-97) to 4.3% (2000-01) and 4.6% (2001-02 RE). The combined Centre-States deficit was 10.0% of GDP (2001-02 RE). The 2002-03 budget aimed at 5.3% (5.5% on the February 2003 GDP estimates). In April-December 2002 the deficit (Rs 86,269 crore) was slightly below a year earlier, but drought relief and subsidies were expected to add pressure.
The Survey grants that slow growth argues for counter-cyclical policy, but says fiscal consolidation must speed up. The interest rate is still below the growth rate, but high primary deficits are pushing up deficit and debt ratios, and interest, wages and pensions pre-empt much of revenue. Remedies: a computerised, impersonal tax system, user charges, wage and pension restraint, market-linked small-savings rates, and rational prices for food, fertiliser, LPG and kerosene.
Quote: "will crowd out the nascent recovery in private investment".
**FLAG (revisions):** 2000-01 Centre deficit 5.5% (ES 2001-02, provisional) → 5.6%. 2001-02: ES 2001-02's 5.1% estimate → 5.9%. ES 2002-03 para 1.74 gives the general government deficit as 9.5% in 1999-2000; ES 2001-02 gave 9.6% for 2000-01.

**ES02-12 — Kelkar Task Forces and disinvestment.** Ch 2, paras 2.50-2.51 and Boxes 2.3-2.4, st. 27-29; Ch 7, Table 7.22, st. 170; Ch 1, para 1.21, p. 6 (st. 421).
Two task forces on direct and indirect taxes were set up in September 2002 under Dr Vijay L. Kelkar and submitted their recommendations to the Government in December 2002 (exemption limits, fewer exemptions, wider service tax). Disinvestment: Rs 5,632 crore realised in 2001-02 and Rs 3,342 crore in 2002-03 (to 31 January 2003), against targets of Rs 12,000 crore each year; Rs 1,869 crore in 2000-01 against Rs 10,000 crore. The administered price mechanism for petroleum was dismantled on 1 April 2002, and the December 2002 decision on BPCL and HPCL cleared doubts about privatisation (para 1.21).
Quote: "an attempt to demystify the process of budget making".

**ES02-13 — Monetary policy: Bank Rate 6.25%, CRR 4.75%, a soft-rate regime.** Ch 1, para 1.12, p. 4 (st. 419); Ch 3, paras 3.13-3.14, st. 39.
The RBI cut the Bank Rate 25 bps to 6.25% in October 2002, the lowest since 1973. The CRR went to 5.0% from 1 June 2002 and to 4.75% from 16 November 2002. Major banks' PLRs fell from 11.00-12.00% to 10.75-11.50%, and sub-PLR lending spread. The 12-year government bond yield hit 6.13% on 31 December 2002. The RBI's mid-term review cut its 2002-03 growth projection from 6.0-6.5% to 5.0-5.5% because of the drought. Para 1.30: the real PLR fell only from 9.6% (1997) to 9.0% (January 2003).
Quote: "with a preference for soft interest rate regime under normal circumstances".

**ES02-14 — Capital flows: FDI up, FIIs out in 2002.** Ch 6, para 6.8 and Tables 6.2, 6.10-6.12, st. 106-107 and 130-131; Ch 1, para 1.14, p. 5 (st. 420).
Net capital flows rose from US$8.41bn (2000-01) to US$10.41bn (2001-02). Net foreign investment was US$5.29bn, the highest in four years: net FDI US$3.27bn (BOP), FII US$1.50bn. Gross FDI inflows were US$3.90bn in 2001-02 (Table 6.10). In 2002, by contrast, there was a small net FII outflow (−US$388m in April-October 2002). UNCTAD data (Table 6.12) give FDI in 2001 as US$46.8bn for China and US$3.4bn for India.
Quote: "indicating the rising confidence of international investors in Indian economy".

**ES02-15 — Rupee: an all-time low in May 2002, then a weak dollar.** Ch 1, para 1.5, p. 3 (st. 418); Ch 6, paras 6.66-6.67, st. 137.
In 2001-02 the rupee moved within Rs 46.56-48.85 per dollar; the monthly average fell 4.0%. RBI action after 9/11 calmed the market. It hit Rs 49.06 in May 2002, then strengthened to Rs 47.80, 2.1% above the end-March 2002 level. Against sterling, the euro and the yen it fell 8.9%, 14.9% and 7.4% (April 2002-January 2003), partly because the dollar was weakening. NEER fell 4.6% and REER 2.1% in April-November 2002.
Quote: "the weakening of the US dollar against these currencies".
**FLAG (internal):** Ch 1 dates the Rs 47.80 level to end-December 2002, Ch 6 to end-January 2003, with the same 2.1% appreciation. Table 1.1 gives 2001-02 depreciation as 4.2% (annual average), the text 4.0% (monthly average).

**ES02-16 — External debt: US$98.5bn, ninth-largest debtor, less indebted.** Ch 6, paras 6.68-6.71, Tables 6.14-6.16, st. 138-140.
External debt was US$98.49bn at end-March 2002 and US$101.97bn at end-September 2002; debt/GDP 20.9% (March 2002); debt service 13.8% of current receipts (2001-02); short-term debt 2.8% of the total. GDF 2002 classes India as less indebted in 1999 and 2000 (moderate in 1998). India is ninth by total debt in 2000, after Brazil, Russia, Mexico, China, Argentina, Indonesia, Korea and Turkey. High-cost loans of US$1,145m were prepaid by end-December 2002.
Quote: "India continued to enjoy the highest proportion of concessional loans".
**FLAG (revision):** end-March 2001 debt is US$99.61bn in ES 2001-02 but US$101.13bn (revised) here; debt/GDP for 2000-01 22.3% → 22.4%. The 2000-01 debt service ratio is 17.3% in Table 6.3 and 15.4% in Table 6.14 (the table note says the methods differ).

**ES02-17 — WTO and China: a new member, a benchmark and a competitor.** Ch 6, paras 6.10, 6.14, 6.33, 6.39-6.41 and Box 6.3, st. 108-109, 117-118, 122-124; Ch 1, para 1.60, p. 16 (st. 431).
On the Doha agriculture negotiations the Survey lists country groups. One consists of newly acceded countries such as China and Croatia, which want no tariff cuts for themselves but large market access from others. This is the only reference to China's accession found in either Survey. Box 6.3 reports India's third WTO Trade Policy Review: members praised the reforms and the software sector but urged lower farm and textile protection.
The Tenth Plan (2002-07) targets 8% growth a year. China is cited as proof that this is feasible: it kept growth above 7% despite the global slowdown. India-China trade grew 28.1% in 2001-02 and 43.4% in April-October 2002; China rose to India's sixth-largest trading partner. India's remittances are about twice China's. Shares of world exports in 2001: India 0.7%, China 4.3%.
Quote: "newly acceded countries (like China and Croatia)".

**ES02-18 — Consumption-led growth; saving above investment in 2001-02.** Ch 1, paras 1.22-1.26, pp. 6-8 (st. 421-423), Tables 1.2-1.4.
With a faltering world recovery, private consumption kept growth going: it rose 5.9% in real terms in 2001-02, against 5.6% for GDP. Gross domestic saving was 24.0% of GDP and gross domestic investment 23.7%, so the saving-investment gap turned positive (+0.2%) for the first time in the table. Real GDCF grew only 3.0%; private corporate investment was 5.7% of GDP at 1993-94 prices (Table 1.4). The public sector's dissaving rose by nearly Rs 10,000 crore.
Quote: "private final consumption expenditure has been the major factor sustaining growth".
**FLAG (revision):** ES 2003-04 puts 2001-02 gross domestic saving at 23.5% (see ES03-7 in `India_ES_2003-2008.md`).

**ES02-19 — Equity markets: India flat while the S&P and Nikkei fell.** Ch 1, para 1.14, p. 5 (st. 420); Ch 4, paras 4.4-4.6 and Tables 4.1-4.3, st. 58-59.
Nifty was 1,087 in January 2002 and 1,073 in January 2003. From December 2001 to December 2002 Nifty rose 1.6%, while the S&P 500 fell 23.4%, the Nikkei 24.5% and Shanghai A 17.1%. The two shocks named for the Indian market are the March 2001 misconduct episode and the WTC attack. NSE and BSE rank third and sixth in the world by number of trades in 2001, after NASDAQ and NYSE.
Quote: "smaller than that in many other countries".

**ES02-20 — Outlook: resilience proven; reserves as a cushion for a Gulf war.** Ch 6, para 6.73, st. 141; Ch 1, para 1.77, p. 21 (st. 436).
Para 6.73 lists the shocks absorbed: the Asian crisis of 1997, the crude oil shock of 2000 and the synchronized global slowdown of 2001. Reserves are a cushion. Para 1.77: with Gulf tensions and rising oil prices, reserves above US$73bn make India one of the top reserve-holding emerging markets, able to pay higher oil bills. Revived domestic demand means growth is unlikely to be seriously hurt.
Quote: "resilience of India’s performance has been vindicated by its successfully handling".

## Problems and caveats

1. **Pages.** ES 2001-02 Ch 1 exists only as HTML, so it has no pages; I cite paragraphs. The ES 2001-02 PDF and every ES 2002-03 chapter except Ch 1 carry no printed page numbers in the extracts, so those citations are stitched pages only. Only ES 2002-03 Ch 1 (p. = st. − 415) and ES 2003-04 Ch 1 (p. = st. − 612) have printed pages.
2. **No ES 2000-01.** The Survey written during the Nasdaq fall and the oil shock (February 2001) is not available. Do not attribute any 2000-01 view to it.
3. **GDP vintages differ.** 2000-01 growth is 4.0 (ES01) or 4.4 (ES02); 2001-02 is 5.4, 5.6 or 5.8; 2002-03 is 4.4 or 4.0. Name the vintage when quoting a number.
4. **Internal inconsistencies.** Details are in the items.
   - ES 2001-02: 2000-01 current-account gap US$2.58bn (Ch 1) vs US$2.68bn (Ch 6; Table 6.2 supports 2.58) (ES01-12). 2000-01 software exports US$6.3bn (Ch 1, Ch 6) vs US$6.2bn (Ch 7) (ES01-11). Debt service ratio 2000-01 16.3% (text) vs 17.1% (Table 6.3) (ES01-14). FDI 2000-01 US$2.34bn gross vs US$1.83bn BOP net (ES01-15).
   - ES 2002-03: surplus after twenty-three years (Ch 1) vs after 24 years, since 1977-78 (Ch 6) (ES02-6). Agriculture 2000-01 −0.4% (Table 1.1) vs +0.4% (Table 1.6) (ES02-1). Rupee at Rs 47.80 dated end-December 2002 (Ch 1) vs end-January 2003 (Ch 6); 2001-02 depreciation 4.0% vs 4.2% (ES02-15). Table 1.1 prints foreign currency assets for 2001-02 as "510,49" (a typo for 51,049, US$ million).
5. **Forecast vs outcome.** ES 2001-02 expected a wider current-account deficit in 2001-02 and got a surplus. It expected Rs 40,000 crore of software exports and got Rs 36,500 crore. It expected a 2002 world recovery that ES 2002-03 says faded. The Centre's 2001-02 deficit estimate went from 5.1% to 5.9%.
6. **The tech bust is visible only indirectly.** Neither Survey uses "dot-com", "Y2K" or "bubble", and the Nasdaq appears only as a chart legend (ES01 Fig. 4.2) and in a table of exchanges (ES02 Table 4.1). The traces are software export growth falling from 57% to 13.1% in dollars, the downturn in IT named as an export risk (ES01-10), and the technology slump blamed for the fall in world trade (ES02-7). Any link between the Nasdaq and India in the book must be made by the author, not attributed to the Surveys.
