### MK-IMF-1. IMF, Global Financial Stability Report October 2025, Chapter 1 "Shifting Ground beneath the Calm: Stability Challenges amid Changes in Financial Markets", 14 October 2025
Issue page: https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/GFSR/Issues/2025/10/14/global-financial-stability-report-october-2025 (title "Global
Financial Stability Report, October 2025: Shifting Ground beneath the Calm"; from the GFSR listing https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/GFSR).
Chapter PDF: https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2025/october/english/ch1.pdf (46 PDF pages; printed page = PDF page).
Read: pp. 1–8 (at a glance, introduction, asset prices, equity valuations); searched the rest for NBFI, outflows, stablecoins.
- p. 1 (at a glance): risk asset prices kept rising and the dollar fell; asset prices "have returned to stretched valuations" despite trade
  and geopolitical tensions; valuation models put risk asset prices "well above fundamentals", raising the chance of disorderly
  corrections. NBFIs and stablecoins kept growing; bank–NBFI interconnectedness and maturity mismatches could amplify shocks.
- p. 1: after a "brief jolt" from the US tariff announcements of 2 April, markets brushed off later shocks; financial conditions eased
  back to accommodative after the abrupt tightening of early April. The calm "masks a degree of complacency"; the US dollar had
  depreciated by 10 percent so far in 2025 (pp. 1–2).
- p. 2: rebound since April helped by the 90-day tariff pause announced a week after 2 April; buyers stepped in believing tariff effects
  would be temporary. Tariffs settling "at their highest levels in almost a century".
- p. 4: equity sentiment buoyed by mega-cap IT and AI stocks; concentration of valuations in a handful of firms (the Magnificent 7:
  Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia, Tesla, fn 6) "is at historical highs".
- p. 6: the S&P 500 12-month forward P/E ratio was back to about the 96th percentile since 1990.
- p. 8: IMF models put the fair-value forward P/E at about the 81st historical percentile, so US equities overvalued by about 10
  percentage points; overvaluation was higher in past episodes such as the dot-com bubble. Concentration is the bigger worry: IT is 35
  percent of the S&P 500, similar to the dot-com peak, but the Magnificent 7 alone are 33 percent; a Herfindahl-based concentration
  measure is "substantially higher than during the dot-com bubble". Normalised concentration in the S&P 500 rose about 20 points in a
  decade, far more than in other countries' benchmarks. If mega-caps fail to earn returns that justify AI investment, a "sudden, sharp
  correction" is possible. Fn 13: investment in information-processing equipment (proxy for AI data centres) contributed around 57
  percent of US real GDP growth since 2024 Q4.

Read: pp. 1–3, p. 16 (EM flows), Box 1.1 pp. 36–37 (DeepSeek); searched the rest.
- p. 1: the October 2024 GFSR had flagged stretched valuations, especially tech stocks, and rising NBFI leverage. Tariff policy uncertainty
  began in February 2025; the "surprise magnitude" of tariffs announced on 2 April moved expectations towards lower growth; stock markets
  highly volatile, EM currencies depreciated, corporate spreads widened. Growth-at-Risk: a 5 percent chance that global growth falls
  below 0.4 percent in the year ahead, nearly a full point worse than in October 2024; risks "have increased significantly".
- p. 2: US equities had outperformed peers for five years, then fell from February; the sell-off "sped up violently" and became worldwide
  after 2 April; markets regained footing after the 9 April postponement of higher tariffs. Fn 1: a targeted tech sell-off in January
  followed DeepSeek's lower-cost AI model (Box 1.1: announced 27 January 2025, p. 36). After 2 April long-term yields first fell
  (safe-haven) then rose strongly within a couple of days.
- pp. 2–3: the Treasury yield rise came from a preference for cash and short assets, a gyrating dollar and the unwinding of leveraged trades
  (swap-spread and cash-futures basis trades); dealers reportedly hit intermediation limits. Even after the sell-off US stocks were at
  about the 80th historical percentile of forward P/E ratios since 1990 (p. 2).
- p. 16: EM currencies depreciated against the dollar while other AE currencies rose; IMF "capital flows at risk": with a 5 percent chance
  EM capital outflows could reach 1.6 percent of GDP over the next year, 1.9 percent if the dollar rises and US equities sell off further.

- p. xi: stability risks elevated with the war in the Middle East; markets "have corrected in an orderly manner so far"; since February
  global equity prices fell 8 percent after being lifted by strong profits; sovereign yields rose sharply on inflation expectations; EM
  assets hit by a stronger dollar and energy prices.
- p. xi: three amplifiers. (1) bond yields gyrate more on auction days as debt rises and price-sensitive investors grow. (2) EMs face
  currency and outflow pressure as carry trades unwind; EM asset valuations had reached their highest in more than a decade before the
  conflict, but capital flows took a "K-shaped pattern", skewed towards debt inflows and carry trades rather than FDI (Figure ES.3:
  net portfolio bond flows up, net FDI and net portfolio equity down relative to 2022 Q4). Nonresident nonbank investors in EMs are more
  sensitive to global risk than residents (Figure ES.4).
- p. xii: (3) forced selling by hedge funds, option sellers and leveraged ETFs; options selling by premium-seeking investors has
  "suppressed volatility" but could amplify swings.

### MK-IMF-4. IMF, Global Financial Stability Report April 2020, "Markets in the Time of COVID-19": Chapter 1 "Global Financial Stability Overview" and Chapter 3 "Emerging and Frontier Markets: Managing Volatile Portfolio Flows", 14 April 2020
Issue page: https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/GFSR/Issues/2020/04/14/global-financial-stability-report-april-2020 (title "Global
Financial Stability Report: Markets in the Time of COVID-19"). PDFs: https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2020/april/english/ch1.pdf
(28 pp., printed pp. 1–28) and https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/gfsr/2020/april/english/ch3.pdf (20 pp., printed pp.
47–66; ch3 PDF page n = printed page 46+n). Read: ch1 pp. 1–6, 9–10, 17; ch3 pp. 47–48, 57; searched the rest.
- ch1 p. 1: "Equity markets experienced the fastest drop in history", the S&P 500 falling 20 percent from its peak in just 16 trading
  sessions; declines at the worst point about half those of 2008–09; investors "rushed for safety and liquidity"; global growth then
  expected to fall 3 percent in 2020. "Emerging and frontier markets have experienced a record portfolio flow reversal."
- ch1 pp. 4–6: the US commercial paper market froze as prime money market funds sold paper to raise cash and dealers, facing balance-sheet
  limits, could not intermediate; strains spilled into FX funding; swap lines enhanced and extended to several EMs. Leveraged investors
  (volatility-targeting funds, Treasury cash-futures basis trades, fn 2 p. 6) were forced to unwind; Treasury market liquidity
  "deteriorated sharply"; the Fed scaled up purchases and repo operations.
- ch1 p. 9: nonresident portfolio outflows from EMs a record in dollar terms (more than $100 billion since 21 January) and the highest
  ever relative to GDP in 2020 Q1; outflows initially strongest from Asia and equities. p. 17: bank equity prices fell about 35 percent on
  average since mid-January, up to 60 percent in some countries.
- ch3 p. 47: outflows over $100 billion equal 3½ percent of asset holdings; volatility of nonresident equity and local-bond flows reached
  unprecedented levels. Global financial conditions influence portfolio flows more in surges and reversals than in normal times; strong
  fundamentals "do not always" attract surges but help mitigate outflows; foreign participation in shallow local bond markets raises yield
  volatility. Policy: reserves to curb excess volatility, capital flow management measures, prepare for funding disruptions.
- ch3 p. 48: foreign participation in EM and frontier markets grew significantly in the decade after the global financial crisis, aided by
  AE monetary easing; Figure 3.1 compares the COVID reversal with the taper tantrum (2013) and China's FX depreciation (2015).
  p. 57: for 80 percent of sample countries there is now no gap between local and foreign currency ratings (50 percent at the GFC, 20
  percent in the Asian crisis): a weaker gain from escaping "original sin" than it looks.

  investor base of global capital flows. Low interest rates and low volatility pushed investors into higher-yielding assets (the carry

  "volatility shock"; carry trades and leverage suggest markets expect low volatility to continue. Ch. II: cross-border claims have
  grown in size and relative to GDP; for some countries inflows fed rapid credit growth and asset price inflation. Policy: develop local
  debt markets; liberalising capital outflows by domestic investors "may help balance" inflows.

### MK-IMF-8. IMF, "The Liberalization and Management of Capital Flows: An Institutional View", 14 November 2012
PDF: https://www.imf.org/external/np/pp/eng/2012/111412.pdf (URL GUESSED from the old policy-papers path; returned 200, a 48-page PDF
with the title and date as printed). Listing page not found (https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/Policy-Papers returned HTTP 404).
Read: Executive Summary pp. 1–2; paras. 22–23 (p. 15); CFM design criteria (p. 20); searched the rest.
- p. 1: capital flows have "increased significantly in recent years"; benefits (efficiency, financial competition, productive investment,
  consumption smoothing) but risks magnified by gaps in financial and institutional infrastructure. Liberalisation works best after
  "thresholds" of financial and institutional development and must be planned, timed and sequenced; "no presumption that full
  liberalization is an appropriate goal for all countries at all times". The 2011 IMFC had called for a "comprehensive, flexible, and
  balanced approach".
- pp. 1–2: inflow surges and disruptive outflows: first line is macroeconomic policy (monetary, fiscal, exchange rate), supervision and
  institutions; "In certain circumstances, capital flow management measures can be useful", but they should not substitute for warranted
  macroeconomic adjustment. Both source and recipient countries have responsibilities.
- p. 15: temporary re-imposition of CFMs during an inflow surge or outflow crisis is "consistent with" a liberalisation strategy, but
  should be limited and temporary (moral hazard, investor confidence).
- p. 20: CFMs should be "transparent, targeted, temporary, and preferably non-discriminatory" (between residents and nonresidents).

### MK-OTH-1. Hélène Rey, "Dilemma not Trilemma: The Global Financial Cycle and Monetary Policy Independence", in *Global Dimensions of Unconventional Monetary Policy*, Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium, August 2013
Listing: https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/jackson-hole-economic-symposium/economic-symposium-conference-proceedings/ → 2013 page
https://www.kansascityfed.org/research/jackson-hole-economic-symposium/global-dimensions-unconventional-monetary-policy/ (both 200;
the variant .../global-dimensions-of-unconventional-monetary-policy/ returned 404). PDF: https://www.kansascityfed.org/documents/4575/2013Rey.pdf
(50 PDF pages; printed pp. 285–334; printed = PDF + 284). Read: pp. 285–287 (introduction), p. 298 (global factor), p. 306 (VAR), pp.
317–319 (policy, conclusion); figure pages skimmed.
- p. 285: financial openness looks like an "irresistible long-run trend"; the textbook "trilemma" says floating exchange rates give
  monetary independence; Rey asks whether global banks and financial globalisation undermine even that.
- p. 286: risky asset prices worldwide, from stocks to corporate bonds, "have a strong common component", and so do capital flows; credit
  flows are particularly procyclical; global cycles bring surges and retrenchments in flows, asset booms and busts, crises; linked to US
  monetary conditions and to risk aversion (VIX). Welfare gains from capital flows look "elusive".
- p. 287: the findings turn the trilemma into a "dilemma", an "irreconcilable duo": independent monetary policy is possible "if and only
  if the capital account is managed", directly or via macroprudential policy.
- p. 298: in 858 risky asset prices across five continents, one global factor explains about 25 percent of the variance of returns
  (Miranda-Agrippino and Rey 2012). Capital inflows are larger when the VIX is low (pp. 295–297, quarterly data 1990–2012).
- p. 306: VAR: a lower federal funds rate is followed by a lower VIX (after about five quarters), higher European bank leverage and higher
  gross credit flows.
- pp. 318–319: international monetary cooperation "seems out of reach"; best answers: macroprudential policy guided by aggressive stress
  tests plus tougher leverage ratios; capital controls as a partial substitute "should not be discarded".

### MK-OTH-2. Ben S. Bernanke, "The Global Saving Glut and the U.S. Current Account Deficit", Sandridge Lecture, Virginia Association of Economists, Richmond, Virginia, 10 March 2005 (Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System)
URL: https://www.federalreserve.gov/boarddocs/speeches/2005/200503102/default.htm (HTML, 200; URL from the Fed's speech archive path,
not a listing page I fetched; flagged as recalled). Read: whole speech (HTML; no page numbers).
- The US current account deficit was $635 billion at an annual rate in the first three quarters of 2004, about 5½ percent of GDP, up from
  $120 billion (1.5 percent) in 1996 and $414 billion (4.2 percent) in 2000.
- Thesis: over the past decade diverse forces created "a global saving glut", which helps explain the US deficit and low long-term
  interest rates. Developing countries' current accounts moved from a deficit of $88 billion to a surplus of $205 billion between 1996
  and 2003; they went from borrowers to "large net lenders" on world capital markets.
- Cause: the financial crises of Mexico 1994, East Asia 1997–98, Russia 1998, Brazil 1999 and Argentina 2002; afterwards Korea,
  Thailand and later China built foreign-exchange reserves as "war chests" against outflows and to hold down exchange rates, by issuing
  domestic debt and buying US assets.
- The pull of the US during the 1990s technology boom and its deep financial markets mattered; the glut first raised US equity values in
  the stock-market boom, and in recent years its main asset-price effect was in housing (low mortgage rates, record construction).

## Synthesis: stock markets and capital flows, 2000–2026

1. **Valuations: two bubbles' worth of heights, and a market narrower than in 2000.** Shiller's CAPE peaked at a record 44.2 in December
   1999, was 27.3 at the October 2007 top, fell to 13.3 in March 2009, and stood at 39.4 in November 2026, against a long-run median near
   16.6 (MK-OTH-8). The IMF's model-based view is less alarming on the level: in October 2025 it put the S&P 500 about 10 points of
   P/E percentile above fair value, smaller than in the dot-com bubble, but called concentration the bigger worry: the Magnificent 7 are
   33 percent of the index and Herfindahl concentration is "substantially higher than during the dot-com bubble" (MK-IMF-1 p. 8). The
   Magnificent 7 share rose from 20 to 30 percent in 2024 alone (MK-IMF-5 p. 10). In 2026 the Fed reports P/E well above median and an
   equity premium near the low end of its range, driven by "market enthusiasm for super intelligence (SI)" (MK-OTH-3 p. 2), and AI-related
   debt issuance of over $680 billion in 2026 (MK-OTH-3 p. 23); the IMF flags circular financing among AI firms (MK-IMF-3b pp. 1, 23).
   **Disagreement/nuance:** the CAPE (trailing ten-year real earnings) says US stocks are near their dot-com extreme; the IMF's
   forward-earnings fair-value models say overvaluation is "well below historical peaks" (MK-IMF-3b p. 10). The book should say which
   measure it uses.
2. **The shocks, in sequence, and what amplified them.** Before 2007 the IMF saw subprime fallout as possibly "contained" while warning of
   carry trades and low volatility (MK-IMF-6 pp. ix–x); by October 2008 deleveraging had become "disorderly", with $1.4 trillion of
   expected US losses (MK-IMF-7 pp. xi, xiii). The 2013 taper tantrum: US long yields up 100 basis points by early July, EME equities down
   16 percent, the rupee among five currencies down over 10 percent, India losing over $10 billion of reserves (MK-OTH-7 pp. 24–28). March
   2020: the fastest drop in history (S&P 500 −20 percent in 16 sessions) and a record EM portfolio outflow of over $100 billion (MK-IMF-4
   ch1 pp. 1, 9); money funds and the Treasury market needed Fed backstops (MK-OTH-5). April 2025: the tariff sell-off, with the dollar
   unusually falling in a risk-off episode (MK-OTH-6 p. 12; MK-IMF-2 p. 2). Spring 2026: the Middle East war, global equities −8 percent,
   so far orderly (MK-IMF-3). Common amplifiers in 2020, 2024 and 2025: leveraged nonbank trades (Treasury basis trades, yen carry trade)
   (MK-IMF-4 ch1 p. 6; MK-IMF-5 p. 10; MK-IMF-2 p. 3).
3. **Why capital flows move: saving glut versus global financial cycle.** Bernanke (2005) explained the US deficit and low long rates by
   net saving from emerging economies that, after the 1990s crises, became "large net lenders" and built reserve "war chests" (MK-OTH-2).
   Rey (2013) shifted attention to gross flows and risk appetite: one global factor drives about a quarter of risky-asset returns, US
   monetary policy moves the VIX and bank leverage, and the trilemma becomes a "dilemma" (MK-OTH-1 pp. 287, 298, 306). The IMF's 2020
   finding that global conditions dominate flows in surges and reversals (MK-IMF-4 ch3 p. 47) and the BIS's 2025 finding of stronger,
   "multi-directional" transmission (MK-OTH-6 p. 47) sit closer to Rey. **Disagreement:** these are complementary rather than opposed, but
   they imply different policy: Bernanke's story points to global imbalances; Rey's to macroprudential policy and, if needed, capital
   controls.
4. **The IMF's position on capital controls moved, but only partly.** In 2012 it said there is "no presumption that full liberalization is
   an appropriate goal" for every country and accepted temporary, targeted CFMs that do not replace macro adjustment (MK-IMF-8 pp. 1, 15,
   20). In 2022 it allowed CFM/MPMs on debt inflows pre-emptively against currency mismatches, without a surge (MK-IMF-9 PDF 2, 5), and
   in 2026 it points EMs to its Integrated Policy Framework for FX intervention and CFMs (MK-IMF-3b p. 2). Rey goes further: capital
   controls "should not be discarded" as a partial substitute (MK-OTH-1 p. 319). WEO April 2016 ch. 2 was not obtained.
5. **Structural change: from banks to funds, and from FDI to bond carry.** NBFI assets rose from 167 to 224 percent of world GDP in
   2009–2023 while banks' barely moved (MK-OTH-6 p. 48). EM inflows are "K-shaped", skewed to debt and carry trades, with net FDI falling
   from its post-pandemic peak and concentrated in a few countries (25 of 101 EMs took over 90 percent of FDI in 2022–24) (MK-IMF-3 p. xi;
   MK-IMF-3b pp. 15–16). Foreign holders' gains on US equities reached nearly $6 trillion, exporting any US correction through wealth
   effects (MK-IMF-3b p. 11).
6. **India, part 1: from managing FII surges to a domestically owned market.** In 2007-08 inflows pushed India to "the limits of its
   absorptive capacity", met with intervention and sterilisation through the MSS, ECB end-use limits, and a "gradual, sequenced" path to
   convertibility (MK-IN-10 pp. 109, 128). FIIs pulled $15 billion in 2008-09 (MK-IN-11 p. 134); the 2013 taper hit the rupee and reserves
   (MK-OTH-7). The FPI regime was rebuilt in 2014 and 2019 (single-investor holdings below 10 percent of a company; MK-IN-9 pp. 16, 30).
   After 2020 came a retail wave: demat accounts 18.5 crore (December 2024), over 21.6 crore (December 2025), 22.5 crore (March 2026);
   unique investors over 12 crore; SIPs as the channel (MK-IN-6 p. 55; MK-IN-5 pp. 116–118; MK-IN-4 p. 3). DIIs overtook FIIs in holdings
   in Q4 FY25 (MK-IN-5 p. 129); in 2025-26 India had its "highest-ever FPI net equity outflows" ($19.7 billion) yet DIIs put in a record
   ₹8.5 lakh crore and FPI ownership fell to a 15-year low of 15.8 percent (MK-IN-4 pp. 2, 23, 48). Market cap to GDP: 77 percent (FY19),
   124 (FY24), 136 (December 2024), 130.1 (March 2025), 118.8 (March 2026) (MK-IN-7 p. 65; MK-IN-6 p. 55; MK-IN-4 p. 3).
7. **India, part 2: the costs and the warnings.** 91.1 percent of individual F&O traders lost money in FY24; ₹1.81 lakh crore of losses in
   FY22–FY24; FPI and proprietary algorithmic traders took the profits (MK-IN-1 p. 2). After SEBI's November 2024 measures, losers fell to
   87.7 percent and losses to ₹91,685 crore in FY26, with traders fewer but losing more each (MK-IN-2 pp. 5, 8). The 2023-24 Survey warned
   India "can ill-afford the economy's over financialisation" (MK-IN-7 p. 37); the 2024-25 and 2025-26 Surveys celebrate participation as
   wealth creation (MK-IN-6 pp. 58–59; MK-IN-5 p. 117). Net FDI nearly vanished (US$0.48 billion in April–November 2024) as repatriation
   rose, which the Survey called "a success story as foreigners take profits" (MK-IN-6 p. 108); it recovered to $5.6 billion in April–
   November 2025 and $13.8 billion in April–August 2026 (MK-IN-5 p. 178; MK-IN-8). In June–August 2026 the RBI's special FCNR(B) swap
   window drew $133 billion of deposits (MK-IN-8), an echo of crisis-time deposit schemes.
   **Disagreements and data cautions:** (a) Tone: ES 2023-24 versus ES 2024-25/2025-26 (above). (b) SIP figures: "over Rs 28,000 crore"
   monthly SIP flows (ES, gross, FY26 April–November; MK-IN-5 p. 118) versus a record average *net* monthly SIP contribution of
   ₹16,413 crore (SEBI AR, FY26; MK-IN-4 p. 4) — different measures, do not mix. (c) Ownership shares differ by date and base: FPI 16.9
   percent and DII 18.7 percent (NSE-listed, September 2025; ES), DII 18.3 vs FII 16.7 by value (Q2 FY26; ES), FPI 15.8 and DII 17 percent
   (March 2026; SEBI AR). (d) Household direct equity: the ES 2024-25 says individuals were net buyers of ₹1.5 lakh crore in NSE cash in
   January–November 2024 (MK-IN-6 p. 59), but SEBI's household-savings paper shows households as net *sellers* of listed equity in the
   secondary market in every year 2022-23 to 2024-25 (−₹54,786 crore in 2024-25; MK-IN-3 p. 6). The definitions (individuals on NSE vs
   households across exchanges; calendar vs fiscal year) differ; check before using either. (e) Net FDI: the Survey reads repatriation as
   success; the IMF reads falling EM net FDI as a loss of durable financing (MK-IMF-3b p. 15).
