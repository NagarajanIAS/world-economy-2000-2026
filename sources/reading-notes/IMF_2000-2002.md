# Reading notes: IMF WEO May 2000 – September 2002 (read 22 Nov 2026)

Method as in IMF_2003-2006.md: each PDF was fetched inside an imf.org page in the built-in browser and
its text streams decoded there. Page numbers come from the printed running heads; "p. ?" means the
decoded text carried no readable running head near the passage. The 2000 PDFs decode with spacing
artefacts ("impr oved"), so nothing from them is quoted, only paraphrased.
Quotes are under 15 words; everything else is paraphrase.
PDF base: https://www.imf.org/-/media/websites/imf/imported-flagship-issues/external/pubs/ft/weo/YYYY/0N/pdf/
Files read: _chapter1pdf.pdf for 2000/01, 2000/02, 2001/01, 2001/02, 2001/03, 2002/01, 2002/02, plus front matter where it decoded.

## WEO May 2000 — *Asset Prices and the Business Cycle*
- Ch. 1: global conditions improved dramatically; the US expansion became the longest on record.
- p. 8: very high equity valuations, especially technology and biotech stocks, posed the risk of a large market correction.
- p. 6: downside scenario assumes a 25% fall in US equities and an almost 20% fall in the dollar.
- Box 4.2 "India: Reinvigorating the Reform Process", Ch. 4 p. 117 (title from the contents; box not read).

## WEO October 2000 — *Focus on Transition Economies*
- Ch. 1: higher oil prices an increasing concern; US equity valuations still high.
- p. 3: oil price averaged $17.98 a barrel in 1999; assumed $26.53 in 2000.

## WEO May 2001 — *Fiscal Policy and Macroeconomic Stability* (title from the issue index page)
- Ch. 1: global growth weakened significantly; 2001 projection 3.2%, 1 point lower than before.
- The Fed's rapid policy response; a reasonable prospect that the slowdown would be short-lived.

## WEO October 2001 — *The Information Technology Revolution*
- Ch. 1: 2001 world growth 2.6%; Japan in its fourth recession of the decade.
- Box 1.1 on the terrorist attacks (finalised just after 11 September).
- p. 16: hopes for launching a new WTO round at Doha. p. 34: China's impending WTO entry.

## WEO December 2001 — *The Global Economy After September 11*
- Ch. 1: the attacks exacerbated an already very difficult situation; projections cut again.
- China, and to a lesser extent India, less exposed to the global slowdown.
- Situation in Argentina described as precarious.
- p. ?: oil prices weakened sharply on weaker demand and OPEC coordination difficulties; this would support activity.

## WEO April 2002 — *Recessions and Recoveries*
- Ch. 1: the slowdown "has bottomed out"; the September 11 impact proved short-lived.
- p. 3: contagion from Argentina limited because the crisis was well anticipated; a sharp output decline in Argentina "appears unavoidable".
- China's WTO entry would boost its imports; China and India, relatively closed, less affected by global downturns.
- Ch. 3 theme: recessions in industrial countries have become milder.

## WEO September 2002 — *Trade and Finance*
- p. 2: equity markets fell sharply since end-March, partly on accounting and governance scandals.
- p. 10: late-1990s "irrational exuberance" in valuations may now be largely eliminated; scandals hurt confidence.
- p. 5: recovery in Asia stronger, notably China, India and Korea.
- Global imbalances (the US current account deficit) flagged as a serious medium-term risk.

## India threads for Phase 1
- India's 2001 growth forecast fell from 6.5 (Oct 2000) to 4.1 (Sep 2002); the IMF saw India as relatively insulated by its low openness (Dec 2001, Apr 2002).
- Asia's recovery in 2002 led by China, India and Korea (Sep 2002, p. 5).

## IMF Independent Evaluation Office, *Report on the Evaluation of the Role of the IMF in Argentina, 1991–2001* (July 2004)
Read: Executive Summary (pdf/exesum.pdf), pp. 3–6. The full report PDF did not decode.
- p. 3: among the most severe recent currency crises. In December 2001, in a third year of recession, Argentina defaulted on its sovereign debt; in early January 2002 it abandoned the convertibility regime that had pegged the peso at parity with the dollar since 1991.
- p. 3: convertibility "enormously successful" at achieving price stability quickly; after the Brazilian devaluation of early 1999 staff began to doubt the peg, but did not raise it with the authorities.
- pp. 3–4: election-driven spending in 1999; public debt rose steadily; the IMF's weak enforcement of fiscal conditionality was its "more critical error"; deficit targets missed every year from 1994.
- p. 4: Argentina lost access to voluntary financing in autumn 2000; January–September 2001 the IMF raised its commitments to $22 billion; in December 2001 the fifth review was not completed, cutting off IMF support.
- p. 5: within a month came the President's resignation, the default and the end of convertibility; the IMF "largely stood by as the crisis unraveled".
- p. 5: Lesson 2: sustainable debt for emerging markets may be lower than thought. Lesson 9: delay raises the eventual cost.

## Other primary sources checked for Phase 1 (fetched 22 Nov 2026)
- WTO press release Press/252, 10 November 2001: Ministerial Conference approves China's accession. WTO China member page: member since 11 December 2001.
- WTO: Fourth Ministerial Conference, Doha, 9–14 November 2001.
- NBER business cycle dates: US peak March 2001 (2001Q1), trough November 2001 (2001Q4); contraction 8 months, following a 120-month expansion.
- ECB "Introduction of the euro": euro launched 1 January 1999 as an accounting currency; euro cash introduced 1 January 2002.
- Greenspan, 5 December 1996 (federalreserve.gov): "irrational exuberance" passage.
- Bernanke, 21 November 2002 (federalreserve.gov): deflation speech; judged the chance of significant US deflation "extremely small".
- WEO Apr 2002 p. 8: weaknesses in accounting and auditing exposed by "the recent collapse of Enron". p. 16: June 2001 US tax cuts plus post-9/11 emergency spending gave substantial fiscal support; Doha round progress needed. Ch. 1: the slowdown "would probably not qualify as a full-fledged global recession" (Box 1.1). p. 32: China's recent WTO entry would boost imports.
- WEO Sep 2002 p. 5: Argentina experiencing "an almost unprecedented collapse in economic activity".
- WEO Dec 2001: at market exchange rates, global growth would be 1.4% in 2001 and 1.2% in 2002 (footnote to Ch. 1 text).
- WEO May 2001 Ch. 1 p. 18: on 19 March 2001 the Bank of Japan adopted a quantitative target for banks' current account balances at the central bank (¥5 trillion), in place of a target for the overnight call rate. Ch. 1: given the rapid policy response by the Fed and others, a reasonable prospect the slowdown would be short-lived. Oct 2001 Ch. 1: Japan's GDP projected to fall 0.5% in 2001, likely its fourth recession of the decade. Dec 2001 Ch. 1: the attacks exacerbated an already very difficult situation.
- WEO Oct 2000 Ch. 1 p. 3: oil prices significantly higher than expected owing to supply constraints and strong global demand; a $5 a barrel rise raises advanced countries' net oil imports by about $40 billion a year.
