# Reading notes: IMF WEO, WEO Updates and GFSR, January – October 2026 (read 22 Nov 2026)

Method: issue pages fetched from imf.org with plain `curl` (default user-agent, no custom headers), starting from the listing pages
https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/WEO and https://www.imf.org/en/Publications/GFSR; chapter files taken from the links on each issue page.
The `.pdf.md` twins were not used; every quote and page number below comes from the PDF, converted with `pdftotext` (plain and `-layout`),
page by page. Page numbers are the printed numbers from the running heads/footers ("International Monetary Fund | Month 2026  N");
"p. ?" where the page prints none (then the PDF page is given). In the 2026 WEO Chapter 1 files and both GFSR Chapter 1 files the PDF page
equals the printed page; in the two Updates the first text page prints no number and from PDF p. 3 the printed number is the PDF page minus one.
Quotes are verbatim and under 15 words, checked by script against the converted text of the cited page; everything else is paraphrase.
Growth figures are annual percent change at PPP weights unless stated. India figures are on a fiscal-year basis (Table 1 / Table 1.1 footnote in
every edition; in January and July "2024" = FY2024/25, in October "2025" = FY2025/26, so "2026" = FY2026/27). Table 1 / Table 1.1 gives
consumer prices only for the world and the two country groups; country inflation figures below come from the table footnote (assumed inflation
for the euro area, Japan and the US) or, for April and October, from Annex Tables 1.1.1–1.1.4 at the end of Chapter 1, and are marked as such.
The Updates have no consumer-price annex.

The war: none of the pages read says who started the war or names the belligerents directly. What the reports do say is recorded under each
report: the start date (28 February 2026, April Foreword); strikes on Iran's South Pars gas field and retaliatory strikes on Gulf facilities
including Qatar's Ras Laffan (April CSF); a ceasefire, ceasefires, and a memorandum of understanding between Iran and the United States (April
CSF, July Update, October CSF); rumours before the war of "an impending US attack on Iran" (October CSF); a reimposed naval blockade affecting Iran
(October Ch. 1, imposer not stated); Houthi and other Iran-aligned militia attacks on Red Sea-bound oil infrastructure (October CSF). Israel
appears only in an annex-table note on regional groupings (April Annex Table 1.1.4) and, in the April GFSR, in a figure label for an earlier
episode ("Israel–Gaza, Oct. 2023"). The GFSR April figures label the 2026 episode "the Iran conflict".

## WEO Update January 2026 — *Global Economy: Steady amid Divergent Forces* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook Update, January 2026: Global Economy: Steady amid Divergent Forces")
Read: whole Update (PDF pp. 2–13: text pp. ?–9, Table 1 on p. 10, Box 1 Global Financial Stability Update on p. 11, Annex Table 1 on p. 12). Published 19 January 2026 (listing page). Written before the war; the Middle East appears only as a risk.
- p. ? (PDF p. 2): global growth 3.3% (2026) and 3.2% (2027), against an estimated 3.3% in 2025; 2026 up slightly from October 2025, 2027 unchanged. Headwinds from trade policy are offset by tailwinds from technology investment, including AI, more in North America and Asia. "This steady performance on the surface results from the balancing of divergent forces." Global headline inflation from an estimated 4.1% (2025) to 3.8% (2026) and 3.4% (2027), with the US returning to target more slowly than other large economies.
- p. ? (PDF p. 2): risks tilted down: a reassessment of AI productivity expectations could cut investment and trigger an abrupt market correction; trade, political or geopolitical tensions; high deficits and debt pushing up long-term rates. Upside: AI investment turning into durable productivity gains.
- p. ? (PDF p. 2): a China–US dispute over export controls on semiconductors and rare earths was followed by a truce that cut bilateral tariffs until November 2026 and paused export controls; the US removed tariffs on some farm products for all countries. A US Supreme Court decision on the president's use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act was widely expected in early 2026.
- p. 2: financial conditions still accommodative; tech stocks pulled further away from the rest (Figure 2). The dollar recovered slightly but came briefly under renewed pressure after an investigation into the Federal Reserve chair was opened. Q3 2025 global growth 2.4% annualised; US 4.3%, with technology investment and spending adding about 0.3 point to average annualised growth in the first three quarters of 2025; Japan contracted 2.3%; China slowed to 2.4% (staff estimate).
- p. 3: the US effective tariff rate in the projections is 18.5% (18.7% in October 2025); the rest of the world's rate on US goods is 3.5%. Energy commodity prices expected to fall about 7% in 2026; oil prices low and expected to fall further on tepid demand and strong supply.
- p. 4: Fed and Bank of England rates expected to keep falling, euro area rate unchanged, Bank of Japan raising gradually; near-term fiscal stimulus in Germany, Japan and the US. US growth 2.4% in 2026 (up 0.3 point), 2.0% in 2027, with a fiscal boost from the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025. Euro area 1.3% (2026) and 1.4% (2027); it benefits less from the tech-investment boost and still suffers the lingering effects of the energy price rise after Russia's invasion of Ukraine.
- p. 5: China 5.0% in 2025 (up 0.2), 4.5% in 2026 (up 0.3, from lower US tariffs under the yearlong truce agreed in November and stimulus), 4.0% in 2027. India 7.3% for 2025 (up 0.7 on a strong third quarter and fourth-quarter momentum), moderating to 6.4% in 2026 and 2027 "as cyclical and temporary factors wane". Middle East and Central Asia to accelerate from 3.7% (2025) to 3.9% (2026) and 4.0% (2027) on higher oil output. World trade growth 4.1% (2025), 2.6% (2026), 3.1% (2027).
- p. 6: US core inflation back to 2% during 2027 as tariff pass-through materialises; UK inflation back to target by end-2026; India's inflation to return near target after a marked 2025 decline driven by subdued food prices; China's inflation to start rising from low levels.
- p. 6: "Risks to the outlook for the global economy remain tilted to the downside." An October 2025 scenario with a moderate AI-stock correction cut 2026 global growth by 0.4 point.
- pp. 6–7: a significant escalation of geopolitical tensions, "particularly in the Middle East or Ukraine", could cause substantial negative supply shocks and disrupt major shipping routes; damaged infrastructure could drive commodity prices higher.
- p. 7: upside: faster AI adoption could lift global growth by up to 0.3 point in 2026 and 0.1–0.8 point a year in the medium term.
- pp. 8–9 (policies): credible medium-term fiscal consolidation; targeted, temporary support only; gradual rate cuts possible where inflation is near target, more caution where above target; central bank independence "paramount"; the tech boom may push up neutral real rates, more in the US.
- Table 1 p. 10 (2024 / 2025 / 2026 / 2027): world 3.3/3.3/3.3/3.2; AEs 1.8/1.7/1.8/1.7; US 2.8/2.1/2.4/2.0; euro area 0.9/1.4/1.3/1.4; Germany –0.5/0.2/1.1/1.5; Japan –0.2/1.1/0.7/0.6; UK 1.1/1.4/1.3/1.5; EMDEs 4.3/4.4/4.2/4.1; China 5.0/5.0/4.5/4.0; **India 6.5/7.3/6.4/6.4**; Russia 4.3/0.6/0.8/1.0; Middle East and Central Asia 2.7/3.7/3.9/4.0; Saudi Arabia 2.6/4.3/4.5/3.6; Middle East and North Africa 2.2/3.4/3.9/4.0. Oil price –1.8/–14.2/–8.5/0.1%. World consumer prices 5.8/4.1/3.8/3.4 (AEs 2.6/2.5/2.2/2.1; EMDEs 7.9/5.2/4.8/4.3).

## WEO April 2026 — *Global Economy in the Shadow of War* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook, April 2026: Global Economy in the Shadow of War")
Read: Foreword (pp. xii–xiv, signed Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, Economic Counsellor), Executive Summary (pp. xv–xvi), Chapter 1 "Global Prospects and Policies" (ch1.pdf; pp. 1–20 main text; Box 1.1 opening p. 21; Table 1.1 pp. 7–8; Table 1.2 p. 9; Commodity Special Feature "Market Developments and the Economics of Rare Earths" pp. 31–32; Annex Tables pp. 39–42). Published 14 April 2026. The projections are a "reference forecast" rather than a baseline.
- Foreword p. xii: the outlook darkened after the outbreak of war in the Middle East on 28 February 2026; the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and damage to production facilities "could cause an energy crisis on an unprecedented scale".
- Foreword p. xii: before the war the IMF was poised to upgrade its growth forecast (tech investment boom, easing trade tensions, fiscal support, accommodating financial conditions). "War in the Middle East will overwhelm these underlying forces."
- Foreword p. xii: three channels: a textbook negative supply shock through energy-intensive goods (fertilizers, chemicals, food, transport, heating); second-round wage-price effects, worse where expectations are poorly anchored; a risk-off episode with capital flight and a stronger dollar. Energy importers and low-income countries are most exposed; Gulf energy exporters also suffer (damage, production closures, export constraints, lost tourism); remittance-receiving countries may lose income.
- Foreword p. xii: reference forecast (short-lived conflict): growth 3.1% in 2026, down 0.2 from January; inflation up from 4.1% (2025) to 4.4% (2026). Adverse scenario (market conditions toward end-March): growth 2.5%, inflation 5.4%. Severe scenario (disruption into next year, de-anchored expectations): growth about 2% this year and next, inflation near 6%.
- Foreword p. xiii: unlike 2022, labour markets are softer and pre-shock inflation more subdued, but cost-of-living memories make expectations more sensitive; a flatter supply curve means "The disinflation this time around could prove more costly."
- Foreword p. xiii (policy): central banks can look through the energy shock only while expectations stay anchored, then must act firmly; avoid untargeted fiscal measures such as energy caps and subsidies; keep price signals; avoid price controls and export restrictions. p. xiv: reopening the Strait of Hormuz and a swift end of hostilities would limit the damage.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: the reference forecast assumes the war has limited duration, intensity and scope, with disruptions fading by mid-2026, consistent with commodity futures as of 10 March. Growth 3.1% (2026) and 3.2% (2027), below about 3.4% in 2024–25; inflation 4.4% (2026) and 3.7% (2027). Without the war, 2026 would have been revised up to 3.4%. EMDE growth cut 0.3 point for 2026; AEs broadly unchanged.
- Exec. Summary p. xv: severe scenario: growth about 2% in 2026, inflation just above 6% by 2027; the EMDE impact almost twice that on AEs. Geopolitical tensions could turn the situation into "the largest energy crisis in modern times".
- Exec. Summary p. xvi: monetary policy may look through the supply shock only if expectations stay anchored and the stance was already well calibrated; fiscal support targeted, timely, temporary and funded within budgets.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: a military conflict engulfing the Middle East since the end of February has damaged critical infrastructure and severely disrupted maritime and air traffic in the region. The US effective statutory tariff rate is about 5.3 points below the October 2025 assumption after court rulings and executive actions; Section 122 tariffs need congressional approval to extend beyond 150 days; USMCA review due July 2026.
- Ch. 1 p. 2: Q4 2025 global growth 3.9% annualised; China 6.1%; US 0.5% (government shutdown); China's goods trade surplus a record $1.2 trillion (6% of GDP) in 2025.
- Ch. 1 p. 3: US core PCE inflation 3.1% in January 2026; tariff incidence has fallen largely on US importers and consumers; Japan's inflation fell below 2% for the first time since Q2 2022 (gasoline tax abolition). Since the war: moderate tightening of financial conditions, higher bond yields, lower equities, EM commodity importers hit hardest; "The US dollar has strengthened somewhat, reaffirming its safe haven status." Sharp swings in the gold price.
- Ch. 1 p. 4: near-term prospects worsened by "the closure of the Strait of Hormuz and the attacks on production facilities". Assumptions: the conflict lasts a few more weeks and production and exports from the region normalise by mid-2026; energy prices +19% in 2026; oil +21.4%, an average of $82 a barrel; gas hit harder than oil; food prices up on energy, fertilizer and shipping costs.
- Ch. 1 p. 4 (monetary assumptions): Fed cutting gradually to about 3.1% by end-2027; euro area policy rate up 50 basis points over 2026; Bank of Japan rising toward about 1.5%.
- Ch. 1 p. 5: US general government deficit to widen 0.7 point to 7½% of GDP in 2026 (OBBBA); Germany's deficit to 3.8%; US public debt from 124% of GDP (2025) to 142% (2031); euro area 87% to 90%; EMDEs 74% to 86%. US effective statutory tariff rate in the projections 13.5% (18.7% in October 2025).
- Ch. 1 p. 6: cumulative 2026–27 growth cut 0.5 point for low-income net energy importers, 0.2 point for energy-importing AEs, with positive or neutral revisions for net energy exporters (Figure 1.9). Adverse scenario: oil +80% from Q2 2026 relative to January (about $100 a barrel in 2026, about $75 in 2027).
- Ch. 1 p. 9: severe scenario oil +100% and staying there in 2027 (about $110 in 2026, $125 in 2027), gas +200%. Adverse: growth 2.5% (2026) and 3.0% (2027), inflation 5.4% and 3.9%. Severe: growth cut 1.3 points in 2026, "a close call for a global recession" (below 2%, only four times since 1980), 2.2% in 2027; inflation 5.8% (2026) and 6.1% (2027).
- Ch. 1 p. 10: war lowers AE growth 0.2 point versus the preconflict forecast; small for the US (net energy exporter) and Japan (offsetting measures), large for net energy importers such as the euro area and UK. US 2.3% (2026), 2.1% (2027). Euro area 1.4% (2025) → 1.1% (2026) → 1.2% (2027). Japan 0.7% and 0.6%. UK 0.8% in 2026 (down 0.5 point from October 2025), 1.3% in 2027.
- Ch. 1 p. 11: China 4.4% (2026), 4.0% (2027). India 7.6% for 2025 (up 1.0 point from October) and 6.5% for 2026 and 2027, helped by the fall in additional US tariffs on Indian goods "from 50 to 10 percent", which outweighs the war. Middle East and Central Asia: 3.6% (2025) → 1.9% (2026) → 4.6% (2027); contractions more pronounced for Bahrain, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar, less for Oman, Saudi Arabia and the UAE, depending on dependence on the Strait of Hormuz and alternative export routes. Iran –6.1% (2026, down 7.2 points); Saudi Arabia 3.1% (down 1.4), 4.5% in 2027; Egypt 4.2%.
- Ch. 1 p. 12: Brazil gains about 0.2 point in 2026 as a net energy exporter; Russia revised up 0.3 point to 1.1% on higher commodity prices; Türkiye cut 0.8 point to 3.4%. Inflation: world 4.1% (2025) → 4.4% (2026) → 3.7% (2027), 0.7 point above October 2025 for 2026; US core back to 2% during 2027; UK inflation to rise temporarily toward 4% before returning to target by end-2027.
- Ch. 1 p. 13: euro area headline inflation temporarily above 2% in 2026 and above target in 2027; India's inflation to return near target. World trade 5.1% (2025), 2.8% (2026), 3.8% (2027).
- Ch. 1 p. 14 (risks): "Risks are firmly on the downside"; fertilizer disruption ahead of planting season could cause substantial food price inflation; balance-of-payments distress and social unrest in commodity importers, especially sub-Saharan Africa. p. 15: a one-standard-deviation rise in geopolitical risk lowers GDP about 0.8% after a year; AI-profitability reassessment and fiscal repricing are other risks.
- Ch. 1 p. 16: the reference forecast includes no direct AI productivity effect; faster AI gains could add up to 0.3 point near term, 0.1–0.8 point medium term. p. 17: a broad US tariff reduction could lift global growth 0.6 point (Box 1.3 scenario C).
- Ch. 1 pp. 17–18 (policies): central banks ready to act decisively; second-round effects could be larger than in 2021–22; where policy was well calibrated before the shock, waiting may be an option. On fiscal policy, "discretionary fiscal support should typically be avoided"; if unavoidable, temporary, targeted, with sunset clauses and offsets (p. 18). p. 20: avoid export controls that worsen supply disruptions linked to the war.
- Box 1.1 p. 21: global growth in 2025 was 3.4%, 0.6 point above the April 2025 reference forecast; the effective statutory tariff rate fell to about 18% by end-2025; the actual collected tariff rate was about half the announced rate in December 2025. (Box p. 21 also notes the dollar fell 6% between 1 April and end-December 2025.)
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 7 (2025 / 2026 / 2027): world 3.4/3.1/3.2; AEs 1.9/1.8/1.7; US 2.1/2.3/2.1; euro area 1.4/1.1/1.2; Germany 0.2/0.8/1.2; Japan 1.2/0.7/0.6; UK 1.3/0.8/1.3; EMDEs 4.4/3.9/4.2; China 5.0/4.4/4.0; **India 7.6/6.5/6.5**; Russia 1.0/1.1/1.1; Middle East and Central Asia 3.6/1.9/4.6; Saudi Arabia 4.5/3.1/4.5; Middle East and North Africa 3.2/1.1/4.8; low-income developing countries 4.8/4.8/4.9. Oil price –14.4/21.4/–7.6%. World consumer prices 4.1/4.4/3.7 (AEs 2.5/2.8/2.2; EMDEs 5.2/5.5/4.6).
- Ch. 1 Table 1.1 p. 7 footnotes: 3 India on a fiscal-year basis, GDP from 2022 based on market prices with FY2022/23 base year (no calendar-year figures given). 5 oil averaged $67.74 in 2025; assumed $82.22 in 2026 and $75.97 in 2027. 7 assumed inflation 2026/2027: euro area 2.6%/2.2%, Japan 2.2%/2.3%, US 3.2%/2.1%.
- Ch. 1 Annex Tables pp. 39–42 (consumer prices 2025/2026/2027): euro area 2.1/2.6/2.2; Germany 2.3/2.7/2.3; UK 3.4/3.2/2.4; Russia 8.7/5.6/4.3 (p. 39); Japan 3.2/2.2/2.3; China 0.0/1.2/1.5; India 2.1/4.7/4.0 (p. 40); US 2.7/3.2/2.1 (p. 41); Saudi Arabia 2.0/2.3/2.1; Iran 50.9/68.9/39.6; Middle East and Central Asia 11.2/11.9/9.1 (p. 42). Growth 2026 (p. 42): Iraq –6.8, Qatar –8.6, Kuwait –0.6, Bahrain –0.5, UAE 3.1, Oman 3.5; oil exporters 0.5, oil importers 4.1.
- CSF p. 31: oil prices rose 57.6% between August 2025 and March 2026 to $105.8 a barrel. Shipments through the Strait of Hormuz, "a critical choke point with few alternatives for rerouting", stopped, curtailing about 8.5 million barrels a day of crude exports; usually about 20 mb/d (20% of world consumption) flows through it, 15 mb/d of it crude. Global inventories at a five-year high of 8 billion barrels give only a partial buffer.
- CSF p. 31: Brent peaked at $119 on 10 March, then retreated after the US administration said it expected a short conflict and then a ceasefire was announced. Europe's TTF gas price rose 61% (peak $17.7 per MMBtu); Asian LNG $20.8 (+80.6%), as over three-quarters of LNG through the strait goes to Asia. Strikes on Iran's South Pars gas field prompted retaliatory strikes on Persian Gulf energy facilities, including Qatar's Ras Laffan on 18 March. US Henry Hub up only 4.9%, to $3.
- CSF p. 32: metals index +36.6% (August 2025–March 2026); gold up 44.4%, reaching "record prices exceeding $5,000 per ounce", then back to start-of-year levels after the conflict began as investors took profits amid higher rate expectations and a stronger dollar. Copper +29.5%, aluminium +29.8% (Middle East about 9% of world aluminium output). Food prices expected to rise 6.0% in 2026; a longer conflict could push them much higher through transport and gas-derived fertilizer costs.

## GFSR April 2026 — *Global Financial Markets Confront the War in the Middle East and Amplification Risks* (issue page title: "Global Financial Markets Confront the War in the Middle East and Amplification Risks"; page heading "Global Financial Stability Report, April 2026")
Read: Executive Summary (pp. xi–xiii); Chapter 1 (same title), "At a Glance" and introduction (pp. 1–4).
- Exec. Summary p. xi: "Global financial stability risks are elevated." Markets have corrected in an orderly way so far but risks are asymmetric; the longer the conflict, the greater the risk of further, more abrupt tightening.
- Exec. Summary p. xi: "Since February, global equity prices have declined by 8 percent", after gains on strong profits. Sovereign yields rose sharply on inflation expectations; EM assets hit by a stronger dollar and energy prices, especially commodity importers.
- Exec. Summary pp. xi–xii: amplification channels: larger bond-yield swings on auction days with more price-sensitive investors and shorter issuance; EM currency and outflow pressures as carry trades unwind (capital flows "K-shaped", skewed to debt); forced selling by leveraged nonbanks (hedge funds, option sellers, leveraged ETFs); rising private-credit defaults, particularly for leveraged borrowers exposed to AI disruption.
- Exec. Summary p. xiii: AI investment could slow if the conflict persists, hitting firms reliant on circular financing with hyperscalers at the centre, though the financial-stability impact appears modest currently; equities and bonds have been selling off together. Policy: liquidity facilities ready; fiscal stances tighter, with new spending limited to protecting the vulnerable from the inflation shock.
- Ch. 1 p. 1: since late February equities fell and bond yields rose sharply on a jump in energy prices; central banks already well calibrated "may benefit from waiting" for clarity; swap lines should be enhanced.
- Ch. 1 p. 2: higher energy prices raised expected inflation over two years by 0.3–0.8 point (inflation swaps); expected policy paths moved up, "in some cases flipping from cuts to hikes". Footnote 1: some projections put AI investment above $3.4 trillion by 2030.
- Ch. 1 p. 4: financial conditions tightened but less than in previous global shocks and remain accommodative historically; market functioning orderly. The 90th-percentile move in the 10-year US Treasury yield on auction days is 11 basis points since 2023, against 6 in 2020–22.

## WEO Update July 2026 — *Global Economy in Crosscurrents of War and Technology* (issue page title: "World Economic Outlook Update, July 2026: Global Economy in Crosscurrents of War and Technology")
Read: whole Update (PDF pp. 2–15: text pp. ?–11, Table 1 on p. 12, Box 1 Global Financial Markets Update on p. 13, Annex Table 1 on p. 14). Published 8 July 2026 (listing page).
- p. ? (PDF p. 2): global growth 3.0% (2026) and 3.4% (2027), down from an average 3.5% in 2024–25 and broadly unchanged cumulatively from April; war effects partly offset by the AI-driven technology cycle. Energy exporters outside the conflict zone and tech-chain economies gain; energy importers outside the tech chain, including many low-income countries, lose. Inflation 4.1% (2025) → 4.7% (2026) → 3.9% (2027): "the disinflation trend in place since the beginning of 2024 has stalled".
- p. ? (PDF p. 2): the global economy has "weathered the shock from the war better than feared", but transmission is early: commercial and strategic destocking has given temporary relief.
- p. 2: ceasefires and a memorandum of understanding between Iran and the United States cooled prices from their April peaks; energy prices roughly 25% above prewar levels. The futures curve implies $78 a barrel for 2026, against $82 in the April reference forecast and $100 in the April adverse scenario, because inventory drawdowns made up part of the lost Hormuz flows (Figure 1). Retail gasoline up 30% in emerging Asia but only 15% in Latin America; LNG up about 50% in Asia and 25% in Europe; Henry Hub up about 10%.
- p. 2: global headline inflation rose for a third month in a row in May; sequential headline inflation jumped almost 4 points between February and April (annualised) while core stayed broadly stable; little evidence of de-anchoring.
- p. 3: financial conditions eased from early-April peaks; markets price higher policy rates. Q1 2026 global growth 3.0% annualised (forecast 2.7%); the top four AI-hardware exporters (Taiwan Province of China, Korea, Thailand, Malaysia) surprised by 4.4 points on average; Korea grew 7.5% (1.8% forecast); China 8.1%; Japan 1.8%. p. 4: Germany 1.4%; US 2.1%.
- p. 4 (assumptions): the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz begins in mid-July, with conditions broadly back to prewar by March 2027; shortages assumed in some EMDEs without stocks that are outbid for cargoes. Oil $89 a barrel (9% above April), +32% on 2025; TTF gas $15 (+22%); fertilizer prices +26%; food prices +8%. Euro area and US policy rates held broadly steady in ex-ante real terms; Japan moves toward neutral.
- p. 5: US effective tariff rate broadly the same as in April; uncertainty elevated through 2027; no exogenous AI productivity boost. World trade 5.0% (2025), 3.5% (2026), 4.3% (2027). US 2.3% (2026), 2.2% (2027); euro area 0.9% (2026, down 0.2, partly Ireland), 1.2% (2027); UK 1.0% then 1.3%; Japan 0.6% then 0.7% (pp. 5–6).
- p. 6: Korea 2.6% (2026) on semiconductor demand; EMDEs 3.8% (2026), 4.5% (2027). China 4.6%. India "remains among the fastest-growing major economies" at 6.4%, on private consumption and services.
