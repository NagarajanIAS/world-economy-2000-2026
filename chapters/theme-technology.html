Technology and productivity

Since 2000 the internet and the mobile phone have reached most of humanity, and artificial intelligence has become the biggest investment story of the 2020s. Yet in most rich countries, and many poor ones, output per hour of work grew more slowly after the mid-2000s than before. This chapter follows the spread of the technology, the slowdown in productivity and the arguments about why, the fears and hopes about jobs, and India's path from software exporter to builder of digital public infrastructure.

2000–2026 · about 20 minutes · Read with Theme 1: Growth and convergence, Phase 1: The bust and the turn and Phase 8: AI, tariffs and fragmentation

In brief

In 2005 about one person in six used the internet; by 2025 three in four did. Over the same years the growth of labour productivity, the main source of rising living standards, slowed almost everywhere. In the United States, output per hour grew by about 3 percent a year in 1995–2004, as businesses put computers to use, then by half that from 2004 to 2019. In Europe and Japan growth was slower still, and it has not recovered. Economists still disagree about why. Big inventions may be rarer, weak demand may have held back investment, statistics may miss the gains, or the gains may simply take years to arrive. Since 2019 US productivity has picked up, and some see the first payoff from new technology; AI investment is booming, but in April 2026 the IMF still left AI's effect on productivity out of its forecasts. India built a world-class software export industry and then a digital infrastructure of identity, payments and bank accounts used by hundreds of millions. Its own productivity growth has been faster than rich countries' but owes more to adding capital than to using it better.

A revolution you can see everywhere

The first quarter of the century was the age in which the world went online. On the World Bank's figures, the share of the world's population using the internet rose from 16 percent in 2005 to 40 percent in 2015 and 74 percent in 2025 (chart T7.1). In China the share rose from 2 percent in 2000 to 92 percent; in India, from under 1 percent to 70 percent. The mobile phone went further and faster. In Sub-Saharan Africa there were 2 mobile subscriptions per 100 people in 2000 and 90 in 2025, far more than the share of Africans using the internet, which was 36 percent. The International Telecommunication Union counts 6.3 billion people online in 2026, 76 percent of the world, but about 2 billion still offline, and only 32 percent of people in low-income countries online.1

The World Bank devoted its 2016 World Development Report to this spread. It called the moment the greatest information and communications revolution in history, and noted that the number of internet users had more than tripled in a decade, to an estimated 3.2 billion by the end of 2015. But its verdict was sober: firms were more connected than ever, yet global productivity growth had slowed. "So, while digital technologies have been spreading, digital dividends have not."2 Pessimism about the world economy, it argued, was "not because of digital technologies, but in spite of them".2

The productivity puzzle

Productivity is output per unit of input. The simplest measure is output per hour worked. A second, total factor productivity (TFP), is the part of output growth left over after allowing for more machines and more, and better-educated, workers. It is the closest thing economists have to a measure of how well an economy uses new ideas. Because it is a remainder, it also soaks up every error in measuring the other parts.

The United States is the best-measured case (chart T7.2). Output per hour in the business sector grew by 2.8 percent a year from 1948 to 1973 and only 1.4 percent from 1973 to 1995. Then came the IT speed-up: 3.1 percent a year in 1995–2004, as firms bought computers and reorganised around them. It did not last. From 2004 to 2019 growth fell back to 1.5 percent a year, and TFP growth to 0.6 percent. Had the pace of 1995–2004 continued, output per hour in 2025 would have been about 32 percent higher than it was. Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson, writing in 2017, summed up the puzzle: "we see transformative new technologies everywhere but in the productivity statistics". The line echoes a famous 1987 complaint by the economist Robert Solow about computers.3

Elsewhere the slowdown was deeper. On the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's (OECD's) measure, GDP per hour worked grew by 1.1 percent a year in the euro area in 2000–07, 0.7 percent in 2007–19 and 0.3 percent since. In the United Kingdom (chart T7.3) growth fell from 1.7 percent a year before the financial crisis to 0.3 percent after it. Italy's output per hour in 2025 was where it had been in 2000, at 100 on an index of 2000 = 100. Korea, still catching up, grew far faster, but its pace also slowed by more than 40 percent, from 6.5 percent a year in 2000–07 to 3.7 percent in 2007–19. The European gap with America widened: by 2025 GDP per hour in the euro area was 85 percent of the US level and in Japan 59 percent, at purchasing-power exchange rates.

Total factor productivity tells the same story more starkly. In the Penn World Table's estimates, TFP in 2023 was only 113 percent of its 2000 level in the United States and 107 percent in Germany. In the United Kingdom (99), France (94) and Italy (82) it was no higher, or lower, than in 2000 (chart T7.4). The World Bank found the slowdown in about 70 percent of economies, rich and poor, home to more than 80 percent of the world's extreme poor. World labour productivity growth fell from 2.7 percent in 2007 to 1.5 percent in 2016, and in emerging and developing economies from 6.6 percent to 3.2 percent in 2015, "the steepest, longest, and broadest yet". At that pace, halving the gap between poorer and richer economies' productivity would take more than a century.4 In 2024 the IMF calculated that "a significant and broad-based slowdown in total factor productivity growth" explained more than half the fall in world growth since the 2000s. In advanced economies TFP growth fell from 1.3 percent a year in 1995–2000 to 0.2 percent after the pandemic.5

Contested question

Why did productivity growth slow just as digital technology spread? There are four main answers, and they are not mutually exclusive. A World Bank study of 2018 began by admitting "there is little consensus".6

The big inventions are behind us

In 2012 Robert Gordon argued that rapid growth since 1750 "could well turn out to be a unique episode in human history". He compared three industrial revolutions. The second, of electricity, the engine and running water, drove eighty years of fast productivity growth. The third, of computers, the web and mobile phones, gave only a short revival in 1996–2004 that had already faded. He added six headwinds, from ageing to debt, that could halve long-run growth.7

Weak demand and low interest rates

In late 2013 Lawrence Summers revived the idea of secular stagnation: saving had come to exceed what firms wanted to invest even at very low interest rates, so economies could run below capacity for years. Among the reasons he listed for falling real rates were slower growth of the workforce and "perhaps slower productivity growth".8 With Łukasz Rachel he later estimated that neutral real interest rates in the industrial world had fallen by at least 3 percentage points over a generation.8

The statistics miss the gains

Perhaps free search, maps and social media, and better phones at the same price, are undercounted. Two studies of 2016 rejected this as the main answer. David Byrne, John Fernald and Marshall Reinsdorf found "little evidence that this slowdown arises from growing mismeasurement"; correcting the prices of IT equipment would make the slowdown worse, not better.9 Chad Syverson showed that the slowdown hit dozens of countries regardless of how much IT they used. Estimates of what consumers gain from internet services came to less than a third of the $2.7 trillion or more of "missing output".10

The gains take time

Brynjolfsson, Rock and Syverson argued that new general-purpose technologies need years of costly reorganisation before they pay off. At first that investment in software, skills and new ways of working depresses measured productivity; later it raises it, a productivity J-curve. Electricity took decades to transform factories, and computers only lifted US productivity in the late 1990s.3

How the evidence weighs up. Most research has turned against mismeasurement as the main cause, and the IMF's 2024 analysis, while calling it an open question, cited Syverson's evidence against it.5 The IMF and the World Bank also point to weaker investment, declining business dynamism, and capital and workers stuck in less productive firms (misallocation).45 The other three explanations remain live. The IMF has suggested that the pick-up in US productivity since 2020 may be an early payoff from new technology,11 as the lag story predicts; but it is too recent, and too American, to settle the argument.

Spending on the future

The two technology booms of the period show up clearly in American investment (chart T7.5). Business spending on computers, communications equipment and software rose from 2.9 percent of US GDP in 1990 to 4.4 percent in 2000, at the peak of the dot-com boom, then fell back to 3.5 percent in 2010. The AI boom took it to a new high of 4.5 percent in 2025 and 5.3 percent in the third quarter of 2026. The World Bank expects the biggest US AI companies alone to spend more than $750 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026.12 The IMF estimated that AI-related investment added about half a percentage point to US growth in 2025, much of it spent on imported equipment, which also helped Asian exporters.11

Research spending tells a story of new entrants (chart T7.6). Korea raised spending on research and development from 2.05 percent of GDP in 2000 to 4.94 percent in 2023, and the United States from 2.62 to 3.45 percent. China's rise was the largest in scale: from 0.88 percent of GDP in 2000 to 2.58 percent, above the European Union's 2.26 percent. In the world as a whole R&D rose from 2.04 to 2.60 percent of GDP. Measured productivity growth slowed even so.

Robots, AI and jobs

Each wave of technology brought a wave of fear for jobs. In 2013 Carl Frey and Michael Osborne estimated that "about 47 percent of total US employment is at risk" of computerisation, a figure that was quoted around the world. It measured which tasks could technically be automated, not a forecast of job losses, and it set no date.13 The World Bank's 2019 report on the changing nature of work pushed back: "the threat to jobs from technology is exaggerated". It found the share of industrial jobs stable or rising in poorer countries, and argued that the bigger problem was the 2 billion people working informally without social protection.14

The arrival of generative AI in late 2022 started a third wave. IMF staff reckoned in January 2024 that "almost 40 percent of global employment is exposed to AI", about 60 percent in advanced economies, where roughly half the exposed jobs might gain and half might lose, and 26 percent in low-income countries. Unlike earlier automation, which mostly hit middle-skill work, AI reaches into the jobs of the highly educated.15 Estimates of the economic payoff range widely. Daron Acemoglu put the likely gain at no more than 0.66 percent on the level of TFP over ten years, "nontrivial but modest", against forecasts by Goldman Sachs of 1.5 percentage points a year on US productivity growth.16 The IMF's 2024 estimate was that AI could add 0.1 to 0.8 of a percentage point to world growth in the medium term.5 The Bank for International Settlements noted that AI was being adopted faster than any earlier technology, and expected "an even steeper J-curve".17

By 2026 the money was flowing but the productivity was not yet visible in the official forecasts. "The reference forecast does not include direct effects of AI on productivity," the IMF wrote in April 2026. It saw a faster payoff, "possibly foretold" by above-trend US productivity growth since 2020, as an upside risk worth up to 0.3 point on world growth soon and 0.1 to 0.8 point later. It also modelled the reverse: a collapse of AI investment with US share prices falling 20 percent.11 The World Bank's 2026 report, devoted to AI, was more hopeful for developing countries. It found that only about 4.5 percent of existing jobs in low- and middle-income countries could be automated by generative AI, against 14.2 percent in high-income countries, and that adoption lags between rich and poor countries were shrinking. Steam took about eighty years to spread from richer to poorer countries; computers and the internet about twenty. But it also found early signs of fewer postings for entry-level white-collar jobs.12 An IMF note of January 2026 found that the spread of AI skills was linked to lower employment in the most exposed occupations, "posing challenges for the youth".18

India: software, phones and a digital state

For India, the digital revolution began as an export business. The Economic Survey of 2001-02 described software and IT-enabled services as having "emerged as a niche opportunity for India". Software exports were $6.2 billion in 2000-01, after growth of more than 50 percent a year through the 1990s, mainly to North America, driven by low costs.19 The niche became an industry. IT and IT-enabled services exports reached $31.3 billion in 2006-07, with 16 lakh (1.6 million) people directly employed.20 On the balance-of-payments data the IMF and World Bank use, India's exports of information and communication technology (ICT) services rose from $5.0 billion in 2000 to $54 billion in 2010 and $178 billion in 2024 (chart T7.7), 47 percent of all its services exports. India's share of the world's ICT services exports rose from 5.5 percent in 2000 to 15.7 percent in 2010, but slipped to 13.6 percent in 2024 as Ireland, home to the European operations of American technology firms, raced ahead. The business has shifted from outsourcing towards in-house centres of foreign firms: by 2023-24 India hosted more than 1,700 global capability centres employing over 19 lakh (1.9 million) professionals.21

At home, the phone came first (chart T7.8). India had 3.6 telephones per 100 people in March 2001, far behind other developing countries.22 By September 2026 the regulator counted 1.37 billion telephone subscriptions and a teledensity of 85 percent, excluding machine-to-machine connections.22 Internet use took off later: 14.9 percent of Indians used the internet in 2015, 29.5 percent in 2019 and 70 percent in 2025. Fixed broadband stayed rare, at 3.2 subscriptions per 100 people in 2024 against 47.2 in China.

India's distinctive contribution was digital public infrastructure: public systems for identity, payments and data on which others can build. The Economic Survey of 2014-15 set out the "JAM trinity" of Jan Dhan bank accounts, Aadhaar identity numbers and mobile phones as a way to pay subsidies directly to the poor. More than 720 million people had an Aadhaar number by December 2014.23 The Unified Payments Interface, launched in 2016, carried 18 million transactions in 2016-17 and 185,866 million in 2024-25, on the National Payments Corporation's figures.24 The government estimates that direct transfers and related reforms saved about 1.1 percent of GDP up to March 2021, mostly by removing duplicate and ineligible beneficiaries. IMF staff, reporting the estimate, cautioned that the effect "is difficult to disentangle" from other changes.25 The World Bank's 2026 report held up UPI as a model of an open system with many providers.12

Has all this raised India's productivity? India's output per worker grew far faster than rich countries': 4.0 percent a year from 2000 to 2023 in the Penn World Table, against 1.3 percent in the United States. But most of that came from more capital per worker and better-educated workers. TFP grew by 0.8 percent a year, about a fifth of the total, compared with 3.0 percent in China. The Economic Survey's own estimate of trend TFP growth is higher, about 1.9 percent a year in 2012-13 to 2019-20 and 1.7 percent in 2022-23 to 2024-25. It assumes a return to 1.9 percent as digital infrastructure pays off, noting that "deeper productivity effects tend to materialise with a lag".26 India still spends little on research, 0.65 percent of GDP in 2020, with 259 researchers per million people against 2,107 in China.

On AI, India's exposure looks lower than rich countries' because most Indian workers are farmers, craft workers or labourers: the IMF put only 26 percent of Indian jobs in the high-exposure group.15 The pressure falls on the white-collar jobs that built the IT industry. The World Bank found that generative AI had already reduced postings for the most exposed white-collar occupations in India, hitting entry-level workers hardest.12 The Economic Survey of 2024-25 cited a survey in which 68 percent of white-collar employees expected their jobs to be partly or fully automated within five years. The 2025-26 Survey argued for an Indian path to AI that is bottom-up, built for particular sectors and open, suited to scarce capital, computing power and energy; it warned that "productivity gains from augmentation have a ceiling".27 In March 2024 the cabinet approved the IndiaAI Mission, with an outlay of ₹10,371.92 crore (about ₹104 billion) and plans for more than 10,000 graphics processors through public-private partnerships.28

Key terms

Labour productivity
Output per hour worked, or per worker.
Total factor productivity
The part of output growth not explained by more capital or more labour.
General-purpose technology
A technology, like electricity or computing, that spreads across the whole economy.
Productivity J-curve
The idea that measured productivity dips, then rises, as firms invest to use a new technology.
Secular stagnation
A long period of weak demand and low interest rates in which saving exceeds desired investment.
Digital public infrastructure
Shared public systems for identity, payments and data, such as Aadhaar and UPI.
