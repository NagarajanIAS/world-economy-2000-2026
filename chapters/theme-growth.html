Growth and convergence

For most of history the rich countries pulled further ahead. In the first decade of this century that changed: most poorer economies grew faster than the rich ones, and the centre of the world economy moved towards Asia. This chapter asks how far the catch-up went, why it slowed after 2010 and almost stopped in the 2020s, and how the experts' expectations for long-run growth fell along the way.

2000–2026, with IMF projections to 2031 · about 20 minutes · Read with Phase 2: The great boom and Phase 5: Slow growth and slowbalisation

In brief

In 2000 the advanced economies produced 58.3 percent of the world's output, measured at purchasing-power parity; by 2025 they produced 39.5 percent, and China had overtaken the United States. Emerging and developing economies grew faster than rich ones in every year, and in 2000–2010 four in five of the poorer economies narrowed the gap with the United States in income per person. Then the catch-up lost pace. Commodity prices fell, China slowed, trade stopped outrunning output, and productivity growth faded almost everywhere. In 2019–2025 fewer than half of the poorer economies gained on the United States, and the IMF now projects no convergence at all over the medium term. Its forecast for world growth five years ahead, above 5 percent in 2007, is about 3 percent. India is one of the few large economies whose prospects have been raised rather than cut.

The great shift

The simplest way to see what changed is to look at who produces the world's output (chart T1.1). Comparing economies needs a common yardstick, and the one used here is purchasing-power parity (PPP), which values each country's output at the same set of prices, so that a haircut in Delhi counts for as much as a haircut in Dallas. On that measure China produced 6.7 percent of world output in 2000 and 19.6 percent in 2025, passing the United States in 2016. India's share rose from 3.9 to 8.2 percent. The shares of the United States, the European Union and Japan all fell. Taken together, the emerging and developing economies overtook the advanced ones in 2009, the year of the global recession, and produced 60.5 percent of world output by 2025. At market exchange rates the shift is smaller, because prices are lower in poorer countries, but it points the same way.

Behind the shift was a gap in growth rates (chart T1.2). In 2000–2007 the emerging and developing economies grew by an average of 6.3 percent a year, the advanced economies by 2.7 percent. China averaged 10.6 percent. The gap was not new, but its breadth was. The IMF noted in 2017 that emerging and developing economies had supplied 70 percent of world growth in 2000–2008, nearly double their share in the 1980s.1

Catching up

Growing faster is not the same as catching up. Poorer countries usually have faster-growing populations, so what matters for living standards is income per person, and what matters for convergence is whether it grows faster than in the richest countries. Economists have long expected it to: a poor country can copy technology and methods that rich ones had to invent. As the Commission on Growth and Development, a panel of mostly developing-country leaders convened by the World Bank, put it in 2008, the fastest-growing economies "imported what the rest of the world knew".2 In practice, catch-up had been the exception. The IMF found that the gap between the typical developing economy and the United States held steady in the 1970s and widened in the 1980s and 1990s, the decades of Latin America's debt crisis and the Asian financial crisis.1

The 2000s broke the pattern. Of the 145 economies whose income per person was below half the US level in 2000, 81 percent grew faster per person than the United States over the following decade (chart T1.3). The World Bank estimates that income per person in emerging and developing economies grew 3.4 percentage points a year faster than in advanced economies in 2000–2010.3 In 2010–2019 the share of poorer economies catching up fell to 65 percent, and in 2019–2025, years that include the pandemic and its aftermath, to 44 percent: fewer than half.

The progress was real but uneven (chart T1.4). Income per person in China rose from about 7 percent of the US level in 2000 to 32.5 percent in 2025; Vietnam's from 7 to 20 percent; Poland's from 33 to 62 percent. Latin America as a whole stood still, at about a quarter of the US level, and Mexico fell back, from 38 to 28.5 percent. Sub-Saharan Africa, home to most of the world's poorest people, barely moved: its income per person was 6.2 percent of the US level in 2000 and 6.9 percent in 2025. Even after two decades of catch-up, the IMF found in 2017, income per person in 90 percent of emerging and developing economies was still less than half the US level.1

Why the catch-up slowed

Part of the answer is that the 2000s had been unusually kind. The IMF's 2017 study found that external conditions, cheap finance, strong demand and high export prices, had become more important for emerging economies' growth, and that easier external finance alone accounted for about a third of the rise in their growth per person.1 China's demand had driven a decade-long rise in commodity prices (Phase 2). When prices fell after 2011, commodity exporters slowed far more than other developing economies, and the IMF cut its forecast of their growth five years ahead by almost a percentage point. Its 2015 study found that their potential growth had also been flattered by the boom, though only about half as much as their actual growth.4 China itself slowed, from an average of 10.6 percent a year in 2000–2007 to 7.7 percent in 2010–2019, as it tried to rely less on investment and exports. And world trade, which had grown twice as fast as output in 1990–2011, grew only as fast as output in the 2010s.3

The deeper cause was productivity. Growth comes from more workers, more capital and better use of both; the last is measured as total factor productivity (TFP). The IMF's 2024 study of the slowdown found that weaker TFP growth explained more than half of the fall in world growth. In emerging markets TFP growth fell from 2.5 percent a year in 2001–2007 to 0.7 percent; in low-income countries from about 2 percent to nearly zero; in advanced economies, where the slowdown had begun in the early 2000s, it fell to 0.2 percent after the pandemic. Part of the cause was misallocation: capital and workers stuck in less productive firms, which the IMF estimated cost 0.6 percentage point of productivity growth a year.5 Population ageing added to the drag: the IMF expects the world's labour force to grow by only 0.3 percent a year by 2030, and the World Bank attributes about half the projected slowdown in potential growth in the 2020s to ageing and falling participation.53

The result was what the World Bank called a "structural growth slowdown". Its estimate of world potential growth fell from 3.5 percent a year in 2000–2010 to 2.6 percent in 2011–2021, and it projects 2.2 percent for the rest of the 2020s, the slowest pace since 2000.3 The catch-up of poorer countries, the IMF found, could explain only about a quarter of the slowdown in world growth. By its measure of the speed of convergence, which differs from the count in chart T1.3, convergence between countries was fastest in 2008–2012, held up until 2019 and stopped after the pandemic. "Current projections point to no convergence over the medium term."5

How long-run expectations fell

The clearest record of changing expectations is the IMF's own forecasts (chart T1.5). In each World Economic Outlook it projects growth five years ahead, when the effects of booms and recessions should have passed. In April 2000 it expected the world to grow by 4.3 percent in 2005. By October 2007, at the top of the boom, it expected 5.1 percent in 2012, with emerging and developing economies growing by 7.3 percent. After the financial crisis the forecasts drifted down, edition after edition: to 3.6 percent in October 2019 and 3.2 percent in October 2022. In April 2023 the IMF called its forecast of 3.0 percent for 2028 "the lowest medium-term growth forecast" in any of its reports since 1990.6 The October 2026 edition expects 3.1 percent in 2031.7

Were the forecasters simply too gloomy? The IMF asked itself that question in 2024. Since 2008, it found, its five-year-ahead forecasts had been cut for nearly 82 percent of economies, and five large emerging economies, Brazil, China, India, Indonesia and Russia, accounted for almost half of the cut in the world forecast. It found no sign of a pessimistic bias. The downgrades, it concluded, reflected "secular forces rather than forecaster pessimism", though they may also have corrected the optimism of the boom years.5 In hindsight the forecasts of the 2000s assumed that conditions which proved temporary, cheap commodities for importers and dear ones for exporters, abundant finance and fast-growing trade, would last.

How the explanations changed

The institutions' account of what drives growth shifted with events. In 2003, with the 1990s crises in mind, the IMF stressed institutions: its analysis found that a measure of the quality of governance explained nearly three-quarters of the differences in income between countries, and that the gains from better institutions were largest for the poorest.8 In 2008 the Growth Report looked at the 13 economies that had grown by 7 percent a year or more for at least 25 years. They had made full use of the world economy, kept prices and public finances stable, saved and invested heavily, let markets allocate resources, and had committed and capable governments. The report thought India and Vietnam might be next, but warned that growth often slows sharply at middle incomes, as it had in Brazil after 1980.2

After the boom ended, the emphasis moved outward. The IMF's 2015 and 2017 studies stressed commodity prices, external demand and finance, and advised developing economies to strengthen their institutions so as to make more of a weaker external impulse.41 By 2024 the focus was on productivity, misallocation and demography. The IMF's remedies were reforms that let capital and labour move to better firms, more women in work, openness to trade and migration, and the hope that artificial intelligence would raise productivity, which it estimated could add between 0.1 and 0.8 percentage point to world growth. It warned that badly designed industrial policy could make misallocation worse.5 The World Bank's prescription was similar, and it judged that restoring the growth of the previous decade would take a "herculean" effort.3 In October 2026 the IMF expected world growth of 3.2 percent a year in 2026–2031, against 4.3 percent in 2000–2007.7

India: a late, steady climber

India's catch-up began earlier than most, with growth stepping up in the early 1980s and again after the reforms of the 1990s, and it has been steadier than China's.9 Its income per person rose from 5.1 percent of the US level in 2000 to 13.1 percent in 2025 (chart T1.4), and from 24 percent of the world average to 44 percent in 2024 (chart T1.6). It grew more slowly than China for most of the period, so it fell further behind China: its income per person was 74 percent of China's in 2000 and 39 percent in 2024. Unlike most emerging economies, though, India did not slow much after the financial crisis. Its growth averaged 6.5 percent a year in 2000–2007 and 6.6 percent in 2010–2019, and the IMF projects 6.9 percent a year in 2026–2031, while it expects China's growth to fall to 3.8 percent. India's share of world output is projected to reach 10.2 percent by 2031 (chart T1.1). In October 2026, when it cut its forecasts for many economies, the IMF wrote that India "stands out with upward revisions".7

The Economic Survey's view of how fast India could grow has swung widely. In 2007-08, after three years of growth above 9 percent, it saw a new, higher growth path and expected growth to average more than 8.9 percent over the following five years; it judged that India could join the small club of economies that had grown by 9 percent a year for a decade.9 By 2013-14 growth had fallen to 4.6 percent for two years, and the Survey's message was that the trend rate itself had to be revived.10 In 2015-16 it used a convergence framework to argue that India, having under-achieved, should catch up faster than the norm: its potential growth was "somewhere between 8 and 10 percent".11 The 2022-23 Survey was more cautious, expecting about 6.5 percent in the medium term and 7–8 percent if reforms continued.12 The 2025-26 Survey raised its estimate of potential growth to about 7 percent, citing cleaner bank balance sheets, public investment and reforms, but assuming only that productivity growth returns to its pre-pandemic pace.13

That leaves a gap with the government's ambition. To become a developed country by 2047, the centenary of independence, the 2024-25 Survey estimated that India would need growth of about 8 percent a year for a decade or two, with investment rising to about 35 percent of GDP from 31 percent. It also argued that the route East Asia took, raising investment ever higher, was less open in a fragmented world, so the efficiency of investment would matter more than its quantity.14 The international studies point the same way: the IMF's analysis of the global slowdown puts productivity, and the freedom of capital and labour to move to better uses, at the centre.5 India has so far defied the slowdown that has affected most emerging economies. Whether it can go further, at a time when world trade grows no faster than output, is the question its next two decades will answer.

Key terms

Purchasing-power parity (PPP)
A way of comparing economies that values their output at a common set of prices, correcting for the fact that many goods and services are cheaper in poorer countries.
Convergence
Poorer economies growing faster per person than richer ones, so that the gap in living standards narrows.
Total factor productivity (TFP)
The part of growth not explained by more workers or more capital: better technology, organisation and use of resources.
Misallocation
Capital and workers stuck in less productive firms or sectors, lowering an economy's overall productivity.
Potential output
The level of output an economy can sustain without pushing inflation up; its growth rate is the economy's long-run speed limit.
