Slow growth and slowbalisation

The crisis was over but the old speed never came back. Oil prices collapsed, China wobbled, interest rates in Europe and Japan went below zero, and politics turned against open trade. By 2019 world trade was barely growing and the United States and China were in a tariff war.

July 2014 – December 2019 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: Uneven recovery · Next: Pandemic shock and response

What happened

World output grew by about 3.5 percent a year from 2014 to 2019, steady but slower than before the financial crisis. In mid-2014 the price of oil began to fall: Brent crude, 112 dollars a barrel in June 2014, averaged 31 dollars in January 2016. Oil importers gained and exporters such as Russia, Brazil and Nigeria fell into recession. In August 2015 China let its currency fall, and a slowing Chinese economy, carrying a large build-up of debt, unsettled markets into early 2016. Central banks in Europe and Japan pushed interest rates below zero, and by mid-2016 the German government could borrow for ten years at a negative yield. Then politics turned. Britain voted in June 2016 to leave the European Union, and in 2018 the United States put tariffs on imports from China in successive lists, the largest covering about 200 billion dollars of goods. China retaliated. World trade, which had grown about twice as fast as world output in the 1990s, now grew no faster than output: the slowbalisation of the title. In 2019 world growth slowed to 3.0 percent, the weakest since 2009. India gained from cheap oil, adopted an inflation target and grew fast until 2018, then slowed sharply.

The oil price collapse

From 2011 to mid-2014 oil cost around 100 dollars a barrel or more. In the second half of 2014 the price fell, and it kept falling. Brent crude averaged 112 dollars in June 2014 and 31 dollars in January 2016, a fall of 73 percent (chart 5.1). In April 2015 the IMF attributed most of the fall to supply rather than to weak demand: steadily rising production outside the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), especially in the United States, where shale oil had come on stream; a faster than expected recovery in some troubled OPEC producers such as Iraq; and, above all, OPEC's decision in November 2014 to keep pumping at the same rate despite the falling price.1

Cheap oil moved income from producers to consumers. The IMF expected the advanced economies, as net importers, to benefit, and saw a chance for governments everywhere to cut fuel subsidies and reform energy taxes.1 For exporters it was a large loss of income. Russia's output fell by 2.0 percent in 2015, Brazil's by 3.5 percent in 2015 and 3.3 percent in 2016, and Nigeria's by 1.6 percent in 2016, all of them hit by falling commodity prices along with troubles at home. By October 2015 the IMF projected that growth in the emerging and developing economies would fall for the fifth year in a row.2 The boost to the importers was smaller than many had hoped. Lower prices pushed headline inflation in the rich countries further below target, and the IMF warned in October 2014 of "secular stagnation" in the advanced economies, a long spell of weak demand and low growth despite very low interest rates.3

China's wobble

China's growth was slowing by design and by necessity. On today's figures it fell from 7.5 percent in 2014 to 6.8 percent in 2016. The stimulus of 2009 had been financed by credit, and borrowing kept growing faster than the economy. Credit to Chinese firms and households, 106 percent of GDP at the end of 2008, reached 170 percent in 2014 and peaked at 191 percent in early 2016 (chart 5.5). As China shifted from building towards consumption, it bought fewer raw materials and machines from the rest of the world. In October 2015 the IMF judged that China's slowdown was in line with forecasts but that its effects abroad, through lower metal prices and weaker exports to China, were "greater than previously envisaged".2

In August 2015 the People's Bank of China announced that it would let market forces play a larger role in setting the exchange rate, and the renminbi fell by about 4 percent against the dollar.4 A small move by most standards, it still fed fears that China would export its slowdown through a cheaper currency. By December 2016 the renminbi had weakened to 6.92 per dollar, from 6.21 in July 2015. Markets fell again at the start of 2016. The IMF's April 2016 report traced the turmoil to doubts about how fast China was slowing and what its authorities intended, to very low oil prices, and to worries that the rich countries had run out of ways to respond. By mid-February markets had begun to recover.5 Later China tightened the rules on its "shadow banks", the lenders outside the formal banking system, and credit stopped rising faster than output. That too slowed growth, as the IMF noted in 2019.6

Money below zero

The European Central Bank had set a negative interest rate on bank deposits in June 2014 (Phase 4). In January 2016 the Bank of Japan followed, charging a negative rate on some of the reserves that banks held with it, and in March the European Central Bank (ECB) eased again, adding corporate bonds to its purchases.5 Long-term rates followed. The German ten-year bond yield, 1.26 percent in June 2014, first averaged below zero in June 2016 (chart 5.4). By October 2016 the IMF noted that a large stock of advanced-economy government bonds was trading at negative yields: investors were paying governments to hold their money.7

The United States went the other way, but slowly. The Federal Reserve raised its target range for the first time since the financial crisis in December 2015, to between ¼ and ½ percent. It reached 2¼ to 2½ percent by December 2018, and then reversed. In July 2019 it cut rates, citing "global developments" and muted inflation.8 By August 2019 the German ten-year yield had fallen to −0.65 percent and Japan's to −0.28 percent; the US ten-year yield averaged 1.63 percent. The ECB cut its deposit rate again in September 2019, to −0.5 percent, and resumed buying bonds.9 Rates this low were widely read as a sign that markets expected weak growth and low inflation for years ahead, the condition the IMF had in 2014 called a risk of "secular stagnation".

A brief upswing

For a while in 2017 it looked as if the world had finally shaken off the crisis. World growth rose to 3.9 percent on today's figures, the fastest of the phase. The advanced economies grew by 2.6 percent, up from 1.8 percent in 2016, the euro area by 2.7 percent and the emerging and developing economies by 4.8 percent. Trade revived with output: world trade volume grew by 5.4 percent in 2017, after 2.5 percent in 2016. India's Economic Survey of January 2018 described a recovery shared across the world, with oil up by about 16 percent during the year to around 61 dollars a barrel, and it named China's credit surge and trade tensions as the risks ahead. Even in this good year, it noted, world trade growth "remains less than half its level of a decade ago".10

The good year did not last. By the second half of 2018 growth had slowed markedly: the euro area lost momentum, and China slowed as it reined in its shadow banks and faced higher US tariffs.6 Protectionism had been building for some time before the tariffs. India's Survey of August 2017 counted 42 new trade-restricting measures by members of the Group of Twenty (G20) between mid-October 2016 and mid-May 2017, and linked the rising hostility to globalisation to the US election and the Brexit vote.12 In 2018 world growth slipped to 3.6 percent, and in 2019 it fell further.

Slowbalisation and the tariff war

For two decades trade had grown much faster than output. On the IMF's figures, the volume of world trade grew by 6.6 percent a year in the 1990s, more than twice world GDP growth of 3.0 percent, and by 7.3 percent a year from 2000 to 2007. From 2012 to 2019 it grew by 3.3 percent a year, no faster than output (chart 5.2). In October 2016 the IMF devoted a chapter to the question. It found that weak economic activity, especially weak investment, which is heavy in traded machinery, explained "up to three-fourths of the slowdown". The rest came from the fading pace of trade liberalisation, a recent rise in protectionism and slower growth of global supply chains, as firms stopped spreading production across more countries.7

Politics then made it worse. On 23 June 2016 British voters chose to leave the European Union. The IMF's chief economist wrote that the vote left the future of Britain's trade with the other 27 members unclear, and that it and the US election campaign showed "a fraying consensus" about the benefits of economic integration.7 Britain itself kept growing, though more slowly: by 1.6 percent in 2018 and 1.4 percent in 2019, against 3.3 percent in 2017 on today's figures. In 2018 the new US administration acted on that mood. In June the US Trade Representative announced a 25 percent duty on about 34 billion dollars of imports from China, collected from 6 July, after an investigation into Chinese practices on technology transfer and intellectual property. In September a further list of about 200 billion dollars of Chinese goods was taxed at 10 percent, with a rise to 25 percent scheduled.11 China retaliated with tariffs of its own. US customs duties as a share of goods imports, the effective tariff rate, were about 1.6 percent from 2010 to 2017 and rose to 3.5 percent by the end of 2019, the highest since 1990 (chart 5.3).

The effects showed up quickly. World trade volume grew by only 1.1 percent in 2019. In the first half of the year, the IMF reported, trade was just 1 percent above a year earlier, the slowest pace since 2012, and manufacturing had weakened to levels not seen since the financial crisis. It estimated that the US–China tariffs, counting their effect on confidence and markets, would leave world output about 0.8 percent lower in 2020 than it would otherwise have been. Its forecast for 2019 growth, 3.0 percent, was "the weakest since 2009".13 Part of the slowdown came from a handful of economies in distress: the IMF reckoned that Argentina, Iran, Türkiye and Venezuela accounted for about half the fall in world growth from 2017 to 2019.13 But the trend was general. The rich world grew by only 2.2 percent a year from 2014 to 2019, and Japan's economy shrank by 0.3 percent in 2019 on today's figures.

India: cheap oil, new rules and a slowdown

India buys most of its oil abroad, so the price collapse was a windfall. The Indian crude basket averaged 105.5 dollars a barrel in 2013-14 and 84.2 dollars in 2014-15, and fell by a further 46 percent in April–December 2015. The government took part of the gain in higher excise duties on petroleum products, whose yield rose by 90.5 percent in 2015-16. The Economic Survey of February 2016 described India as a "haven of stability and an outpost of opportunity" in a turbulent world.14 The bill for oil imports fell from 138 billion dollars in 2014-15 to 83 billion in 2015-16, the current account deficit narrowed to 0.6 percent of GDP in 2016-17, and the Centre's fiscal deficit came down from 4.5 percent of GDP in 2013-14 to 3.5 percent in 2016-17.

China's jolt reached India through the currency. The rupee had traded between 62 and 64 to the dollar until early August 2015. After the renminbi's devaluation on 11 August and the fall in Chinese share prices on 24 August it weakened past 66, and it touched 68.44 on 12 February 2016. The Survey's worst case was a large currency readjustment in Asia after a Chinese devaluation, setting off a round of competitive devaluations that might "resemble more the events of the early 1930s".14

India also changed how it ran monetary policy, as inflation fell with the price of oil. In February 2015 the government and the Reserve Bank signed an agreement on a monetary policy framework. The Reserve Bank of India Act was amended in May 2016 to give the Bank a formal inflation target: consumer price inflation of 4 percent, with a tolerance of 2 points either side, from August 2016 to March 2021, to be pursued by a new Monetary Policy Committee, which was set up in September 2016.15 Consumer price inflation fell from 9.5 percent in 2013-14 to 3.6 percent in 2017-18, and the repo rate was cut from 8 percent to 6 percent (chart 5.6). The cuts reached borrowers only in part: in 2015 the RBI cut by 1.25 points, but banks' lending rates fell by only about half a point.14

One reason was the state of the banks. The Survey of February 2016 gave the problem a name, the twin balance sheet problem: over-borrowed companies that could not repay, and public-sector banks weighed down by their bad loans.14 By September 2016 non-performing loans were about 9 percent of bank advances, and about 12 percent at the public-sector banks; the following Survey said the problem was "festering".15 On 8 November 2016 the government withdrew the 500 and 1,000 rupee notes, 86 percent of the cash in circulation, as legal tender. The Survey estimated that this demonetisation would cut growth in 2016-17 by between a quarter and half a percentage point.15 The goods and services tax was launched on 1 July 2017, large defaulters were sent to the new Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, and the public-sector banks were promised new capital of about 1.2 percent of GDP.16 On today's figures growth was 8.0 percent in 2015-16 and 8.3 percent in 2016-17, then 6.8 percent in 2017-18.

In 2018 the outside world turned against India again. The 2018 Survey had estimated that each 10-dollar rise in the price of oil cut India's growth by 0.2 to 0.3 points and widened its current account deficit by 9 to 10 billion dollars.16 In October 2018 crude rose above 80 dollars. The deficit widened to 2.6 percent of GDP in April–December 2018, and the rupee hit 74.4 to the dollar in October. The Survey of July 2019 blamed high oil prices, rising US interest rates, the US–China trade war, sanctions on Iran and outflows of portfolio money. The RBI raised its repo rate in June and August 2018 and then, in what the Survey called a "u-turn", cut it from February 2019 as the Fed paused.17 At home a new strain had appeared. Subsidiaries of Infrastructure Leasing & Financial Services (IL&FS) defaulted between June and September 2018. India's shadow banks, the non-bank finance companies, which relied on borrowed money (what the Survey called "public funds") for about 70 percent of their liabilities, found it harder to borrow, and in 2019 the housing financier DHFL defaulted too.1718

Growth fell sharply. The Survey of January 2020 put world growth in 2019 at 2.9 percent, the slowest since 2009, and India's at 4.8 percent in the first half of 2019-20. It traced the slowdown to a sharp fall in investment and to a financial system still working through the bad loans of the earlier boom, and the RBI cut its repo rate by 1.1 points over the year.18 On today's figures India grew by 6.5 percent in 2018-19 and only 3.9 percent in 2019-20, the weakest since the crisis year of 2008-09 on the back series.

Key terms

Slowbalisation
A period in which world trade grows no faster than world output.
Secular stagnation
A long period of weak demand and low growth even with very low interest rates.
Negative yield
A bond price so high that a buyer holding it to maturity gets back less than they paid.
Effective tariff rate
Customs duties collected as a share of the value of imports.
Twin balance sheet problem
Over-borrowed companies and the banks that lent to them, each weakening the other.
Inflation targeting
A framework in which the central bank is given a numerical target for inflation and sets interest rates to meet it.
