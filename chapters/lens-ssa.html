Sub-Saharan Africa

Debt relief and a commodity boom gave Sub-Saharan Africa its fastest growth in a generation in the 2000s. The oil-price collapse of 2014–16 ended it, a new wave of borrowing followed, and in the 2020s Zambia, Ghana and Ethiopia defaulted. Through it all the region's people grew more numerous, more connected and, relative to the rest of the world, poorer.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy, Theme 6: Development and Theme 9: Demography and labour

In brief

Sub-Saharan Africa, home to 1.32 billion people in 2025, produced 3.6 percent of world output at purchasing-power parity, against 2.8 percent in 2000.1 Growth averaged 5.6 percent a year from 2000 to 2014 and 2.8 percent from 2015 to 2025, so income per person was lower in 2025 than in 2014. Public debt, cut by debt relief to 21.0 percent of GDP in 2008, was back to 56.8 percent in 2025. By 2024 about seven in ten of the world's extremely poor people lived in the region.

Debt relief and the boom, 2000–2014

The century began with a clean slate. In 1996 the IMF and World Bank had launched the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) initiative, and in 2005 they added the Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative, which cancelled all eligible debts owed to the IMF, the World Bank and the African Development Fund by countries that completed HIPC; it took effect in January 2006.23 By 2009 the IMF counted US$73 billion of relief committed to 28 of the 30 eligible countries in the region.4 The region's public debt fell from 58.0 percent of GDP in 2000 to 21.0 percent in 2008; Zambia's fell from 261.0 percent to 18.9 percent in 2010 (chart SSA.2). Of the 37 countries that have now received full HIPC relief, 31 are in Africa.5

Commodity prices did the rest (see Phase 2). In April 2006 the IMF wrote that the region was heading for its best growth in over 30 years.6 Its 2007 regional report noted a third year in a row of growth of about 5 to 6 percent, debt relief freeing money for spending on the poor, and a dramatic rise in trade with Asia, above all China.7 Growth averaged 6.3 percent a year from 2004 to 2008, and 12.2 percent in oil-rich Angola. The global crisis barely interrupted it: in October 2009 the IMF expected growth of about 1 percent that year, but it came in at 4.1 percent.4

Investors noticed. Ghana sold its first ten-year Eurobond in 2007, Zambia its first in September 2012, and Côte d'Ivoire and Kenya theirs in 2014, when the region's issuance passed the record US$6.5 billion of 2013.89 Borrowing was still cheap to service: external debt service took 11.4 percent of export earnings in 2011, against 32.2 percent in 2000 (chart SSA.3). Ethiopia grew fastest of the large economies, averaging 9.1 percent a year from 2000 to 2014 on official figures, through a state-led plan of roads, railways, dams and factories; in 2014 the IMF doubted the official data and marked its own growth estimate down to 8–8½ percent, against the government's 11 percent.10 In the same year Nigeria rebased its national accounts from 1990 to 2010 prices, which raised the measured size of its economy, to 0.81 trillion dollars.11

Yet progress against poverty was slow. In 2014 the World Bank reckoned that the region had achieved only a third of the Millennium Development Goal of halving extreme poverty, which the world as a whole had already met.12

The bust and the new borrowing, 2014–2019

The halving of oil prices in 2014–15 (see Phase 5) hit the two biggest oil producers hardest. In Nigeria government revenue fell to 7.8 percent of GDP and exports by about 40 percent; the central bank held the naira close to a fixed rate and rationed foreign exchange, which the IMF said it did not support.13 Output shrank 1.6 percent in 2016. Angola's economy shrank every year from 2016 to 2019. Its new government let the kwanza float in January 2018, after which it lost 45 percent by October, and in December the IMF lent it US$3.7 billion; the IMF counted Angola's public debt at 91 percent of GDP in 2018, up from 39.8 percent in 2014.14 For the region, growth of 1.2 percent in 2016 was, in the IMF's words, "the lowest in two decades", and oil exporters' debt service rose from 8 percent of government revenue in 2013 to 57 percent in 2016.15

Debt kept rising, and more of it was owed to bond markets and Chinese lenders on harder terms than aid.16 The region's public debt rose to 44.4 percent of GDP by 2019, and Zambia's external public debt more than quadrupled between 2014 and 2019.17 The World Bank's count of the region's countries at high risk of debt distress or already in it rose from 25 percent in 2012 to 50 percent in 2019.16 By 2016 debt service again took 27.4 percent of export earnings.

Pandemic, defaults and the Common Framework, 2020–2026

The pandemic brought the region's first recession in a quarter-century: output fell 3.1 percent in 2020, close to the IMF's October forecast of a contraction that would be "still the worst outcome on record".1819 The Group of Twenty (G20) first suspended debt payments, which deferred them without reducing them,18 and in November 2020 agreed with the Paris Club a Common Framework for restructuring the debts of poor countries. A debtor would need an IMF programme, private creditors would have to give comparable treatment, and in principle "debt treatments will not be conducted in the form of debt write-off or cancellation".203 Zambia defaulted on its Eurobonds that same month.17

Three defaults tested the Framework. Zambia, which applied in January 2021, had total public debt of US$33.8 billion, 133 percent of GDP on the IMF's wide definition, at the end of 2021; it signed a memorandum with its official creditors in June 2024, exchanged its Eurobonds, and by 2025 had agreements covering about 94 percent of the debt being restructured.172122 Ghana lost access to markets in late 2021; inflation reached 54 percent in December 2022, when it suspended payments on its external commercial debt and swapped most of its domestic bonds for new ones; it took a US$3 billion IMF loan in May 2023 and reached agreement with its official creditors in January 2024.2324 Ethiopia asked for treatment in February 2021 and defaulted when it missed a US$33 million coupon in December 2023. In July 2024 it took an IMF loan of about US$3.4 billion and let its currency float, as the IMF concluded that "the public investment-led growth model has reached its limits".25

Elsewhere, borrowers went to the brink without defaulting. With no government in the region able to issue a Eurobond between spring 2022 and early 2023,26 Kenya faced a US$2 billion bond falling due in June 2024; it sold a new one in February and bought back most of the old. That June, protests against the tax increases in its Finance Bill, in which people died, forced the president to withdraw the bill.27 In Nigeria the government removed fuel subsidies from 2023, stopped central-bank financing of the budget and freed the exchange rate. Inflation reached 33.2 percent in 2024 and, as the naira fell, the IMF's estimate of dollar income per person halved, from US$1,597 in 2023 to US$806 in 2024.28 The region's inflation peaked at 20.9 percent in 2024 (chart SSA.4).

By 2025 the IMF saw growth of about 4.5 percent, "the fastest in a decade", and upgrades to the credit ratings of Ghana, Zambia and South Africa; Eurobond sales reached US$14 billion.29 But more than a third of the region's countries were still at high risk of debt distress or in it, and in 2024, 20 of 48 countries "paid more in debt service than for healthcare and education combined".2916 Aid was falling too: bilateral aid fell by about a quarter in 2025, and the war in the Middle East in 2026 raised the prices of fuel, fertiliser and shipping.3029 In October 2026 the IMF still expected growth of 4.5 percent in 2026 and 4.5 percent in 2027.30

Poverty, people and phones

Measured against the rest of the world, the region fell behind. Its income per person at purchasing-power parity was 28 percent of the world average in 2000 and 23 percent in 2025 (chart SSA.5).

The share of people living on less than $3 a day fell from 62.4 percent in 2000 to 45.3 percent in 2014, then hardly moved: it was 45.0 percent in 2024 (chart SSA.6). Because the population grew so fast, the number of poor people rose, and as poverty fell elsewhere, especially in South Asia, the region's share of the world's extremely poor rose from about 19 percent in 2000 to about 70 percent in 2024.31

The population almost doubled, from 0.68 billion in 2000 to 1.32 billion in 2025, and its share of the world's people rose from 11.0 to 16.1 percent. Fertility fell from 5.74 to 4.26 births per woman, still about twice the world rate. On projections cited by the World Bank, Nigeria alone will grow from 213 million to 791 million people by 2100, second only to India.32

Technology spread faster than income. Mobile phone subscriptions rose from 1.7 per 100 people in 2000 to 90 in 2025, and by 2024, 40 percent of adults had a mobile money account, up from 27 percent in 2021, making mobile money, in the World Bank's words, "a Sub-Saharan African success story".33 The countries also agreed to trade more with each other. The African Continental Free Trade Area was signed in Kigali on 21 March 2018, entered into force on 30 May 2019 and began trading on 1 January 2021; by May 2026, 49 of the African Union's 55 members had ratified it.3435

Contested question

Is Africa rising?

Yes: the fast growers keep growing

Several economies have grown fast for two decades: Ethiopia averaged 8.6 percent a year from 2000 to 2025 on official figures, Rwanda 7.5 percent and Tanzania 6.2 percent, and Côte d'Ivoire 6.2 percent since 2015. In 2026 the IMF expected the economies that do not depend on natural resources to grow 6.0 percent.30 Ghana and Zambia restructured their debts and grew faster than the IMF had expected, more adults rely on mobile money than in any other region, and the free-trade area is in force.33

No: the region as a whole is falling behind

Average income per person was lower in 2025 than in 2014 and fell from 29 to 23 percent of the world average over those years. The IMF expects income per person to grow 2.3 percent a year over the medium term, against 3.6 percent in other emerging and developing economies.29 The three largest economies of the 2010s, Nigeria, South Africa and Angola,14 grew 1.5, 0.7 and 0.9 percent a year from 2015 to 2025, and the region now holds most of the world's extreme poor. Angola still earned 95 percent of its exports from oil in 2024, and the IMF judged that "oil dependence remains a drag on medium-term growth".36

How the evidence weighs up. Both are true, for different countries. The resource exporters that dominate the regional average, and much of the region's debt trouble, have stagnated since 2014; a group of smaller, mostly non-resource economies has kept growing. Convergence depends on the second group growing large enough to lift the average, and on debt being restructured faster than in the 2020s.

India and Africa

In 2000 India was poorer than Sub-Saharan Africa: its income per person at purchasing-power parity was 0.85 times the African average. India passed the average in 2009, and by 2025 its income per person, 10,039 dollars at 2021 prices, was 2.0 times Africa's (chart SSA.7). India's share of world output rose from 3.9 to 8.2 percent over the quarter-century, while the region's rose from 2.8 to 3.6 percent. The poverty gap opened the same way: India's extreme-poverty rate was 2.6 percent in 2023, against 45.0 percent in Sub-Saharan Africa in 2024.

On some measures India long trailed Africa. The Economic Survey of 2015-16 noted that 61 percent of rural Indians defecated in the open in 2015, against 32 percent of rural people in Sub-Saharan Africa.37 The Survey of 2004-05 noted India's reputation as a low-cost producer of drugs against HIV/AIDS for patients in Africa.38

Africa became a market India pursued on purpose. The export-import policy of 2002–07 launched a "Focus Africa" programme, extended in 2003-04 to the region's remaining 11 countries.3940 Under a later scheme, 22 of the 41 countries whose markets earned exporters an extra duty credit were African.41 From 2008 India gave the least developed countries duty-free access to most of its market; by 2014 the scheme covered 96.4 percent of tariff lines, and 21 of its 31 beneficiaries were in Africa.42 Africa's share of India's exports rose from 6.7 percent in 2004-05 to 9.9 percent in 2013-14.43

Nigeria was the anchor. Its crude oil made it the source of 4.4 percent of India's imports in April–October 2006 and India's 14th-largest trading partner in 2011-12, with India running a large deficit.4441 After the United States raised tariffs on Indian goods to 50 percent in 2025, the 2025-26 Survey described pharmaceutical exports as redirected: in April–November 2025 exports of drug formulations rose 58.0 percent to Nigeria and 50.6 percent to Tanzania, and Nigeria's share of India's crude imports rose from 2.2 to 3.3 percent.45 India's first trade agreement of the 2020s was with Mauritius, signed in February 2021, and the Survey of 2023-24 named African markets as a target of the new agreements.46

India also lends. Government-backed lines of credit run through the Export-Import Bank of India; by October 2026, 201 of the 295 signed lines, worth about US$9.8 billion of US$25.5 billion, were with African countries, 12 of them, worth US$712 million, with Ethiopia (this book's sums from the bank's list).47 At least 75 percent of the value of the contracts they finance must be sourced from India. And in 2023 the BRICS group, which India helped found, invited Ethiopia to join from 2024.48

The deepest contrast is in population. India's fertility rate fell from 3.35 births per woman in 2000 to 1.96 in 2024; Sub-Saharan Africa's fell from 5.74 to 4.26 (chart SSA.8). People of working age, 15 to 64, made up 68.4 percent of India's population in 2025 and 56.4 percent of Africa's. India's demographic dividend has arrived; Africa's is still to come. Of the 1.2 billion young people the World Bank expects to reach working age in developing economies by 2035, more than 330 million will be in Sub-Saharan Africa.49

Key terms

HIPC
The Heavily Indebted Poor Countries initiative, launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996 to cut the debts of the poorest countries to a level they could manage.
MDRI
The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative of 2005, which cancelled all eligible debts owed to the IMF, the World Bank and the African Development Fund by countries that had completed HIPC.
Common Framework
The G20's framework, agreed with the Paris Club in November 2020, for restructuring the debts of low-income countries with their official creditors in the G20 and the Paris Club together, and with comparable treatment from private creditors.
Eurobond
Here, a bond sold by a government on international markets in a foreign currency, usually dollars.
Mobile money
An account held with a mobile phone company, not a bank, used to store, send and receive money by phone.
AfCFTA
The African Continental Free Trade Area, signed in 2018 and trading since 2021, which aims to remove most tariffs on trade among African Union members.
