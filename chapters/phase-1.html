Part I · Phase 1 of 9

The bust and the turn

The century opened with the end of a stock market bubble. The US recession that followed was short and mild, because central banks cut rates fast. The larger events of these years were quieter: China joined the World Trade Organization, and the emerging economies, India among them, began to pull away.

January 2000 – December 2002 · about 20 minutes · Next: The great boom

What happened

In March 2000 the Nasdaq, the US stock index dominated by technology firms, reached its peak. By October 2002 it had lost 74 percent of its value, and the United States had a recession from March to November 2001. The Federal Reserve cut its policy rate from 6.5 percent to 1.75 percent during 2001. The attacks of 11 September 2001 hit confidence but did not turn the slowdown into a slump. World growth fell from 4.8 percent in 2000 to 2.5 percent in 2001, and the IMF judged that this was not a full global recession. Argentina was the exception: it defaulted on its debt in December 2001, gave up its dollar peg the next month and lost nearly a fifth of its output. In the same month China joined the World Trade Organization. Its share of world exports, 3.8 percent in 2000, began a climb that would reach 10.3 percent by 2010. India grew slowly, by about 4 percent a year, but its current account moved into surplus and its reserves began to pile up.

The bubble bursts

In December 1996 the chairman of the Federal Reserve, Alan Greenspan, had asked in a speech how a central bank could tell "when irrational exuberance has unduly escalated asset values".1 Shares kept rising for three more years. On one long-run measure, the cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio, US shares in December 1999 were priced at 44.2 times their average real earnings of the previous ten years. That was the highest reading in the series, which starts in 1881, and three times its average of 14.8 before 1996. The most extreme prices were in technology. The Nasdaq Composite, which averaged 1,570 in January 1998, averaged 4,803 in March 2000 (chart 1.1).

The IMF saw the danger. Its World Economic Outlook of May 2000 described the US expansion as the longest on record, but warned that very high valuations, particularly of technology and biotechnology shares, carried the risk of a large correction. It modelled a downside case with a 25 percent fall in US equities and a fall of almost 20 percent in the dollar.2 The fall came, and it was deeper than that. By October 2002 the Nasdaq averaged 1,242, 74 percent below its peak. The broader S&P 500 index fell by 44 percent between August 2000 and February 2003, on monthly averages.

The bust exposed fraud as well as optimism. In April 2002 the IMF noted the weaknesses in accounting and auditing that the recent collapse of Enron, a large energy-trading firm, had brought to light, and worried that other firms' finances might also prove weaker than they looked.3 By September 2002 share prices had fallen sharply again since the end of March, partly because of further accounting and governance scandals. The IMF thought the "irrational exuberance" of the late 1990s had by then largely been wrung out of valuations, but that the scandals had damaged confidence.3

A short recession and a fast response

A second blow came from oil. As the world economy boomed in 1999 and 2000, the price of Brent crude rose from an average of 13 dollars a barrel in 1998 to 18 dollars in 1999 and 29 dollars in 2000. In October 2000 the IMF called higher oil prices an increasing concern. It put the rise down to supply constraints among producers and strong world demand, and reckoned that each extra 5 dollars a barrel added about 40 billion dollars a year to the advanced economies' net oil import bill.4 The Fed and the European Central Bank (ECB) had both raised rates through 2000 (chart 1.3). So when share prices fell, the world economy was already losing speed, and the oil importers among the emerging economies, India among them, faced larger import bills. Prices eased as demand weakened, to 25 dollars in 2001.

The US economy peaked in March 2001 and reached its low point in November 2001, according to the committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research that dates American business cycles. At eight months it was a short recession, and it ended a ten-year expansion.5 As in earlier downturns, business investment and inventories fell. Consumer spending held up, supported by lower interest rates, tax cuts, strong wage growth and rising house prices.6 On today's figures US output grew by 1.0 percent in 2001, after 4.1 percent in 2000 (chart 1.2). Unemployment rose from 3.8 percent in April 2000 to 5.5 percent in November 2001, and it kept rising after the recession had officially ended, to 6.3 percent in June 2003.

The policy response was fast (chart 1.3). The Fed had raised its target rate to 6.5 percent in May 2000. It began cutting in January 2001 and by December had cut by 4.75 percentage points, to 1.75 percent. It went on cutting, to 1.0 percent in June 2003. The European Central Bank moved later and less: from 4.75 percent in October 2000 to 3.25 percent at the end of 2001 and 2.0 percent by June 2003. The Bank of Japan had no room left: its rate was already a quarter of a percent. In March 2001 it stopped targeting an interest rate at all and set a target instead for the reserves that banks held with it, an early form of quantitative easing.7 In the United States, tax cuts enacted in June 2001 added a fiscal push.6 In May 2001 the IMF thought that, given the rapid response of the Fed and other central banks, there was "a reasonable prospect" that the slowdown would be short-lived.7

Then came the attacks of 11 September 2001. The IMF's December 2001 forecast said they had made an already very difficult situation worse: confidence fell around the world and spending in the United States dropped sharply for a time.8 Its forecast for world growth in 2001 fell with each issue, from 4.2 percent in October 2000 to 3.2 percent in May 2001 and 2.4 percent in December. Japan was in what the IMF called its fourth recession of the decade, and, on today's figures, German output shrank by 0.2 percent in 2002 and 0.5 percent in 2003.

But the shock passed quickly. By April 2002 the IMF judged that the slowdown "has bottomed out" and that the effects of 11 September had been more moderate than feared. It concluded that, if the recovery held, the downturn "would probably not qualify as a full-fledged global recession".6 On today's figures world output grew by 2.5 percent in 2001 and 2.8 percent in 2002. The advanced economies grew by only 1.6 and 1.7 percent. The emerging and developing economies grew by 3.8 and 4.4 percent, and China by 8.3 and 9.2 percent. The IMF had noticed in December 2001 that China, and to a lesser extent India, were less exposed to the slump in the rich countries than others.8

Argentina's collapse

The worst crisis of these years was in Argentina. Since 1991 a convertibility law had fixed the peso at one US dollar. It brought prices under control quickly, and the IMF's own evaluators later called it "enormously successful" at that. But it left the country unable to devalue when Brazil, its biggest neighbour, devalued in early 1999. Argentina slid into recession, its public debt kept rising, and in the autumn of 2000 it lost access to private lending. Between January and September 2001 the IMF raised its commitments to Argentina to 22 billion dollars. In December it declined to complete a review of the programme, cutting off its support. Within a month the President had resigned, the government had defaulted on its debt, and in early January 2002 the peg was abandoned.9

The peso collapsed. Against a basket of its trading partners' currencies it lost 72 percent of its value between December 2001 and June 2002 (chart 1.4). Output fell by 10.9 percent in 2002, after three years of decline, and in 2002 it was 18 percent below its 1998 level. Unemployment reached 22.4 percent. The IMF's September 2002 report described "an almost unprecedented collapse in economic activity" outside countries in war or post-communist transition.10 Yet the crisis spread less than earlier emerging-market crises had. In April 2002 the IMF observed that contagion had so far been limited, because the default had been widely expected and because little money was still flowing in.10 Uruguay and Brazil came under pressure in mid-2002, as the IMF reported in September,10 but the cascade of 1997–98 in Asia was not repeated. The IMF's independent evaluators concluded in 2004 that the Fund had lent too long to a policy it doubted, and that delay had raised the final cost.9

The turn: China joins the world economy

The event of these years that mattered most for the next two decades drew few headlines at the time. On 10 November 2001, at its meeting in Doha, Qatar, the World Trade Organization's ministerial conference approved China's accession; China became a member on 11 December 2001.11 The same meeting launched a new round of global trade negotiations, the Doha round.12 In April 2002 the IMF expected membership to raise China's imports, especially of machinery, faster than its exports, and to add competitive pressure for reform of its farms, factories and banks.6

It turned out otherwise: exports were the story. China's merchandise exports, 249 billion dollars in 2000, were 438 billion by 2003. Its share of world exports rose from 3.8 percent in 2000 to 5.7 percent in 2003 and 8.6 percent in 2007 (chart 1.5). The IMF had expected imports to lead; in the event exports did, and China's growing trade surpluses became part of the global imbalances of Phase 2.

Two other changes in 2002 would shape later phases. On 1 January 2002 euro notes and coins replaced the national currencies of the euro-area countries; the euro itself had existed since 1999 as an accounting currency.13 And with rates in the United States already low and Japan stuck in falling prices, central bankers began to talk about deflation. In November 2002 Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor, set out how a central bank could fight it even with interest rates at zero, though he judged the chance of significant US deflation "extremely small".14 The policy of cheap money that began here would run, with breaks, for most of the next two decades. In September 2002 the IMF was already warning about one of its side effects: the US current account deficit, financed by the rest of the world (4.1 percent of GDP in 2002 on today's figures).15

India: a slowdown, and a quiet turn

India began the decade slowing. The Economic Survey of February 2002 opened by saying that "the Indian economy is passing through a difficult phase". It blamed two poor farm years at home and a world slowdown made worse by the attacks of 11 September.16 On the figures of the time, growth had fallen from 6.1 percent in 1999-2000 to 4.0 percent in 2000-01; it was later revised to 4.4 percent. On today's GDP series, which has been rebuilt on a later base, growth was 3.8 percent in 2000-01, 4.8 percent in 2001-02 and 3.8 percent in 2002-03. Industrial growth in April–December 2001 was, the Survey said, the lowest in ten years. Business investment had stalled, and it saw no recovery in sight for 2001-02.16

The American bust reached India through two channels. The first was the stock market. The BSE Sensex, the main index of the Bombay Stock Exchange, fell by 36 percent between the end of March 2000 and 12 April 2001, which the Survey put down to fears of a global slowdown and to irregularities in the market at home. After 11 September it fell again, to 2,600 on 21 September, and foreign portfolio investors were net sellers that month, the only month between April 2001 and January 2002 in which they were.16 The second channel was software. India's software exports had grown by about 52 percent a year in the five years to 1999-2000, and in 2000-01 they grew by another 57 percent, to 6.3 billion dollars; about three-fifths of them went to North America. In 2001-02 growth slowed to 13 percent in dollar terms, at a time, the 2003 Survey noted, of a "marked slowdown in IT trade world over".17 Neither Survey mentioned the dot-com collapse by name. Both noticed something new: firms abroad were starting to send work such as accounting, call-centre services, insurance claims and medical transcription to India.17

Otherwise India was, as the IMF had said, relatively sheltered. The 2002 Survey agreed that "China and India are expected to remain relatively insulated from the global slowdown", because trade was a small part of their economies, though it warned that a more open India would not stay insulated for long.16 Exports did stall: they grew by only 0.6 percent in April–December 2001, after 21 percent the year before. The bigger external shock had come earlier, from oil. Brent crude rose from about 10 dollars a barrel in February 1999 to about 33 dollars in November 2000, and India's oil import bill went from 6.4 billion dollars in 1998-99 to 15.6 billion in 2000-01. Reserves fell until October–November 2000, when a special deposit scheme, the India Millennium Deposits, brought in 5.5 billion dollars.16

Policy eased, within limits. The Reserve Bank cut its Bank Rate to 6.5 percent in October 2001, the lowest since 1973, and lowered the cash reserve ratio from 8 percent to 5.5 percent during 2001; in 2002 it cut both again, to 6.25 and 4.75 percent. It stated a preference for "softer interest rates".16 Fiscal policy had no room to help. The Centre's deficit reached 5.9 percent of GDP in 2001-02, against a budgeted 4.7 percent, and with the states the total was about 10 percent. The 2002 Survey called this "a situation not dissimilar to that prevailing in the early 1990s". A fiscal responsibility bill to cap the Centre's deficits had been introduced in December 2000.18

Then came the turn. In 2001-02 the current account showed a surplus of 1.35 billion dollars, 0.3 percent of GDP, for the first time since 1977-78. A year earlier the Survey had expected a deficit. Exports were flat, but imports fell, mainly oil, and net earnings from services and remittances were the highest in a decade.18 Net foreign investment was the highest in four years. Reserves rose from 42 billion dollars at the end of March 2001 to 54 billion a year later and 76 billion by March 2003 (chart 1.6). By January 2003 the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI's) net foreign assets exceeded the whole of its reserve money, which the Survey likened to a currency board.18 The 2002 monsoon was the weakest since 1987-88 and farm output fell sharply, yet inflation stayed low, because the government had large stocks of grain to release.18 The Tenth Plan (2002–07) aimed for growth of 8 percent a year, and the Survey cited China as proof that such a rate could be sustained.18 In 2003-04 growth jumped to 7.9 percent. India's boom had begun.

Key terms

Cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio (CAPE)
A share price divided by average real earnings over the previous ten years.
Currency board (convertibility)
A rule that fixes the exchange rate and backs the money supply with foreign currency.
Sovereign default
A government's failure to pay its debts as agreed.
Quantitative easing
A central bank creating money to buy assets when its interest rate is near zero.
Deflation
A sustained fall in the general price level.
Cash reserve ratio (CRR)
The share of deposits that Indian banks must keep with the Reserve Bank.
Foreign exchange reserves
Foreign currency, gold and similar assets held by the central bank.
