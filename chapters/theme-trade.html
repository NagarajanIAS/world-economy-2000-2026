Trade

In the first years of this century trade grew far faster than the world economy, carried by China's entry into the world trading system and by factories spread across borders. After 2011 it stopped outrunning output. Since then a pandemic, a war and a return of tariffs on a scale not seen for generations have tested it. This chapter traces what drove the boom, what ended it, and why trade has proved harder to stop than many expected.

In brief

From 2000 to 2007 the volume of world trade grew by 7.3 percent a year, against 4.3 percent for world output. China joined the World Trade Organization at the end of 2001, and its share of the world's goods exports rose from 3.8 percent in 2000 to 14.3 percent in 2025. After the financial crisis the engine slowed: from 2012 trade grew no faster than output, as investment weakened, China made more of its own parts and the spread of cross-border supply chains stalled. Trade in services kept growing. Trade fell sharply in 2009 and 2020 and recovered quickly both times. Geopolitics then took a larger role, and the United States raised tariffs, first on China in 2018 and then on almost everyone in 2025. The customs duties it collected reached 11.3 percent of its goods imports late in 2025, the highest since the series began in 1959. Yet world trade grew by about 5 percent in 2025. India's story is different: it is a large exporter of services and a small exporter of goods, and it buys more from China than it sells to the United States.

The great expansion

Trade had been growing faster than output for decades. In 1985–2007, the IMF calculated, world trade grew on average twice as fast as world GDP.1 The years after 2000 continued the pattern (chart T2.1). The volume of trade, which strips out changes in prices, grew by 7.3 percent a year in 2000–2007, about 1.7 times as fast as world output. Measured against GDP, exports plus imports rose from 50.1 percent of world output in 2000 to 60.3 percent in 2008 (chart T2.2).

Two forces did most of the work. The first was China. It became a member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) on 11 December 2001,2 and cut its own tariffs. Its average applied tariff fell from 14.7 percent in 2000 to 4.9 percent in 2005 (chart T2.4). Its share of world goods exports more than doubled between 2000 and 2007, to 8.6 percent. It passed the United States in 2007 and Germany in 2009, and by 2025 Japan's share had fallen from 7.4 to 2.8 percent (chart T2.3). Trade with China was not costless for everyone. The IMF later cited research finding that US local labour markets more exposed to Chinese imports suffered higher unemployment and lower wages, and warned that such costs of adjustment "should not be underestimated".1

The second force was the global value chain. Cheaper transport and communications let companies split production into stages and place each where it was cheapest: a phone, for example, might be designed in one country, use chips made in a second and be assembled in a third. Parts crossed borders several times, so the same final good generated far more trade than before. The World Bank's 2020 World Development Report found that trade in global value chains had grown fastest from 1990 to 2007 and accounted for almost half of all trade. It credited them with an unprecedented convergence of poor countries on rich ones, with Bangladesh, China and Vietnam as examples, and estimated that a 1 percent rise in a country's participation in value chains raised its income per person by more than 1 percent, five times the gain from ordinary trade.3 Tariffs elsewhere were falling too: India's average applied tariff fell from 23.4 percent in 2000 to 6.0 percent in 2008, and Brazil's from 12.7 percent to under 7 percent.

Slowbalisation

The financial crisis produced the sharpest fall in trade in the period: the volume of world trade fell by 10.2 percent in 2009 (Phase 3). It rebounded in 2010, but from 2012 it settled into a slower rhythm. In 2012–2019 it grew by 3.3 percent a year, slightly less than world output at 3.4 percent. Trade as a share of world GDP drifted between about 54 and 59 percent. A new word, slowbalisation, came into use for the era (Phase 5).

The IMF's explanation, in 2016, was mainly weak demand. Investment, which uses far more imported machinery and materials than consumption does, had slowed, especially in China as it shifted towards consumption. Weaker economic activity explained up to three-quarters of the slowdown in import growth. The rest came from a slower pace of trade liberalisation, a creeping rise in protection and the levelling off of value chains: since 2011, it found, participation in them "seems to have leveled off".1 The World Bank's account, four years later, put more weight on structure. The splitting of production had matured; China was now making more of its own parts, so imported inputs fell from about half of the value of its goods exports in the 1990s to a little over 30 percent in 2015; and there had been no new wave of liberalisation since the Doha Round stalled.3 The WTO, looking back in 2023, agreed that the flat ratio of trade to GDP after 2008–09 did not reflect trade tensions, since trade costs had kept falling, but slower unbundling of production.4 The three accounts fit together; they differ on weight. Either way, the IMF expected that trade would not regain the pace of the boom, when investment in China and other emerging economies had been unusually high and value chains were spreading fast.1

Services rise

The slowdown was a slowdown in goods. Services, from software and finance to tourism and engineering design, were more resilient. The IMF found that in 2012–2015 the volume of services trade grew by about 5½ percent a year, while goods trade grew by just under 3 percent.1 The WTO's 2019 World Trade Report, devoted to services, found that services trade had grown by 5.4 percent a year in 2005–2017 against 4.6 percent for goods, and that services accounted for close to half of the value added in world trade once their contribution to manufactured goods was counted. It described services as "the backbone of the global economy".5 Services rose from 20.7 percent of world exports of goods and services in 2008 to 27.3 percent in 2025. Much of the growth was in things that can be delivered over a wire. India was a prominent example: the WTO counted it the second-largest exporter of information technology services in 2017, after the European Union.5

Pandemic, war and blocs

The pandemic was expected to break trade. It did not. At the low point, in the second quarter of 2020, goods trade was 12.2 percent below its pre-pandemic level and services trade 21.4 percent below, because travel stopped. But goods trade was back to its pre-pandemic level by October 2021, much faster than after the financial crisis, as households stuck at home bought goods instead of services (Phase 6). The IMF found that lockdowns in trading partners explained up to 60 percent of the fall in imports in the first half of 2020, but that these spillovers were short-lived. Value chains had proved adaptable, and policies "such as reshoring are likely misguided"; countries would be safer diversifying their suppliers, including away from domestic ones.6

The bigger change was political. The IMF dated the slowdown of integration to the years after the financial crisis, and noted that foreign direct investment had fallen from 3.3 percent of world GDP in the 2000s to 1.3 percent in 2018–2022. By 2023, after Russia's invasion of Ukraine (Phase 7), it was studying geoeconomic fragmentation: governments steering trade and investment towards friendly countries, especially in strategic sectors such as semiconductors. A split into blocs, it estimated, could lower world output by up to 2 percent in the long run, with emerging economies hit hardest.7 The WTO put the cost of splitting the trading system into two blocs at about 5 percent of global real income. It found that since the war in Ukraine trade between hypothetical blocs had grown 4–6 percent more slowly than trade within them, but judged that claims of deglobalisation were "still greatly exaggerated".4

The return of tariffs

For most of the period the United States collected about 1.6 percent of the value of its goods imports in customs duties (the effective tariff rate, chart T2.5). That began to change in 2018. In July the United States imposed 25 percent tariffs on about $34 billion of imports from China, and in September 10 percent on a further $200 billion.8 The effective rate roughly doubled, to 3.1 percent in 2019. The World Bank warned that if the conflict escalated, global income could fall by up to $1.4 trillion and more than 30 million people could be pushed into poverty.3

The second round was much larger. After a series of measures against Canada, China and Mexico from February 2025, on 2 April the United States announced a minimum tariff of 10 percent on almost all imports and higher rates for about 60 countries. The IMF estimated that the average US tariff rate implied by the announced measures had jumped from under 3 percent in January to about 25 percent by mid-April, a level "not seen in a century", and cut its forecast for world trade growth in 2025 by 1½ percentage points.9 Many of the higher rates were then paused or negotiated down, and by October 2025 the IMF put that announced rate at about 19 percent.10 The duties actually collected lagged behind, because importers had stockpiled ahead of the tariffs, some rates were paused and goods were rerouted;10 in December 2025 the collected rate was about half the announced one.11 Measured this way, it rose from 2.6 percent in 2024 to 11.3 percent in the last quarter of 2025, the highest since the series began, then eased to 6.8 percent by the third quarter of 2026, as court rulings and executive actions lowered the statutory rate.11

World trade did not collapse. Firms front-loaded shipments ahead of the tariffs, retaliation was limited,10 and a boom in trade in the equipment for artificial intelligence lifted Asian exports. The IMF estimates that world trade grew by 5.0 percent in 2025, faster than output. The WTO's narrower measure, goods only, shows 4.2 percent; it found that equipment for artificial intelligence, such as chips and servers, accounted for nearly half of the growth in world goods trade in the first half of 2026.12 The tariffs changed where trade went more than how much there was. US imports from China fell by 29 percent in 2025, and China's share of US imports fell to 9.3 percent, from more than 20 percent before 2018; the WTO now sees US–China decoupling, rather than a wider split into blocs, as the main source of divergence in trade.12 The IMF projects world trade growth of 4.6 percent in 2026, despite the war in the Middle East (Phase 9), and its advice is to diversify trade rather than bring production home, which it judges not viable for most countries.13

India: services star, goods laggard

India opened up later than East Asia, and in its own way. Its average applied tariff fell from 23.4 percent in 2000 to 4.6 percent in 2022 (chart T2.4); on a different measure, the average tariff on goods that pay duty, the Economic Survey reports a fall from 48.9 percent to 17.3 percent between 2000 and 2024.14 Trade rose from 26.9 percent of India's GDP in 2000 to 53.4 percent in 2008 (chart T2.2). By 2011, the 2016-17 Survey noted, India was more open by this measure than China, a sharp turn for a country that had traded far less than its size predicted before 1991. The same Survey warned that a world turning against globalisation might not have the "carrying capacity" for another China-sized export boom.15

India's comparative advantage turned out to lie in services (chart T2.6). Its share of world services exports rose from 1.0 percent in 2000 to 4.3 percent in 2025; information and communication technology services made up 48.6 percent of them in 2025. The Survey ranks India the world's seventh-largest exporter of services.16 In goods its share rose only from 0.7 to 1.7 percent, against China's 14.3 percent; on World Bank data Vietnam, a far smaller economy, exported slightly more goods than India in 2025. Services exports reached $421 billion in 2025-26; merchandise exports, the latest year in this series, were $438 billion in 2024-25. Services have long cushioned the current account.17

The weakness in goods worried policymakers. The 2019-20 Survey argued that the US–China trade war gave India an unprecedented chance to follow China into labour-intensive assembly of "network products", such as phones, computers and cars, and set a target of 3.5 percent of world exports by 2025 and 6 percent by 2030. It warned against "misplaced insecurity on the trade front".17 The government took a more cautious line. In November 2019 India declined to join the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), the Asia-Pacific trade agreement that includes China, saying the deal did not address its outstanding concerns.18 The Survey published two months later found that India's free trade agreements had, on balance, raised its trade surplus with partners, rejecting the view that they had failed.17 By the 2024-25 Survey, tariffs were again described as a legitimate tool of industrial policy, to be used with care.14

The dependence on China grew regardless (chart T2.7). India's imports from China rose from $4.1 billion in 2003-04 to $131.6 billion in 2025-26, and have exceeded its exports to the United States, its largest market, in every year since 2007-08. Those exports, $87.3 billion in 2025-26, made India vulnerable in 2025. After a reciprocal tariff of 25 percent, the United States added a penal tariff of another 25 percent in August, leaving most Indian goods facing an effective rate of 50 percent, among the highest of any country. Exports of gems and jewellery to the United States fell by 44 percent in April–November 2025, though total exports held up.16 By April 2026 the US tariff on most Indian goods had been cut from 50 percent to 10 percent.11

India's response was to sign trade agreements rather than retreat. By early 2026 it had concluded or signed agreements with the United Kingdom, Oman and New Zealand, had one with the European Free Trade Association that came with a $100 billion investment commitment, and had concluded negotiations with the European Union after three years of talks.16 The Foreign Trade Policy of 2023 aims for exports of $2 trillion by 2030;19 total exports of goods and services were $825 billion in 2024-25.16 Reaching the target would take growth of exports far faster than world trade is expected to grow, which is why the question the 2019-20 Survey posed, whether India can become a large exporter of goods as well as services, still matters.

Key terms

Trade volume
The quantity of goods and services traded, after removing changes in prices.
Global value chain
Production split across countries, with parts and services crossing borders, often several times, before a finished product reaches its buyer.
Applied tariff
The import duty a country actually charges, which may be below the maximum it has promised the WTO.
Effective tariff rate
Customs duties collected as a share of the value of imports.
Slowbalisation
A period in which world trade grows no faster than world output.
Fragmentation
The splitting of the world economy into rival blocs shaped by geopolitics.
Free trade agreement
A treaty to remove most tariffs on trade among its members.
