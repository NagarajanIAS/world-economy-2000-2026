Part I · Phase 7 of 9

Inflation, tightening, war and energy

In 2022 prices rose faster in the rich world than at any time since the early 1980s. Russia's invasion of Ukraine sent the price of energy and food soaring, and central banks raised interest rates faster than in a generation. Many forecasters expected a recession. What came instead was a banking scare, a long squeeze on living standards and, by the end of 2023, a slowdown softer than almost anyone had predicted.

January 2022 – December 2023 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: Pandemic shock and response · Next: AI, tariffs and fragmentation

What happened

Prices, already rising fast at the end of 2021, kept climbing. Consumer prices were 9.0 percent higher than a year earlier in the United States in June 2022, and 10.6 percent higher in the euro area in October. On 24 February 2022 Russia invaded Ukraine. The price of gas in Europe rose to 15.6 times its 2019 average, and the price of food jumped. Central banks raised interest rates quickly: the Federal Reserve's policy rate rose by 5.26 percentage points in 16 months. The dollar rose, emerging markets came under pressure and, in March 2023, a run on Silicon Valley Bank and the collapse of Credit Suisse briefly shook the banking system. China slowed sharply under its zero-COVID policy and a property slump. Yet world output kept growing, by 3.8 percent in 2022 and 3.3 percent in 2023, and by late 2023 inflation was falling without the recession many had feared. India, the fastest-growing large economy, paid more for its oil and raised its own rates, but kept growing at 7 percent or more a year.

Inflation at four-decade highs

At the end of 2021 the IMF and most central banks still described the jump in prices as largely temporary: a result of shortages and of spending switching from services to goods as economies reopened (Phase 6). In 2022 it became clear they were wrong. In January 2021 consumer prices had been only 1.4 percent higher than a year earlier in the United States and 0.9 percent higher in the euro area and the United Kingdom. By mid-2022 inflation in all three had reached rates not seen in decades (chart 7.1). The US rate peaked at 9.0 percent in June 2022 on the seasonally adjusted index, the highest since November 1981 (the unadjusted figure published at the time was 9.1 percent).1 The euro area's peaked at 10.6 percent and Britain's at 9.6 percent, both in October 2022.

The IMF wrote in April 2022 that inflation in the United States and some European countries had reached "its highest level in more than 40 years".2 In October it added that inflation in the advanced economies as a group had reached its highest rate since 1982.3 On today's figures consumer prices rose by 7.3 percent in the advanced economies in 2022 and by 9.6 percent in the emerging and developing economies; world inflation was 8.7 percent. In a few countries with weak policy frameworks it ran out of control: 72.3 percent in Türkiye in 2022 and 133.5 percent in Argentina in 2023. Japan was the exception among the rich economies, with inflation of only 2.5 percent in 2022; China's was 2.0 percent.

The causes were several, and economists still argue about their weights. Demand had been boosted by large fiscal support and saved-up income, above all in the United States. Supply had been held back by bottlenecks in shipping and in parts such as microchips. Labour markets were tight. Then came the war, which added a shock to the prices of energy and food. The IMF's own analysis found that its forecast errors were linked both to the size of fiscal stimulus in the advanced economies and to supply shocks.3 For households the result was what the IMF called a cost-of-living crisis, the title of its October 2022 report: wages rose more slowly than prices, and real incomes fell.

War and the energy shock

Russia's invasion of Ukraine on 24 February 2022 was first a human catastrophe. By early April more than 4.5 million refugees had left Ukraine, half of them for Poland.2 The IMF expected Ukraine's economy to shrink by 35 percent in 2022 and Russia's by about 8.5 percent, as Western governments excluded some Russian banks from the SWIFT payments network and froze the assets of Russia's central bank.2 Ukraine's output fell by 28.8 percent. Russia's fell by only 1.4 percent.

For the rest of the world the war worked through the prices of commodities. The IMF likened its effects to "seismic waves" spreading from an earthquake.2 Russia was a major exporter of oil, gas and metals, and together Russia and Ukraine accounted for close to 30 percent of world exports of wheat.2 Brent crude, 87 dollars a barrel on average in January 2022, averaged 123 dollars in June. On the IMF's annual indices, food prices in 2022 were 46 percent higher than in 2020 and energy prices 227 percent higher. Food weighs most heavily on the poor: it makes up about 40 percent of what households in sub-Saharan Africa buy.3 A deal to let Ukraine ship grain from its Black Sea ports gave some relief.3

Europe was hit hardest, because it had come to depend on Russian gas delivered by pipeline. Over 2022 Russia cut those deliveries to about a fifth of their level a year earlier.3 The price of gas in Europe, which had averaged 4.5 dollars per million British thermal units in 2019, reached 70.0 dollars in August 2022 (chart 7.2). Governments spent heavily to shield households and firms, and the IMF warned that the coming winters would be hard: "winter 2023 will likely be worse" than 2022.3 It was not. Europe bought liquefied natural gas from elsewhere, cut its use of gas and enjoyed a mild winter, and by April 2023 the IMF could report that a gas crisis had been averted.4 The price had fallen to 11.3 dollars by December 2023, still more than twice its 2019 level. Germany, the industrial economy most reliant on Russian gas, shrank by 1.0 percent in 2023.

The war also changed how governments thought about trade. In April 2022 the IMF warned of the risk of "a more permanent fragmentation of the world economy into geopolitical blocks", with separate technology standards, payment systems and reserve currencies.2 By its count almost 3,000 new restrictions on trade were imposed in 2022, against fewer than 1,000 in 2019.5 This fragmentation, and the security-minded industrial policy that went with it, carried on into the next phase.

The fastest tightening in a generation

Central banks had been slow to react, and in 2022 they made up for it. The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate for the first time in March 2022 and kept going; by July 2023 the midpoint of its target range had risen from 0.12 percent to 5.38 percent (chart 7.3). The Bank of England raised its rate from 0.25 percent to 5.25 percent. The European Central Bank, whose deposit rate had been negative since 2014 (Phase 4), raised it from -0.50 percent in July 2022 to 4.00 percent by September 2023. Several emerging-market central banks had started earlier: Brazil's had raised its rate by 9.75 percentage points in the year to April 2022,2 and it peaked at 13.75 percent. The Bank of Japan, facing much lower inflation, kept its rate at -0.10 percent.

The IMF backed the tightening. In July 2022 it wrote that "taming inflation should be the first priority for policymakers".1 It argued that the risks of doing too little and too much were not symmetric: misjudging inflation a second time could gravely damage central banks' credibility.3 In April 2023 it warned against easing too early, recalling that the Fed had loosened policy too soon after a first round of tightening in the early 1980s.4

The fear was a wage-price spiral, in which workers demand higher pay to make up for rising prices, firms pass the cost on, and inflation becomes self-sustaining, as it did in the 1970s. The IMF studied past episodes and concluded that the chances of persistent spirals "appear limited"; when real wages had been flat or falling, as they were in 2022, spirals had not tended to follow.3 Its chief economist, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, wrote in April 2023 that he remained "unconvinced" a spiral was under way.4 Long-term interest rates rose with the policy rates. The yield on 10-year US Treasury bonds, 1.47 percent in December 2021, averaged 4.80 percent in October 2023. The era of very cheap money that had begun after 2008 was over.

A strong dollar and strained borrowers

Because US rates rose faster than most, and because investors seek the safety of the dollar in troubled times, the dollar rose. Against a broad basket of currencies it gained 10.1 percent between December 2021 and October 2022 (chart 7.4). Against individual currencies the moves were bigger: in 2022 it rose about 15 percent against the euro, 20 percent against sterling and 25 percent against the yen, and Japan intervened in September to support its currency.3 The IMF wrote that the dollar was "at its highest level since the early 2000s".3

A strong dollar makes imports dearer for everyone else and raises the cost of debts owed in dollars. Money left emerging markets right after the invasion as fast as it had at the start of the pandemic, though it was concentrated in a few countries and a quarter of it soon came back.2 The IMF advised emerging-market governments to "batten down the hatches", to let their exchange rates adjust and to save their reserves for real emergencies; too many low-income countries, it added, were in or near debt distress.3 A year later it counted more than half of low-income developing countries in debt distress or at high risk of it.5 Higher rates also exposed fragile corners of rich-country finance: in the autumn of 2022 the British government bond market went through a bout of instability.4

The banking scare of March 2023

Rising rates reduce the value of bonds that banks already hold. At Silicon Valley Bank, a Californian lender to technology firms with many large, uninsured deposits, this became fatal. On 6 March 2023 it disclosed a loss of 1.8 billion dollars on securities, and on 8 March it announced a plan to raise capital. On 9 March depositors withdrew 42 billion dollars, and the next day the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation took the bank over. Signature Bank in New York, which had lost a fifth of its deposits, was closed on 12 March.6 US authorities guaranteed all deposits at the two banks, including uninsured ones, and the Fed set up a new facility to lend to banks against their bonds.6

The panic spread to Europe. Credit Suisse, a large Swiss bank with a long record of losses and scandals, lost the confidence of its investors in mid-March. On 19 March it was taken over by its rival UBS for 3 billion Swiss francs, with a state guarantee of 9 billion francs. Holders of 16 billion francs of additional tier 1 (AT1) bonds, debt designed to absorb losses in a crisis, were wiped out, while shareholders kept 3 billion.6 The IMF noted that social media had helped spread the panic, and that the repricing of expected interest rates was comparable in size to the stock market crash of 1987.6 It asked whether these failures were "a canary in the coal mine".6

They were not, or not yet. The central banks kept raising rates through the turmoil: the European Central Bank (ECB) by half a point on 16 March and the Fed by a quarter-point on 22 March.6 By July the IMF judged that strong action by the US and Swiss authorities, together with the settlement of a stand-off over the US debt ceiling, had reduced the immediate risks, and that "the March 2023 banking scare remained contained" to US regional banks and Credit Suisse.7 Banks did lend more cautiously, and the episode showed how quickly deposits could flee a bank in the age of mobile banking.

China slows

China, the engine of world growth for two decades, sputtered. It held on to a strict zero-COVID policy long after other countries had reopened, locking down whole cities, including manufacturing hubs, in 2022.2 At the same time its property sector, which the IMF put at about one-fifth of economic activity, was weakening fast as developers that had borrowed heavily ran out of money.3 China's growth fell to 3.1 percent in 2022, the lowest in more than four decades apart from 2020.3 It relaxed its COVID restrictions in November and December 2022 and reopened fully,8 and growth recovered to 5.4 percent in 2023. But the rebound faded within months, and in the second half of 2023 Country Garden, the largest property developer, was under severe strain.5 Prices in China barely rose: inflation was 0.2 percent in 2023. While the rest of the world fought inflation, China was drifting towards the opposite problem.

A soft landing, mostly

The gloom peaked in October 2022. The IMF then expected world growth of only 2.7 percent in 2023, put the chance of growth below 2 percent at one in four, and warned that "for many people 2023 will feel like a recession".3 In April 2023, after the bank failures, its medium-term forecast of 3 percent growth was the lowest in any of its reports since 1990.4

The world economy proved more resilient. On today's figures it grew by 3.3 percent in 2023. The United States did best, growing 2.4 percent in 2022 and 2.9 percent in 2023, with unemployment staying low; unlike the euro area and China, its output rose above its pre-pandemic path.5 Europe stagnated: the euro area and the United Kingdom each grew by only 0.4 percent in 2023. Inflation fell as energy prices dropped and supply chains healed. By December 2023 it was 3.3 percent in the United States, 2.9 percent in the euro area and 4.2 percent in Britain, still above the 2 percent targets but falling. In October 2023 the IMF wrote that the world economy was "limping along, not sprinting", and that its forecasts were increasingly consistent with a soft landing, in which inflation falls without a recession.5

The costs were real all the same. World output in 2023 was about 3.4 percent below the path the IMF had projected before the pandemic, and more than 6.5 percent below it in the low-income countries.5 Prices had risen far more than wages for two years, and voters in many countries remembered it. Interest rates were now high, public debts were large, and the world was more divided. These were the conditions in which the next phase began.

Why the forecasters were surprised

Few forecasters saw the inflation coming, and the IMF was no exception. In October 2021 it expected consumer prices in the advanced economies to rise by 2.3 percent in 2022; in April 2022, after the invasion, it raised that to 5.7 percent; the outturn was 7.3 percent (chart 7.5). For the euro area the October 2021 forecast was 1.7 percent and the outturn 8.4 percent. In a box in its October 2022 report the IMF examined its own record: in 2021 and 2022 its inflation forecasts had been too low by 3.2 percentage points on average, against an average error close to zero in the decade before the pandemic.3

The growth forecasts erred the other way. In April 2022 the IMF projected world growth of 3.6 percent for both 2022 and 2023;2 by October 2022 it had cut 2023 to 2.7 percent.3 Today's figures are 3.8 and 3.3 percent. The models that had served well during two decades of low inflation struggled with a world of supply shocks, war and very large fiscal support, and the forecasters underestimated both how high prices would go and how well economies would cope with higher rates.

India: dear oil, higher rates and steady growth

India's Economic Survey of January 2023 counted "at least three shocks" to the world economy since 2020: the pandemic, the war with the inflation it brought, and the synchronised rise in interest rates led by the Federal Reserve, which pulled capital into the United States and strengthened the dollar.9 For India, which imports most of its oil, the war's first effect was on the import bill. The Indian basket of crude peaked at 116 dollars a barrel in June 2022.9 Oil imports, 83 billion dollars in 2020-21, rose to 162 billion in 2021-22 and 209 billion in 2022-23 (chart 7.6). The government cut excise duties on petrol and diesel in November 2021 and again in May 2022, and spent more on food and fertiliser subsidies than it had budgeted; the Budget, the Survey noted, had been drawn up "not anticipating" the war.9

Consumer inflation rose above the 6 percent upper limit of the Reserve Bank's target range in January 2022 and stayed there for ten months, peaking at 7.8 percent in April before falling below 6 percent in November.9 Food was a large part of it: an early heat wave hurt the wheat harvest, and the government prohibited exports of wheat flour from 13 May 2022 and put a 20 percent duty on exports of most rice from September.9 Over 2022-23 as a whole inflation averaged 6.7 percent.10 The Survey argued that India's inflation was lower than in many economies because India "chose not to overstimulate" during the pandemic.9

The Reserve Bank moved later than the Fed. It changed its stance in April 2022, when it also introduced a Standing Deposit Facility, and then, at an unscheduled meeting in May, raised its repo rate from 4.0 to 4.4 percent and banks' cash reserve requirement by half a point. Four more rises took the repo rate to 6.25 percent by December 2022,9 and a sixth and last to 6.50 percent in February 2023, where it stayed through 2023-24: 2.5 percentage points in all.10

The external accounts came under strain. The current-account deficit reached 4.4 percent of GDP in July–September 2022 as the oil bill swelled.9 The rupee, 74.5 to the dollar on average in 2021-22, averaged 80.4 in 2022-23. The Survey insisted that "it is not that the INR has weakened": the dollar had strengthened, and the rupee had fallen less than many currencies.9 The Reserve Bank spent reserves to steady it. In the first half of 2022-23 reserves fell by 25.8 billion dollars on a balance-of-payments basis, and by 74.6 billion including losses from valuation as the dollar rose.9 At the end of March 2023 they stood at 578 billion dollars, against 607 billion a year earlier. Exports of services, notably software and business services, and remittances from Indians abroad, which the Survey expected to reach 100 billion dollars in 2022, softened the blow.9 For the year as a whole the deficit was 2.0 percent of GDP; in 2023-24, with cheaper oil, it narrowed to 0.7 percent, and reserves rose to 646 billion dollars.10

Growth held up. The government kept raising its capital spending, which the Survey argued was crowding in (drawing in, rather than displacing) private investment, while bringing its deficit down from 6.7 percent of GDP in 2021-22 to 6.4 percent in 2022-23 and 5.6 percent in 2023-24.910 Growth in 2022-23 was first estimated at 7.0 percent, and in 2023-24 at 8.2 percent, well above the Survey's January 2023 projection of 6.5 percent;10 the latest figures are 7.6 and 9.2 percent. The Surveys say little about one large change of these years: they do not discuss Russian crude, the sanctions on Russia or the bank failures of March 2023. India's experience in this phase, an importer of energy that kept growing while raising rates and cutting its deficit, set it apart from both the struggling economies of Europe and a slowing China.

Key terms

Cost-of-living crisis
A period in which prices of essentials such as food and energy rise faster than incomes, so that living standards fall.
Wage-price spiral
A cycle in which higher prices lead workers to demand higher wages, and higher wages lead firms to raise prices further.
Soft landing
A slowdown in which a central bank brings inflation down without causing a recession.
AT1 bonds
Additional tier 1 bonds: bank debt that can be written off or turned into shares when a bank gets into trouble, so that its holders, not taxpayers, bear losses.
Fragmentation
The splitting of the world economy into rival blocs, with trade, investment and payments increasingly shaped by geopolitics.
Standing Deposit Facility
A window introduced by the Reserve Bank of India in April 2022 through which banks can deposit surplus funds with it without collateral; its rate is the floor of the RBI's interest-rate corridor.
