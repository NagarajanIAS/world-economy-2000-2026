Part III · Country lens 5 of 7

India

India entered the century as a poor and fairly closed economy. By 2026 it was among the fastest-growing large economies in the world, its share of world output had roughly doubled, and it had become one of the largest exporters of services. The path was uneven: a boom to 2008, a near-crisis in 2013, a decade spent cleaning up bad loans, a deep recession in the pandemic and a strong recovery.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Theme 1: Growth and convergence, Theme 3: Money, Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy and Theme 6: Development

In brief

India's economy grew by an average of 6.3 percent a year from 2000-01 to 2025-26, against 3.5 percent for the world. Growth averaged 7.9 percent in the five years to 2007-08, fell to 3.1 percent in 2008-09, slowed again in 2011–13 and dropped by 5.8 percent in the pandemic year 2020-21, then averaged 7.5 percent from 2022-23 to 2025-26. Inflation averaged 9.5 percent a year in 2008-09 to 2013-14 and 4.1 percent in 2016-17 to 2019-20, after India adopted an inflation target. The share of Indians living on less than $3.00 a day fell from 27.1 percent in 2011 to 2.6 percent in 2023. India's share of world output at purchasing-power parity rose from 3.9 percent in 2000 to 8.2 percent in 2025. At market exchange rates its economy ranked 13th in the world in 2000 and 6th in 2025, and is projected to rank 4th from 2027.

Take-off, 2000–2008

India began the century growing at 4.2 percent a year in 2000-01 to 2002-03. Then it took off. Growth averaged 7.9 percent a year from 2003-04 to 2007-08 (chart IN.1; Indian figures are for fiscal years running from April to March). The World Bank later described 2004–08, on the statistics then in use, as "an unprecedented high of 8.8 percent a year", helped by a global boom and a decade of earlier reform.1 The IMF's 2005 review found India's performance "impressive for the third year running" and wrote that "India’s growth spurt provides a golden opportunity" to push through more reform, starting with "a goods and services tax".2 Two years later it spoke of "India’s dream run of strong growth and macroeconomic stability", with growth of about 8½ percent four years running.3

Investment drove the boom. Gross capital formation rose from 25.7 percent of GDP in 2000-01 to 42.0 percent in 2007-08, and gross domestic saving from 24.8 to 37.8 percent (chart IN.2). The current account, in surplus by 2.3 percent of GDP in 2003-04, moved into deficit, but capital inflows were larger, and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) bought the surplus dollars: foreign exchange reserves rose from US$42 billion at the end of March 2001 to US$310 billion at the end of March 2008 (chart IN.3). The IMF kept underestimating the boom. In December 2005 it projected growth of 6.8 percent for 2006-07 and 6.5 percent a year to 2009-10; the outturns, as it recorded them five years later, were 9.7, 9.2, 6.7 and 7.4 percent.24

Crisis, inflation and a near-miss, 2008–2013

The IMF titled a section of its January 2009 review "First International Financial Crisis Since India Went Global", and warned that "India’s growth is likely to slow sharply", to 6¼ percent in 2008-09.5 On today's statistics growth was 3.1 percent that year (Phase 3 explains why the figures changed). The RBI cut its repo rate to 5 percent by March 2009, and the general government deficit widened from 3.7 percent of GDP in 2007-08 to 8.1 percent in 2008-09 (chart IN.5). Growth recovered to 7.9 and 8.5 percent in the next two years. In December 2010 the IMF wrote that "India’s growth remains among the strongest in the world" and projected 8.1 percent a year to 2014-15.4

Instead, inflation and a widening external deficit took over. Consumer prices rose by an average of 9.5 percent a year from 2008-09 to 2013-14, with a peak of 10.6 percent in 2009-10 (chart IN.4), and growth slowed to 5.2 percent in 2011-12 and 5.5 percent in 2012-13. In December 2012 the IMF recalled that a year earlier estimates of potential growth had been 8–9 percent, "with some forecasting India overtaking China"; its own work now suggested that "potential growth has declined to about 6½ percent".6 The current-account deficit reached what the IMF called "a record 4.8 percent of GDP in 2012/13", swollen by imports of oil and gold.7

When the Federal Reserve signalled in 2013 that it would slow its bond purchases, the taper tantrum (see Phase 4) hit India hard: portfolio outflows struck the rupee, shares and bonds. The authorities tightened liquidity, raised import duties on gold, eased limits on foreign borrowing and offered dollar swaps to oil companies and to banks raising deposits from Indians abroad, and markets calmed after the Fed held off in September.7 The IMF's January 2014 review expected growth of 4.6 percent, "the lowest level in a decade", judged that "India has very little room to adopt countercyclical policies", and put about two-thirds of the slowdown down to domestic causes.7 The rupee, 40.26 to the dollar on average in 2007-08, averaged 60.50 in 2013-14. A new national accounts series published in January 2015 then revised growth for 2012-13 and 2013-14 upwards, partly through changes of method, and the IMF noted that the lack of comparable back data made it hard to compare the new figures with the old.8

Reform and repair, 2014–2019

The response to 2013 shaped the next decade. In January 2014 an RBI committee chaired by Urjit Patel recommended a target for consumer price inflation of 4 percent, plus or minus 2 percent, to be approached along a glide path.9 An amendment to the RBI Act in 2016 handed rate decisions to a Monetary Policy Committee, and the government set the target from 5 August 2016 (see Theme 3).10 Under inflation targeting, inflation averaged 4.1 percent a year from 2016-17 to 2019-20, less than half its rate in 2008–13 (chart IN.4), and the IMF's February 2025 review called the framework successful.11

The other legacy of the boom was bad debt. Companies that had borrowed heavily in the boom could not repay, and the public-sector banks that had lent to them carried the losses: the Economic Survey called it the twin balance sheet problem.12 By 2016 the IMF was urging that "NPAs on public banks’ balance sheets should be fully recognized" (NPAs are non-performing assets, or bad loans).8 After an RBI review of banks' asset quality from 2015, the ratio of non-performing loans for all banks rose to 11.6 percent in March 2018, and the IMF judged that the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code of 2016 "has the potential to improve significantly NPA resolution".13 The government recapitalised the public-sector banks, and in July 2017 a national goods and services tax, the reform the IMF had urged in 2005, came into force.14 By 2025-26 the RBI could report that the bad-loan ratio had fallen to "a multi-decadal low".15

Two shocks interrupted the recovery. On 8 November 2016, in what became known as demonetisation, the government withdrew the ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes, "which accounted for 86 percent of the value of currency in circulation"; the IMF cut its growth forecast for 2016-17 to 6.6 percent.16 Today's figures put growth that year at 8.3 percent. Then, amid stress among non-bank lenders and weak rural incomes, "growth slowed to a six-year low in the first half of 2019".17 Growth in 2019-20 was 3.9 percent.

Contested question

How fast can India grow?

Seven percent or more

The Economic Survey of 2015-16 put potential growth "somewhere between 8 and 10 percent".18 The 2025-26 Survey was more cautious but still raised its estimate to about 7 percent, citing cleaner bank balance sheets, public investment and reforms.19 In 2025 the authorities told the IMF that its estimate of 6.5 percent was "at the lower end" of their own range.11 Growth averaged 7.5 percent a year from 2022-23 to 2025-26, and in October 2026 the World Bank raised its forecast for 2026-27 to 7.1 percent.20

Nearer six

IMF staff estimates of potential growth have fallen from about 7¾ percent in 2016 and 2018 to about 6 percent after the pandemic and 6.3–6.5 percent since 2023.813212223 In 2018 the World Bank noted that India had sustained 8 percent growth only once in five decades, in 2004–08.1 Over the whole period from 2000-01 to 2025-26, growth averaged 6.3 percent a year.

How the evidence weighs up. The record fits both views: growth over the whole quarter-century was close to the IMF's estimates, and growth in the latest four years close to the Survey's. Part of the disagreement is about measurement. India's GDP series was rebased twice in the period, in 2015 and in February 2026, and in 2025 the IMF rated India's national accounts C on its scale of data adequacy, citing an outdated base year and gaps between the production and spending estimates.2315 The World Bank calculates that reaching high income by 2047 would need income per person to rise "nearly 8 times", which would take growth well above either estimate.24

Pandemic, rebound and new shocks

The pandemic brought the sharpest contraction of the period. In 2021 the IMF reported that GDP "contracted an unprecedented 7.3 percent in FY2020/21"; on today's figures the fall was 5.8 percent.21 The RBI cut its repo rate to 4 percent, the general government deficit reached 13.2 percent of GDP, and debt peaked at 90.4 percent of GDP in 2020-21 (chart IN.5). Growth rebounded by 9.7 percent the next year. The IMF expected lasting damage: it put potential growth at about 6 percent and projected 6.6 percent for 2023-24 and 6.3 percent for 2024-25.21 Growth was 7.3 and 7.2 percent. By 2022 the IMF was also praising "a world class public digital infrastructure", the identity and payment systems that the state had built (see Theme 7).25

Public debt became a running argument with the IMF. In 2023 its staff calculated that if shocks like those of 2000–20 recurred, "debt would exceed 100 percent of GDP in the medium term". India's Executive Director at the Fund replied that, with interest rates below growth rates, "the debt-GDP ratio cannot exceed 100%". The same review reclassified the rupee's regime "from “floating” to “stabilized arrangement”" because the currency had moved in a very narrow range, a change the authorities rejected.22 Debt was 84.3 percent of GDP in 2025-26, and the IMF projects 75.1 percent by 2031-32 (see Theme 4).

Shocks from abroad returned in 2025. The United States raised its tariff on most Indian goods to 50 percent, a 25 percent "reciprocal" rate plus 25 percent for India's purchases of Russian oil, and the IMF's November 2025 review assumed it would last.23 After a US court ruling, India's rate fell to 10 percent in February 2026 (see the US lens).26 The 2026 energy shock (see Phase 9) then pushed inflation up from 2.0 percent in 2025-26 to 4.8 percent by August 2026,20 and on 7 October 2026 the RBI raised its repo rate from 5.25 to 5.50 percent.27 The rupee averaged 95.52 to the dollar in October 2026, against 45.68 in 2000-01.

Growth has reached the poor. On the World Bank's estimates, the share of Indians living below the $3.00-a-day poverty line fell from 46.4 percent in 2004 to 27.1 percent in 2011 and 2.6 percent in 2023 (chart IN.6), though there were no survey estimates between 2011 and 2022.28 Finance has deepened too: bank credit rose from 25.5 percent of GDP in 2001-02 to 61.7 percent in 2025-26.

India in the world economy

In 2000 India produced 3.9 percent of the world's output measured at purchasing-power parity, which values goods and services at common prices. By 2025 it produced 8.2 percent, and the IMF projects 10.2 percent by 2031 (chart IN.7). China's share rose faster, from 6.7 to 19.6 percent, but is projected to level off, while India's is still climbing; the United States' share fell from 20.4 to 14.6 percent and Japan's from 6.8 to 3.3 percent. The 2022-23 Economic Survey noted that "India is the third-largest economy in the world in PPP terms".29 At market exchange rates the World Bank finds the same doubling: India's "share in the global economy has doubled", from 1.6 percent in 2000 to 3.4 percent in 2023.24

At market exchange rates India's GDP rose from US$0.47 trillion in 2000-01 to US$3.91 trillion in 2025-26. Official claims about where this puts India in the world have run ahead of the data. The 2019-20 Economic Survey spoke of "India having become the fifth largest economy in the world in 2019"; the 2023-24 Survey expected India soon to become the third-largest; and the 2025-26 Survey described it as "the world's fourth-largest economy".303132 On the IMF's October 2026 data, which include India's rebased GDP series, this book's own ranking puts India 6th in 2019, 5th in 2022 to 2024, and 6th in 2025 and 2026, behind Japan and the United Kingdom. On the IMF's projections it moves to 4th in 2027 and 3rd by 2031. Ranks in dollars move with exchange rates and statistical revisions as well as with output.

Average incomes tell a slower story. GDP per person at purchasing-power parity rose 3.3-fold from 2000 to 2025, from 24 percent of the world average to 46 percent; but China grew faster still, so India's income per person fell from 74 percent of China's to 40 percent. The World Bank moved India from low income to lower-middle income in fiscal 2009, and India was still in that group in fiscal 2027.33 Its gross national income per person, US$2,760 in 2025 by the Bank's Atlas method, was about 60 percent of the US$4,636 threshold for upper-middle income.34 The IMF's Board has noted "India’s ambition of becoming an advanced economy by 2047".11

India's distinctive route has been services. Its share of world exports of commercial services rose from 0.98 percent in 2000 to 4.31 percent in 2025, and has been above China's since 2023 (chart IN.8). Its share of world merchandise exports rose only from 0.65 to 1.69 percent. As early as 2014 the Economic Survey noted that India's share of services exports "has been increasing faster than its share in world merchandise exports"; in 2024 it called India the seventh-largest services exporter, "with a phenomenal rise from its 24th position in 2001".3531 On the World Trade Organization's (WTO's) figures India was the eighth-largest exporter of services in 2025, with US$412 billion and 4.3 percent of the world total.36 Net exports of software services alone were US$180 billion in 2025-26. China's rise came mainly through manufactured exports (see the China lens); India's has come mainly through services.

The forecasters expect India to stay among the fastest-growing large economies. The World Bank's October 2026 update forecast growth of 7.1 percent for 2026-27;20 the IMF projects 7.0 percent, and an average of 6.9 percent a year from 2027-28 to 2031-32. At that pace India's share of world output would keep rising for years, but its income per person would still be far from the rich-country level.

Key terms

Goods and services tax (GST)
India's national tax on goods and services, in force from July 2017.
Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code (IBC)
India's 2016 law for resolving companies that cannot pay their debts.
Demonetisation
The withdrawal of the ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes as legal tender on 8 November 2016.
Twin balance sheet problem
Over-borrowed companies and the banks, mostly public-sector, that lent to them, each weighed down by the other's losses.
Lower-middle income
A World Bank income group, defined by gross national income per person; for fiscal 2027 it runs from US$1,176 to US$4,635.
