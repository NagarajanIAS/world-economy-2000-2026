Part II · Theme 8 of 9

Energy, commodities and climate

Oil, gas, metals and food went through the biggest price boom in a century, a long slump and a series of violent shocks, the latest from the war in the Middle East in 2026. Meanwhile the world burned more fossil fuel than ever, the planet warmed faster than the reports of 2000 had expected, and solar and wind power went from curiosities to the cheapest new electricity. This chapter follows the prices, the emissions, the agreements and the technology, and India's place in all three as a large oil importer, a coal-fired economy and a solar builder.

2000–2026 · about 20 minutes · Read with Phase 7: Inflation, tightening, war and energy, Phase 9: 2026, a new energy shock and Theme 6: Development

In brief

Between 2002 and 2008 the World Bank's index of energy prices rose about fivefold, as China and other emerging economies grew fast and supply was slow to respond. The commodity boom survived the financial crisis, began to fade in 2011 and broke in 2014, when American shale oil and a change of strategy by the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) sent oil prices down by more than two-thirds. Since 2020 the shocks have come from outside the market: a pandemic that briefly pushed one US oil price below zero, Russia's invasion of Ukraine, which sent European gas prices to records in 2022, and in 2026 a war that closed the Strait of Hormuz. Carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels rose by about half between 2000 and 2025, almost all of the increase in Asia. China now emits about a third of the world total and more per person than the European Union. The IPCC put warming at 1.1°C above pre-industrial levels in 2011–20; 2024 and 2025 were about 1.4°C to just over 1.5°C warmer, depending on the dataset, and 2024 was the warmest year on record. The goals agreed in Paris in 2015 are being missed, but the cost of solar power and batteries has collapsed, and in 2025 renewables generated more of the world's electricity than coal for the first time. India is a large oil importer, still gets most of its electricity from coal and has emissions per person well below the world average. It has promised net zero by 2070, and it reached its 2030 target for clean power capacity years early, in 2025.

The biggest boom in a century

In January 2000 a barrel of Brent crude, the benchmark for most of the world's oil, cost about $25. By July 2008 the monthly average was $133.9, and on 3 July the daily price touched $143.95.1 Oil was not alone. The World Bank's energy price index averaged 34 in 2002 (on a scale where 2010 = 100) and peaked at 173.5 in July 2008, 5.1 times as high. Its metals index rose from 33 to 126, and its index of fertiliser prices from 35 to 256 (chart T8.1). In 2009 the World Bank called this boom "the most marked of the past century", longer, larger and broader than any since 1900. Over five years real energy and metal prices more than doubled and real food prices rose by 75 percent.2 The rise in food prices between 2005 and early 2008 was enough, the Bank estimated, to push extreme poverty up by a percentage point or more in East Asia, the Middle East and South Asia.2

The causes were demand and slow supply. In July 2008 the IMF traced the boom's origins to the strong, commodity-hungry growth of emerging economies in 2003–07. Growth alone could not explain "the broadest and most buoyant commodity price boom since the early 1970s", it added. Limited spare capacity and supply problems mattered too, and the IMF expected tight oil markets to persist.3 Prices had been low in the 1980s and 1990s, which had discouraged investment in new mines and wells, so when demand jumped supply could not follow quickly.2 China was at the centre. By the mid-2010s it accounted for 30 percent or more of world demand for copper, iron ore, nickel, aluminium and soybeans.4

The IMF's warning of persistently tight markets was overtaken within months. As the world economy fell into recession, Brent dropped to a monthly average of $41.6 in December 2008 (chart T8.2). Then the boom resumed. Brent averaged more than $100 a barrel for most of 2011 to mid-2014 and reached $124.9 in March 2012. The World Bank later dated the end of the "super-cycle" to early 2011, when new mines came on stream just as China's growth tilted away from heavy industry. Metals and food fell first; oil was held up for a time by OPEC.4

The bust and the shocks

Oil's turn came in the second half of 2014. Brent fell from a monthly average of $111.9 in June 2014 to $30.8 in January 2016. In January 2015 the World Bank said the plunge "may signal an end to a price 'supercycle'", and it put the main cause on the supply side.5 American shale oil, extracted from tight rock by horizontal drilling and fracking, had beaten expectations year after year, by about 0.9 million barrels a day in 2014 alone. In late November 2014 OPEC failed to agree output cuts, signalling that it would defend its market share rather than the price.5 By 2023 tight oil made up about 64 percent of American crude output.6 The World Bank expected prices to stay low in 2015 and rise only a little in 2016;5 in fact Brent averaged $52 in 2015 and less than $44 in 2016.1

Since 2020 three shocks have come from outside the commodity markets. The first was the pandemic. Lockdowns cut travel and transport, about two-thirds of oil demand, and in April 2020 "some benchmarks" traded "at negative levels", in the World Bank's words. It was the largest one-month fall in oil prices on record.7 On 20 April the US West Texas Intermediate spot price closed at −$36.98 a barrel, an early sign, the World Bank said, that storage was running out; Brent's monthly average that April was $23.3.1

The second was Russia's invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 (see Phase 7). Brent's monthly average reached $120.1 in June 2022, and the energy index matched its 2008 peak at 173.5. The worst damage was to gas. Russian pipeline deliveries to Europe fell to about a fifth of their level a year earlier, and European gas prices reached all-time highs in August 2022.8 The IMF's measure of the European gas price averaged $70.0 per million British thermal units that month, against $4.8 in 2019. Food and fertiliser prices peaked too: the World Bank's fertiliser index hit 294 in April 2022, and its food index 153 in May.

The third, still unfolding, is the war in the Middle East that began at the end of February 2026 (see Phase 9). The Strait of Hormuz normally carries about 20 percent of the world's oil consumption; in March shipments through it stopped.9 The daily Brent price peaked at $138.21 on 7 April.1 In October the World Bank wrote that the conflict "has dealt a historic shock to energy supplies". It expected world oil production to fall by more than 5 percent in 2026 and oil demand by 2.5 percent, the third-largest drop on record after 2020 and 1980. Government oil stocks in rich countries had fallen to their lowest in more than three decades.10 Brent was back at a monthly average of $125.0 in October 2026, and the World Bank's energy index at 160. In July the IMF judged that the war's blow to growth was being partly offset by the AI investment boom, but that the fall in inflation since early 2024 "has stalled".11

One price has moved differently in this decade. The World Bank's metals index set a new high of 141 in March 2022 and stood at 143 in October 2026, with aluminium, copper and tin at records. Its October 2026 outlook saw "structural conditions for a sustained spell of elevated base metal prices", because investment is shifting towards metal-hungry clean energy, AI data centres and defence.10 Clean energy needs a lot of metal. In 2020 the World Bank estimated that holding warming to 2°C would require output of graphite, lithium and cobalt to rise by more than 450 percent by 2050.12 Supply is concentrated. China's share of mining of the most common rare earths fell from 97 percent in 2010 to 58 percent in 2024, but it still does 88 percent of the separation and 93 percent of the refining, and makes 90 percent of the world's permanent magnets.9

Emissions move east

Burning fossil fuels and making cement released 25.5 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide in 2000 and 39.0 billion in 2025, on the Global Carbon Budget's figures, an increase of 53 percent. They fell noticeably only in the world recessions of 2009 and 2020, when they dropped to 35.1 billion tonnes, then rebounded. The geography changed completely (chart T8.3). China's emissions rose 3.4 times, from 3.7 to 12.4 billion tonnes, and its share of the world total from 14 to 32 percent. India's rose 3.3 times, to 3.2 billion tonnes, 8 percent of the total. American emissions peaked in 2005 and by 2025 were 18 percent lower; the European Union's fell by 33 percent between 2000 and 2025. In 2000 the United States and the EU together produced 38 percent of the world's carbon dioxide; in 2025, 19 percent.

Two other measures change the picture. Per person, an American still emitted 14.5 tonnes in 2025, three times the world average of 4.7 tonnes. China, at 8.8 tonnes, has passed the European Union (5.4 tonnes); India, at 2.2 tonnes, is less than half the world average (chart T8.4). And what warms the planet is the carbon dioxide that has built up over time. Of all fossil and industrial emissions since 1750, the United States accounts for 23 percent, China and the EU for 16 percent each, and India for 4 percent. The World Bank's 2010 World Development Report put the point bluntly: high-income countries, with a sixth of the world's people, were responsible for nearly two-thirds of the greenhouse gases in the atmosphere.13

Emissions grew more slowly than the economy. Between 2000 and 2021 the energy used per dollar of output fell by 25 percent worldwide, by 42 percent in China and by 40 percent in India, on the World Bank's measure of energy intensity. In 2026 the Global Carbon Project expects fossil emissions to fall by 0.4 percent, helped by solar and wind and by the war's effect on oil use. Its lead author warned that a fall "driven partly by geopolitical shocks is not a sustainable climate solution".14

A warming world

In 2023 the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concluded that human activities "have unequivocally caused global warming". It put the global surface temperature in 2011–20 at 1.1°C above its 1850–1900 average, and expected the 1.5°C mark to be reached in the near term.15 The warming has continued (chart T8.5). On the measure of NASA (the US National Aeronautics and Space Administration), against its 1951–80 base, the world was 0.39°C warmer in 2000, 1.01°C in 2016 and 1.28°C in 2024, the warmest year on record; 2025, at 1.19°C, was second. Against pre-industrial times NASA put 2024 at about 1.47°C. The European Copernicus service, using a different dataset, found 2024 to be the first calendar year clearly above 1.5°C, and the average for 2023–25, at 1.52°C, the first three-year average above 1.5°C since instrumental records began. Its estimate of the long-term level of warming, which smooths out year-to-year swings, points to crossing 1.5°C around 2029. The World Meteorological Organization put 2025 at 1.43°C, the second or third warmest year.16 The Global Carbon Project estimates that the remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C is 150 billion tonnes of carbon dioxide, less than four years of emissions at the current rate.14

From promises to plans

The World Bank's 2010 report warned that warming could reach 5°C or more by 2100 and that even the best efforts were unlikely to hold it below 2°C. It urged the world to "act now, act together, act differently", and put the cost of cutting emissions in developing countries at $140–175 billion a year by 2030, against about $8 billion a year of finance then available.13

The Paris Agreement of December 2015 asked every country to set its own nationally determined contribution and to submit successive ones, in pursuit of a common goal: holding warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels" and pursuing efforts to limit it to 1.5°C.17 The first global stocktake, at the Dubai summit (COP28) in December 2023, found the world off track. The world had already used about four-fifths of the carbon budget for 1.5°C, and staying on a 1.5°C path would need emissions 43 percent below their 2019 level by 2030. For the first time a summit called for "transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems", and for tripling renewable capacity by 2030.18

The economists' preferred tool, a carbon price, has spread slowly. In 2026 carbon taxes and emissions trading schemes covered 29 percent of the world's emissions, at an average price of about $21 a tonne, double the 2016 level.19 That is well below what the IMF estimated in 2020 would be needed, $40–150 a tonne by 2050 alongside a green investment push. The IMF judged that policies to cut emissions had been "grossly insufficient".20 Meanwhile governments still subsidise fossil fuels heavily. The IMF counted $7 trillion of fossil fuel subsidies in 2022, 7.1 percent of world GDP. Most of this was the unpriced cost of pollution and warming; explicit subsidies, mostly for keeping fuel prices down, were $1.3 trillion.21

Sun, wind and batteries

The biggest surprise of the period is technology. Between 2010 and 2019, the IPCC reported, the unit cost of solar energy fell by 85 percent, wind by 55 percent and lithium-ion batteries by 85 percent.15 In 2000 solar and wind produced 0.2 percent of the world's electricity; in 2025, 17.3 percent (chart T8.6). In the European Union the share reached 30.3 percent, in China 21.8 percent and in the United States 18.9 percent. Coal's share of world electricity fell from 38 percent in 2000 to 33 percent, and in China from 78 to 54 percent. The think tank Ember calculated that in 2025 renewables, including hydropower, produced 33.8 percent of the world's electricity and overtook coal for the first time. Solar met three-quarters of the growth in demand, China built more than half the world's new solar capacity, and battery costs fell by a further 45 percent.22

Electricity is only part of the story. Fossil fuels still produced 57 percent of the world's electricity in 2025, and electricity is only one use of energy; oil for transport is another. Here too change has begun: in 2026, the World Bank estimated, electric vehicles displaced about 0.6 million barrels a day of China's oil use.10

Contested question

Who should cut emissions, how fast, and who pays?

Those who caused it should lead and pay

Rich countries put most of the carbon now in the air, and their people still emit far more each. The World Bank's 2010 report estimated that developing countries' emissions per person averaged a third of high-income countries', and that their costs of cutting emissions would far exceed the finance on offer.13 The Paris Agreement wrote "common but differentiated responsibilities" and making finance flows consistent with low-emission development into its aims.17 At Glasgow in 2021 India's prime minister said that the rich world's promises of climate finance had proved empty.23

Everyone must cut, and quickly

The physics does not care who emits. The IPCC and the Dubai stocktake set out pathways in which world emissions fall by 43 percent by 2030 and carbon dioxide reaches net zero by 2050.18 Since most of the growth in emissions now comes from emerging economies, the case goes, those targets cannot be met unless China, India and others cut too.

Put a price on carbon

The IMF's 2020 analysis argued that an initial green investment push followed by steadily rising carbon prices, with part of the revenue returned to households, would cut emissions at a modest cost to output. In its estimates world output would be about 0.7 percent a year higher for the first 15 years, and slightly over 1 percent lower by 2050, against expected growth of 120 percent over the 30 years.20

Weigh the trade-offs

India's Economic Survey of 2023-24 argued that the world's "current approach to dealing with climate change is flawed" because it ignores trade-offs with growth, energy security and poverty. It pointed to rich countries themselves delaying their targets, such as Britain's postponement of its ban on new petrol and diesel cars from 2030 to 2035.24

How the evidence weighs up. All four views are borne out in part. Emissions per person and historical responsibility are still heavily skewed towards rich countries, but China's emissions per person now exceed the EU's. Carbon prices have stayed low and patchy, yet clean electricity has spread fast anyway because it became cheap. The thing that has changed most since 2000 is cost: the unit costs of solar power and batteries fell by 85 percent in a single decade.15 The things that have changed least are finance and speed. World emissions in 2025 were the highest on record, and the 1.5°C carbon budget is almost spent.14

India: oil bills, coal and the solar build-out

India imports most of its oil, and the oil price has shaped its economy all century (chart T8.7). The oil import bill rose from $16 billion in 2000-01 to $91 billion in 2008-09 and $165 billion in 2013-14, 8.9 percent of GDP. The collapse of 2014–16 nearly halved it, to $83 billion in 2015-16, 4.0 percent of GDP. Russia's invasion of Ukraine pushed it to a record $209 billion in 2022-23, and it was $174 billion in 2025-26. Dependence on imported crude rose from 71 percent of supply in 2001-02 to 89 percent in 2024-25. In 2026 the war hit hard. Between April and October 2026 India paid $111.0 billion for imported crude, against $72.3 billion a year earlier, for only slightly more oil.25

For years the government shielded consumers. State oil companies sold petrol, diesel, kerosene and cooking gas below cost and were compensated partly with oil bonds and by upstream producers. Their under-recoveries rose from ₹40,000 crore (₹400 billion) in 2005-06 to ₹103,292 crore (₹1.03 trillion) in 2008-09, when the Indian crude basket peaked at $142.04 a barrel on 3 July 2008, and to ₹139,869 crore (₹1.40 trillion) in 2013-14.26 In 2010 the government said petrol and diesel prices would be "market determined";27 petrol was freed, and diesel prices were raised in small monthly steps.26 The oil collapse of 2014 gave it the chance to finish the job. Diesel was deregulated in October 2014, and as world prices fell the government raised excise duties in a series of steps. The Economic Survey 2014-15 calculated that this amounted to a "de facto carbon tax equivalent to US$ 60 per ton" of carbon dioxide on petrol.27

Electricity is still mostly coal (chart T8.8). Coal produced 68 percent of India's electricity in 2000 and 71 percent in 2025, and fossil fuels supplied almost 84 percent of India's primary energy in 2022-23.24 But the build-out of solar power has been rapid. Solar's share of generation rose from 0.5 percent in 2015 to 9.4 percent in 2025, and wind's from 2.5 to 5.0 percent. In 2025, Ember found, fossil-fuel generation fell in India, as in China, for the first time this century, and India installed more new solar capacity than the United States.22

India's climate commitments have grown with this. Its first nationally determined contribution (NDC), in 2015, promised that 40 percent of power capacity would be non-fossil by 2030. At Glasgow in 2021 the prime minister set out five aims, the "Panchamrit", including 500 gigawatts of non-fossil capacity by 2030 and net zero by 2070.23 The updated NDC of August 2022 raised the capacity target to about 50 percent by 2030 and promised to cut the emissions intensity of GDP by 45 percent from its 2005 level. It made both targets dependent on technology transfer and low-cost international finance.28 The non-fossil share of capacity rose from 27.3 percent in 2014-15 to 40.4 percent in 2022, and the 2025-26 Survey reported that India had "already surpassed the goal of 50 per cent", reaching 51.93 percent at the end of December 2025.2829 Capacity is not generation, though: solar panels work only in daylight, and non-fossil sources still produced only about 27 percent of India's electricity in 2025. India started a national emissions trading scheme in 2026.19

The government's case rests on fairness and development. The 2023-24 Survey noted that India's emissions per person were about a third of the world average.24 On the Global Carbon Budget's figures for 2025 they are a little under half, 2.2 tonnes against 4.7, and India's share of all the carbon dioxide emitted since 1750 is 4 percent. The Survey also pointed out that GDP had grown by about 7 percent a year and emissions by about 4 percent between 2005 and 2019.24 But India is now the world's third-largest emitter, at 8 percent of the total, and its emissions are still growing fast: the Global Carbon Project projects a rise of 4.4 percent in 2026, against falls in China, the United States and the EU.14 The 2026 oil shock showed again what that dependence on imported fuel costs.

Key terms

Commodity supercycle
A rise and fall in raw-material prices lasting a decade or more, driven by long swings in demand and investment.
Shale (tight) oil
Oil extracted from dense rock by horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing.
Carbon budget
The total carbon dioxide that can still be emitted while keeping warming below a given level.
Nationally determined contribution (NDC)
A country's own climate pledge under the Paris Agreement.
Net zero
Emissions balanced by removals, so that the total added to the air is zero.
Carbon price
A tax or tradable permit that makes emitters pay for each tonne of carbon dioxide.
Under-recoveries
The losses of India's state oil companies from selling fuel below cost.
