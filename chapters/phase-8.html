Part I · Phase 8 of 9

AI, tariffs and fragmentation

By 2024 inflation was falling, recession had been avoided and central banks began to cut rates. Then, in 2025, the United States raised its tariffs to levels not seen in a century, and the rules that had governed world trade since the Second World War were thrown into doubt. At the same time a boom in artificial intelligence drove American investment and share prices. The world economy bent but did not break.

January 2024 – December 2025 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: Inflation, tightening, war and energy · Next: 2026: a new energy shock

What happened

In 2024 inflation kept falling and the big central banks began cutting interest rates: the European Central Bank in June, the Bank of England in August and the Federal Reserve in September. The Bank of Japan went the other way and raised its rate for the first time in years. From February 2025 the United States imposed tariffs on its neighbours, on China and then, on 2 April, on almost every country. Customs duties collected rose from 2.8 percent of the value of US goods imports at the start of 2025 to 11.3 percent at the end. Markets fell, then recovered as many tariffs were paused or cut in deals. Meanwhile companies poured money into data centres for artificial intelligence: US business spending on computers rose 1.82-fold during 2025, and technology shares soared. China kept growing about 5 percent a year with prices barely rising, and its current-account surplus swelled. Europe stagnated. World output grew by 3.5 percent in 2025, more than any IMF forecast had expected. India remained the fastest-growing large economy, though from August 2025 most of its exports to the United States faced a 50 percent tariff.

The landing, and the first cuts

The year 2024 opened with relief. The world had avoided recession, banks had held up, and the inflation surge had not set off wage-price spirals. "Most indicators point to a soft landing," wrote the IMF's chief economist, Pierre-Olivier Gourinchas, in April 2024, adding that the US economy had "already surged past its prepandemic trend".1 By October he declared that the global battle against inflation had "largely been won".2 Consumer prices rose 2.9 percent in the year to December 2024 in the United States and 2.4 percent in the euro area, close to the 2 percent targets.

Central banks began to cut (chart 8.1). The European Central Bank moved first, announcing a cut on 6 June 2024.3 The Bank of England followed in August, and the Federal Reserve "pivoted to cutting rates in September", starting with half a percentage point.2 By December 2025 the midpoint of the Fed's target range was 3.62 percent, down from 5.38 percent, and the European Central Bank's (ECB's) deposit rate 2.00 percent, half its peak. The cuts were cautious, because prices of services were still rising fast; the IMF noted in July 2024 that services inflation was "holding up progress on disinflation".3

Japan, which had lived with near-zero or negative rates for a quarter of a century, moved the other way. The Bank of Japan raised its rate in March 2024 and again in July. Early in August weak US jobs figures and the Japanese rise set off "a rapid unwinding of Japanese-yen-funded carry trades", in which investors had borrowed cheaply in yen to buy higher-yielding assets elsewhere, and share prices fell sharply for a few days.2 Calm returned quickly. By the end of 2025 Japan's policy rate was 0.75 percent.

The year was also one of elections: about half the world's population voted, or would vote, in 2024, and the IMF warned that new governments might change trade and fiscal policy sharply.2 In the United States, they did.

The tariff shock of 2025

The new US administration moved quickly. Executive orders of 1 February 2025 imposed tariffs on Canada, Mexico and China. Goods from China faced an extra 10 percent from 4 February and another 10 percent from 4 March; goods from Canada and Mexico not covered by their free-trade agreement faced 25 percent from 4 March. Tariffs on steel and aluminium were extended from 12 March, with aluminium raised to 25 percent, and on cars from 3 April. China and Canada retaliated.4

On 2 April, in what the IMF called the "Rose Garden" announcement, the United States set a minimum tariff of 10 percent on imports from almost every country, in force from 5 April, and higher rates of up to 50 percent for about 60 countries. Pharmaceuticals, semiconductors, energy and some minerals were exempted. China answered with tariffs of 34 percent.4 The IMF wrote that measures and countermeasures had raised US and global tariffs "to centennial highs".4 Share prices fell steeply and bond yields jumped.4 On 9 April the United States paused the higher country rates for 90 days, keeping the 10 percent minimum, but raised its tariffs on China again. By 14 April the IMF put the US effective tariff rate on imports from the world at about 25 percent, up from under 3 percent in January; the US effective rate on Chinese goods was 115 percent (the headline rate had been raised to 145 percent) and China's on American goods 146 percent.4

Much of this was then rolled back. On 12 May the United States and China agreed to lower their tariffs for 90 days. The pause for other countries was extended to 1 August; deals were struck with some trading partners, and countries without deals were reset to higher rates.5 By October the IMF judged that effective rates were "gravitating toward a range between 10 percent and 20 percent for most countries", far above 2024, and put the overall US rate at about 19 percent.6 Duties actually collected lagged the announced rates, because importers had stocked up in advance and some goods were rerouted or exempted.6 On the national accounts, US customs revenue rose from an annual rate of 89 billion dollars at the end of 2024 to 367 billion a year later (chart 8.2). Whether the president had the legal power to impose many of these tariffs under an emergency-powers law was challenged in the courts.5

The economic damage proved smaller than feared. In April the IMF had cut its forecast for world growth in 2025 from 3.3 to 2.8 percent, describing tariffs as "a negative supply shock for the economy imposing them".4 By October it had raised the forecast back to 3.2 percent and said the impact was "at the modest end" of the range it had feared, thanks to firms rushing goods in before the tariffs, rerouted supply chains, the trade deals and restraint by other countries, few of which retaliated.6 World trade volumes even grew faster, by 5.0 percent in 2025 on today's figures against 3.8 percent in 2024, flattered by that front-loading. Higher tariffs fed through to US prices only slowly; foreign exporters did not cut their prices to absorb them, so the cost fell mostly on American buyers.6 The IMF warned that it would be premature to conclude the tariffs had done no harm.6

The AI boom

The other great story of these years was artificial intelligence. Generative AI, the technology behind chatbots that write and reason, was in the IMF's view a leap in what machines could do in some cognitive tasks. By 2024 the IMF wrote that "much hope rests on artificial intelligence (AI) delivering strong productivity gains", while warning that in the advanced economies it could affect about 60 percent of jobs, about half for better and half for worse.1 The hope translated into spending. Large technology firms raced to build data centres packed with specialised chips. US business investment in computers and peripheral equipment, 186 billion dollars at an annual rate at the end of 2024, reached 340 billion a year later (chart 8.3). The IMF estimated that investment in information-processing equipment had contributed around 57 percent of US real GDP growth since the end of 2024.7 Data centres are hungry for electricity: they and AI used an estimated 400–500 terawatt-hours in 2023, more than twice as much as in 2015.4

Investors bet heavily on the technology. The Nasdaq Composite index, dominated by technology firms, more than doubled between December 2022 and December 2025, rising 116 percent; the broader S&P 500 rose 75 percent (chart 8.4). There were wobbles. In January 2025 technology shares fell on news of a potentially cheaper AI model from the Chinese company DeepSeek,8 and in April the tariff shock pulled the Nasdaq 15 percent below its December 2024 level on a monthly average. Both falls were soon recovered. By October 2025 seven giant technology firms, the so-called Magnificent 7, made up 33 percent of the S&P 500.7

The IMF grew uneasy. It found prices of risky assets "well above fundamentals".7 Gourinchas wrote in October 2025 that "the current AI boom presents some parallels with the dot-com boom" of the late 1990s (Phase 1): if the expected profits failed to arrive, a fall in share prices could hit household wealth and spending, and investment could drop sharply.6 Whether AI would eventually lift productivity, as the optimists hoped, was too early to tell.

China: cheap goods, falling prices

China was the country hit hardest by the US tariffs, yet its economy slowed only a little. It grew 5.0 percent in 2024 and 5.0 percent in 2025 on today's figures. The reason was exports. Chinese manufacturers, already strong in electric vehicles and solar panels, sold more to Asia and Europe as the American market closed, helped by a cheaper currency in real terms.6 China's current-account surplus rose from 257 billion dollars in 2023 to 459 billion in 2024 and 724 billion in 2025 (chart 8.5).

At home, the picture was darker. More than four years after its property bubble burst, investment in real estate was still shrinking, consumers remained cautious and consumer prices barely rose: by 0.2 percent in 2024 and 0.1 percent in 2025. Producer prices were falling. The IMF warned in October 2025 that China "teeters on the verge of a debt-deflation cycle", in which falling prices raise the real burden of debts, and that its large subsidies to manufacturing "have reached their limit".6 China's flood of cheap exports, a symptom of weak demand at home, was one more source of tension with its trading partners.

Europe stalls, and rearms

Europe, which had suffered most from the energy shock of 2022 (Phase 7), struggled to recover. Germany's economy shrank in 2023 and did not grow in 2024 (0.0 percent on today's figures), weighed down by weak manufacturing, high energy costs and competition from China, not least in cars.2 The euro area grew 1.2 percent in both 2024 and 2025. In 2025 Germany changed course. It loosened its constitutional limit on borrowing, the "debt brake", to finance more spending on defence and public investment; the IMF expected its primary deficit (the deficit before interest payments) to widen by about 1 percent of GDP by 2030 as a result.4 Other European countries also faced lasting increases in defence spending.4

Debt, the dollar and gold

Governments emerged from the pandemic and the energy crisis with much larger debts, and many kept borrowing. US general government debt rose from 109 percent of GDP in 2019 to 124 percent in 2025, with a deficit of 7.1 percent of GDP in 2025; France's debt rose from 98 to 116 percent and China's, on the IMF's measure, from 60 to 99 percent. In October 2024 the IMF wrote that for the United States and China "debt dynamics are not stabilized under current fiscal plans".2 A large US tax-and-spending law passed by July 2025, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, was expected to add about 1.5 percent of GDP to the US deficit in 2026, half of it offset by tariff revenue.5 The IMF had warned in January 2025 that looser US fiscal policy could eventually force a disruptive adjustment and weaken the role of US Treasury bonds as the world's safe asset.9

Investors began to hedge. Contrary to the expectation that tariffs would strengthen it, the dollar weakened in 2025; the IMF noted that it had "depreciated further, defying expectations".5 The broad trade-weighted dollar index fell 5.8 percent between December 2024 and December 2025. Gold, the oldest safe haven, rose to records: by 21.9 percent between February and August 2024, and to above 3,400 dollars an ounce in 2025, helped by purchases by central banks.26 The IMF also worried about pressure on central banks from governments, which it said threatened their hard-won credibility.6 These were signs that confidence in the post-war order, with the dollar and US government bonds at its centre, could no longer be taken for granted. The IMF summed it up: "The rules of the global economy are in flux."6

How the forecasts fared

For two years the IMF expected world growth of a little over 3 percent, below the pre-pandemic average, and that is broadly what happened (chart 8.6). The exception was April 2025, when the tariff shock led it to cut its forecast for 2025 to 2.8 percent. Growth turned out stronger than every forecast in the run, including those made before the tariffs: 3.5 percent on today's figures.10 The forecasters did less well on the composition. They expected US growth in 2025 to fall to 1.8 percent after the tariffs; today's figure is 2.3 percent, propped up by AI investment. They expected China's inflation to return to about 2 percent in 2025; it was close to zero.110

India: fast growth, a tariff wall and falling prices

India began the period strongly. Its government was returned for a third term in the 2024 elections,11 and the economy kept growing faster than the world average (chart 8.7) and, as the Survey put it, than any other major economy. Growth did dip in the middle of 2024, to 5.4 percent in July–September from 6.7 percent in the previous quarter, which the Survey put down to slower manufacturing exports, heavy monsoon rains and the timing of festivals; public investment had also slowed during the general elections of April–June.11 For 2024-25 as a whole it was 6.5 percent, and the first estimate for 2025-26 was 7.4 percent, above the Survey's own forecast of 6.3–6.8 percent.1112 The January 2025 Survey's main message was deregulation: the state, it argued, should move from treating business as "guilty until proven innocent" to the reverse, and it estimated that India needed growth of about 8 percent a year for a decade or two to become a developed country by the centenary of its independence in 2047.11

The tariffs came as a shock. India had been expected to gain from the US–China rift; instead, the January 2026 Survey wrote, its treatment "surprised many". A "reciprocal" US tariff of 25 percent was followed in August 2025 by an additional penal tariff of 25 percent on most goods, so that exports to the United States faced an effective tariff of 50 percent, among the highest of any country.12 The Survey does not give the reason for the second tariff; the US order it cites refers in its title to Russia. The hit fell on labour-intensive goods. In April–November 2025 exports of gems and jewellery to the United States fell by 44.3 percent on a year earlier, and textiles, auto components and marine products also fell, while exports of the same goods to the rest of the world held up or grew. Pharmaceuticals, largely exempt, were little changed.12 India stepped up trade talks, with the United States and others: an agreement with the United Kingdom was concluded, one with Oman was signed in December 2025 and talks with New Zealand concluded the same month.12 It also bought more crude oil from the United States, whose share of its crude imports rose from 4.6 to 8.1 percent (figures for April–November).12

The government answered partly with reforms at home. Under Budget 2025-26 income of up to 12 lakh rupees a year paid no income tax, and from 22 September 2025 the goods and services tax was simplified into two main rates, 5 and 18 percent, with a 40 percent rate on "de-merit" goods.12 Prices helped. Consumer inflation, 4.6 percent in 2024-25, fell to 1.7 percent in April–December 2025, the lowest in the series, and just 0.3 percent in October 2025, as food prices dropped.12 The Reserve Bank, which had held its repo rate at 6.5 percent through 2024 while cutting banks' cash reserve ratio in December,11 cut the repo rate by 1.25 percentage points from February 2025, to 5.25 percent by December, and lowered the cash reserve ratio to 3 percent.12 The central government kept to its plan to cut borrowing, bringing its deficit down to 4.8 percent of GDP in 2024-25, with a target of 4.4 percent for 2025-26, less than half the 9.2 percent of 2020-21; the rating agency S&P raised India's credit rating in August 2025, its first upgrade by a major agency in nearly two decades.12

The weak spot was money from abroad. Foreign portfolio investors took out a net 3.9 billion dollars in 2025-26 up to December, which the Survey linked partly to "AI-related financial investments" in places such as the United States.12 The rupee, which averaged 84.6 to the dollar in 2024-25, fell 5.4 percent between 1 April 2025 and 15 January 2026, even as the dollar weakened against most currencies; reserves stood at 701.4 billion dollars in January 2026, swollen by the rising price of gold.12 AI posed a longer-term question. India's information-technology services, a pillar of its exports, were exposed: the Survey warned that AI "risks hollowing out India's core value proposition if adaptation lags", and noted that India produced a large share of the world's data but hosted only about 3 percent of its data centres.12

Key terms

Effective tariff rate
The average tariff actually paid on imports, often measured as customs duties collected divided by the value of imports.
Front-loading
Buying or shipping goods early to beat an expected tariff or price rise, which boosts trade for a while and depresses it later.
Carry trade
Borrowing in a currency with low interest rates, such as the yen, to invest in assets that pay more elsewhere; it can unwind abruptly when rates or exchange rates move.
Generative AI
Artificial-intelligence systems that produce text, images, code or other content in response to prompts.
Debt deflation
A spiral in which falling prices raise the real burden of debts, leading borrowers to cut spending, which pushes prices down further.
Penal tariff
A tariff imposed to punish a trading partner for its policies rather than to protect a domestic industry; India faced one from the United States in August 2025.
