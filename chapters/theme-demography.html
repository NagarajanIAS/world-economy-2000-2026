Demography and labour

Since 2000 birth rates have fallen almost everywhere, and in parts of East Asia and Europe to levels never seen in peacetime. Populations are ageing, China's has begun to shrink, and most of the world's future growth in people will be in Africa. Meanwhile labour markets went through two very different recessions, women's work changed slowly, workers' share of income fell in rich countries, and migrants sent home record sums. This chapter follows the people behind the economy, and India's attempt to turn the world's largest population into a dividend.

2000–2026 · about 20 minutes · Read with Theme 1: Growth and convergence, Theme 6: Development and Phase 6: Pandemic shock and response

In brief

The world's fertility rate fell from 2.75 births per woman in 2000 to 2.25 in 2023, and more than half of all countries are now below the replacement rate of about 2.1. The UN expects the world's population to peak at about 10.3 billion in the 2080s. China's peaked in 2021 and is falling; South Korea's fertility rate is 0.72. Ageing will slow growth in much of the world, though healthier older people are working longer. Sub-Saharan Africa will supply more than half of the world's population growth to 2050 and a vast new generation of young workers who need jobs. Labour markets recovered slowly from the financial crisis but quickly from the pandemic, which left the United States with two job vacancies for every jobseeker in 2022. Workers' share of national income fell in rich countries, and the number of international migrants and refugees reached about 304 million. India passed China as the most populous country around 2023, and its fertility rate is now below replacement. Its demographic dividend will peak within about a decade, and its central problem remains creating good jobs. Women's reported work has risen sharply, but much of it is unpaid work in family farms and businesses. India receives more money from its workers abroad than any other country.

Fewer births

Women are having fewer children almost everywhere (chart T9.1). On the UN's estimates the world's fertility rate fell from 3.31 births per woman in 1990 to 2.25 in 2023, and more than half of all countries are now below 2.1, roughly the rate at which a population replaces itself without migration.1 In China the rate was 1.63 in 2000 and 1.00 in 2023; in South Korea it fell to 0.72. The UN counts nearly a fifth of countries, including China, Italy, South Korea and Spain, as having "ultra-low" fertility, below 1.4. For countries like these whose populations have already peaked, it puts the chance of fertility returning to 2.1 within 30 years at about one in a thousand.2 Even in the United States the rate fell from 2.03 to 1.62. The exception is Sub-Saharan Africa, where it was still 4.3 in 2023, though falling.

The fall has repeatedly been faster than expected. The UN's 2024 projections show a smaller world population than its projections of a decade earlier, partly because fertility in China came in lower than expected.2 It now expects the world's population, 8,092 million in 2023, to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s, and gives an 80 percent chance that it will peak this century, against about 30 percent a decade ago. By the late 2070s people aged 65 and over will outnumber children under 18.1

Older, and in places smaller

Fewer births and longer lives mean older societies (chart T9.2). In Japan the share of the population aged 65 and over rose from 17 percent in 2000 to 30 percent in 2023, and the median Japanese was 49 years old. In South Korea, the UN projects, the share will rise from 18 percent in 2023 to 40 percent in 2050, overtaking Japan, and in China from 14 to 31 percent. The European Union's population is expected to peak at 453 million in 2026. By 2070 there will be 59 people aged 65 and over for every 100 of working age, against 36 in 2022.3

Some populations have begun to shrink (chart T9.3). Japan's peaked in 2009, Italy's in 2014 and China's, on the UN's estimates, in 2021. In 2025, China's statistics office reported, 7.92 million babies were born in China and 11.31 million people died. The population fell by 3.39 million, to 1,404.89 million, and 15.9 percent of Chinese were 65 or older.4 The UN expects China to lose 204 million people by 2054, the largest fall of any country, and by 2100 more than half its present population.2

Ageing weighs on growth. In April 2025 the IMF projected that world growth in 2025–50 would be 1.1 percentage points a year lower than its 2016–18 average, almost three-quarters of the fall due to demographics. But it found that older people are also healthier: across 41 economies, a 70-year-old in 2022 had the cognitive ability of a 53-year-old in 2000, or in its words "the 70s are the new 50s". Healthier ageing could add about 0.4 point a year to world growth by keeping more people at work for longer.5 In 2018 the IMF had already found participation rising among women and older workers in rich countries while it fell among the young and prime-age men.6

The young continent

The other half of the story is youth. In 2006 the World Bank counted 1.5 billion people aged 12 to 24, "the most ever in history". It argued that falling birth rates opened a window in which a country has many workers and few dependants, but only if it invested in its young people. Sub-Saharan Africa, it noted, would not reach its peak number of young people for 20 years.7 Twenty years later the World Bank's arithmetic is starker. Over the decade to 2035 about 1.2 billion young people in developing economies will reach working age, "the largest youth cohort the world will likely ever see", more than 330 million of them in Sub-Saharan Africa. On an illustrative extrapolation of recent trends, only just over 400 million of the 1.2 billion would be employed in 2035.8 Sub-Saharan Africa's population was 655 million in 2000 and 1,212 million in 2023, and the UN projects 2,094 million in 2050. That is 56 percent of the world's population growth over that period.

Work, wages and women

"Development happens through jobs", the World Bank's 2013 report on jobs began. It counted more than 3 billion people at work, but only about 1.65 billion of them for a regular wage; in developing countries nearly half of all workers were farmers or self-employed.9 Women's work has changed slowly (chart T9.4). In 2012 the World Bank reported that women made up more than 40 percent of the world's labour force and that the gap between men's and women's participation had narrowed from 32 to 26 percentage points between 1980 and 2008.10 On the International Labour Organization's (ILO's) modelled estimates, 49 percent of women aged 15 and over worldwide were working or looking for work in 2025, against 73 percent of men. The rate rose in the euro area and Japan, fell in China and remained lowest in the Middle East and North Africa, at 20 percent. The ILO notes that part of the narrowing of gender gaps came from young men dropping out.11

The two big recessions of the period treated workers very differently (chart T9.5). After the financial crisis US unemployment peaked at 10.0 percent in October 2009 and took years to come down. In the euro area it kept rising until 2013, when it averaged 12.0 percent; in Spain and Greece more than a quarter of the labour force was out of work, and more than half of young people (55 and 58 percent at the peak). The pandemic was a much sharper shock. US unemployment jumped to 14.8 percent in April 2020, the highest since records began in 1948. It was back to 3.5 percent by July 2022. By then American employers were scrambling for workers (chart T9.6). There were 2.0 job openings for every unemployed person in March 2022, against 0.15 in July 2009, and the share of workers quitting their jobs each month reached a record 3.0 percent in November 2021. Their part in the inflation of 2021–23 is discussed in Phase 7. By September 2026 openings were back to about one per unemployed person. Worldwide, the ILO put unemployment at a steady 5 percent in 2024, but warned that the recovery had lost much of its ability to improve jobs further.11

Workers' share of the income they produce, the labour share, has fallen. In 2017 the IMF found that in advanced economies labour shares "began trending down in the 1980s", reached their lowest level in half a century just before the 2008 crisis and had not recovered. It attributed about half the decline to technology, which made capital goods cheaper and replaced routine jobs, and a smaller part to global integration.12 On the US Bureau of Labor Statistics' index of the labour share in business output (2017 = 100), the figure was 111.6 in 2000 and 96.0 in 2025. The ILO calculated that in high-income countries between 1999 and 2024 labour productivity rose by 29 percent but real wages by only 15 percent. After the inflation of 2022 cut real wages worldwide, they rose by 2.7 percent in 2024, "the largest gain in more than 15 years".13

Migration and money sent home

As populations age in rich countries and swell in poor ones, people move. The IMF counted 304 million migrants and refugees in 2024, 3.7 percent of the world's population and almost twice as many as in 1995; about one in six were refugees or asylum seekers.14 (The World Bank's 2023 report, using a narrower definition, counted about 184 million migrants.)15 In 2018 the IMF had argued that ageing rich countries should "rethink migration policies to boost labor supply"; net migration had accounted for about half of their population growth over three decades.6 The World Bank's 2023 report agreed that wealthy countries would need foreign workers.15 Politics moved the other way. The IMF found acceptance of migrants deteriorating in several major destinations between 2016 and 2023.14

Migrants send money home. The International Organization for Migration estimated that remittances reached about $905 billion in 2024, of which $685 billion went to low- and middle-income countries, more than official aid and foreign direct investment combined.16 On the World Bank's data, India received $151 billion in 2025, more than twice as much as Mexico, the next largest recipient, at $65 billion (chart T9.7).

Contested question

Is ageing an economic crisis?

Yes: fewer workers, slower growth

The IMF's 2025 projections put world growth 1.1 percentage points lower in 2025–50 than in 2016–18, mostly because of demographics, and warned that many countries would need large efforts to stabilise their debts.5 The EU expects total hours worked to fall by 9 percent by 2070 even as more people work, and the number of people over 65 per working-age person to rise by about two-thirds.3 In South Asia, the World Bank warns, countries are "growing old before growing rich".17

No: people are healthier and working longer

The same IMF chapter found that healthier ageing could offset part of the blow, adding about 0.4 point a year to growth.5 Participation by older workers and women has risen in rich countries. The EU projects the participation rate of 55–64-year-olds rising from 65.4 to 75.5 percent by 2070,3 and the share of Americans aged 25–54 in the labour force was 83.7 percent in October 2026, close to its levels of 2000.

Migration can fill the gap

Young workers are abundant in Africa and South Asia and scarce in Europe and East Asia. The IMF and the World Bank have both argued that rich countries will need migrants to sustain their workforces.615 The UN projects that in 50 countries immigration will soften population decline.1 But acceptance of migrants has fallen in several major destinations.14

How the evidence weighs up. Ageing is certain, and its effect on the size of the workforce is large and predictable; its effect on living standards is not. Healthier, longer working lives and higher participation by women have so far cushioned it in rich countries. Migration could do more, but the politics are moving against it. The harder case is countries that are growing old before they grow rich, as the World Bank now warns for South Asia.17

India: the dividend and the jobs question

For most of this century India's Economic Surveys have counted on a demographic dividend. The 2006-07 Survey, published in 2007, expected the share of the population of working age to keep rising and India to benefit until 2045.18 The numbers did move as expected: on the UN's estimates the share of Indians aged 15–64 rose from 60 percent in 2000 to 68 percent in 2023 (chart T9.8). But the transition has come faster than that Survey assumed. Fertility fell from 3.35 births per woman in 2000 to below replacement. The National Family Health Survey put it at 2.0 in 2019-21, down from 3.4 in 1992-93,19 and the 2018-19 Survey noted that the number of Indians under 20 had already peaked, and proposed planning for a phased rise in the retirement age.20 The UN Population Fund's 2023 report put India's population, at 1,428.6 million, ahead of China's for the first time (the UN's 2024 revision, used in the charts, gives slightly different figures).21 The UN projects that the working-age share will peak in 2034, and the 2025-26 Survey puts the peak of the dividend around 2030.22 The World Bank warns that South Asia will go from "ageing" to "aged", with 14 percent of people over 65, in about 25 years, far faster than France (126 years) or the United States (72) made the same transition.17

The question is whether the extra workers find productive work. "The central long-run question facing India is where will good jobs come from?", the 2012-13 Survey asked.23 The 2023-24 Survey estimated that the economy needed to create nearly 7.85 million non-farm jobs a year.24 The official Periodic Labour Force Survey (PLFS), started in 2017-18, shows labour-force participation rising from 49.8 to 60.1 percent of people aged 15 and over by 2023-24, and unemployment falling from 6.0 to 3.2 percent (chart T9.9).25 The biggest change was among women. Their participation rose from 23.3 to 41.7 percent, "driven mainly by the rising participation of rural women".25 The ILO's modelled estimates, which put it at 32 percent in 2025, are lower.

The kind of work matters as much as the amount (chart T9.10). Between 2017-18 and 2023-24 the share of workers who were self-employed rose from 52.2 to 58.4 percent, while regular wage and salaried jobs fell from 22.8 to 21.7 percent. Among rural women workers the share who were unpaid helpers in household enterprises rose from 38.7 to 42.3 percent, and the share in agriculture from 73.2 to 76.9 percent.25 The World Bank finds 88 percent of South Asia's workers informal.17 The 2017-18 Survey had found that on women's employment, contraception and son preference "development has not proved to be an antidote", and estimated that a skewed sex ratio implied about 63 million "missing" women.26 The four Labour Codes, consolidating 29 central labour laws, were brought into force by notification on 21 November 2025.22

Indians working abroad have become a large source of income at home. Remittances rose from $55.6 billion in 2010-11 to $135.4 billion in 2024-25, about 3.5 percent of GDP. The 2025-26 Survey called India the world's largest recipient, but warned that immigration controls in the countries Indians favour could cap the growth.22

Key terms

Total fertility rate
The number of children a woman would have at current birth rates; about 2.1 keeps a population stable.
Demographic dividend
The boost to growth when the working-age share of the population is rising.
Old-age dependency ratio
People aged 65 and over per 100 people of working age.
Labour force participation rate
The share of people of working age who are working or looking for work.
Labour share
The part of national income paid to workers as wages and benefits.
Remittances
Money that migrants send to their home countries.
