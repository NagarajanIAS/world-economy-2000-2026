2026: a new energy shock

The year began with forecasters expecting steady growth and slowly falling inflation. On 28 February war broke out in the Middle East, shipping through the Strait of Hormuz stopped, and oil and gas prices leapt. For the second time in four years an energy shock pushed inflation up and central banks to raise rates. This time a boom in artificial intelligence held the world economy up, but it split the world into those who gained and those who paid.

History in progress

This chapter was written in November 2026, while the events it describes were still unfolding. Monthly prices, interest rates and inflation are actual figures up to the latest month published by 22 November 2026, and the latest months may yet be revised. Every figure for 2026 as a whole is an estimate, and every figure for 2027 a projection: unless stated otherwise they come from the IMF's World Economic Outlook of October 2026, and they will change. The reports quoted were written before the end of the story, and so is this chapter.

What happened

In January 2026 the IMF expected world growth of 3.3 percent and falling inflation. On 28 February war broke out in the Middle East. Shipments of oil and liquefied gas through the Strait of Hormuz, the channel that normally carries about a fifth of the world's oil, almost stopped, and strikes damaged gas and oil facilities in Iran and across the Gulf. Oil prices nearly doubled within weeks, fell back after a mid-June agreement between Iran and the United States briefly reopened the strait, and rose again from August as low-intensity fighting continued and spread to the Red Sea. Inflation rose again in the United States, the euro area and the United Kingdom, and central banks that had been cutting rates began to raise them. The world economy held up better than feared, helped by releases from oil stocks, extra production elsewhere and a continuing boom in AI investment, and the IMF's October estimate of world growth in 2026 was 3.1 percent. But the Gulf oil producers went into deep recession, and energy importers outside the technology supply chain, including many poor countries, bore the heaviest costs. India grew fast but paid more for its oil.

A steady start

At the turn of the year the world economy looked, as the IMF put it in January, steady on the surface. Its Update put world growth at 3.3 percent in 2026, a little higher than it had expected three months before, with the drag from US tariffs offset by the boom in investment in technology, including artificial intelligence. Inflation was expected to keep falling, from 4.1 percent in 2025 to 3.8 percent.1 Trade tensions had eased. A truce between China and the United States had cut their tariffs on each other until November 2026, and in April the IMF noted that court rulings and executive actions had brought the US tariff rate it assumed down by about 5 percentage points from its October 2025 assumption.12 Oil was cheap: Brent crude averaged 62.5 dollars a barrel in December 2025, and the IMF assumed it would stay near that level in 2026.1 The main risks it named were a fall in AI share prices and high public debt. A conflict in the Middle East was on the list, but as one risk among several.1

War and the Strait of Hormuz

Prices began to rise in January and February, which the IMF later linked to rumours of a US attack on Iran.3 On 28 February 2026 war broke out in the Middle East.2 The IMF's reports, which are the main source for this chapter, describe its economic effects in detail but say little about the fighting itself: they do not set out who began it or list the parties. India's Ministry of Finance was more direct: its monthly review for February described an attack on Iran by the United States and Israel, followed by Iranian attacks on several Gulf states.4 What the IMF reports do record is that shipping through the Strait of Hormuz, between Iran and the Arabian peninsula, stopped. About 20 million barrels of oil a day, a fifth of world consumption, normally pass through the strait, and there are few other routes: it is the world's most important oil chokepoint. Strikes on Iran's South Pars gas field were followed by strikes on energy facilities around the Gulf, including Qatar's Ras Laffan, one of the world's largest gas-export plants, on 18 March.2 In its April report the IMF warned that the closure and the damage "could cause an energy crisis on an unprecedented scale".2

Prices jumped (chart 9.1). Brent, which averaged 70.9 dollars a barrel in February, averaged 117.3 dollars in April. The daily spot price reached 138 dollars on 7 April (the IMF quotes somewhat different peaks from other price series; see the data note). The European gas price nearly doubled between December and March, and in Asia, which takes more than three-quarters of the gas shipped through the strait, the price of liquefied natural gas rose by 80 percent.2 In the United States, which produces more oil and gas than it uses, the domestic gas price barely moved.2

The shock was softened in three ways. Countries drew on oil stocks, including governments' strategic reserves, which were at a five-year high when the war began. Producers elsewhere, in Brazil, Kazakhstan, the United States and Venezuela, pumped more. And buyers used less: by the IMF's reckoning, in the second quarter of 2026 stocks supplied about 4 million barrels a day, extra output outside the Gulf 2 million, and lower demand about 5 million. China cut its crude imports by almost a quarter in March–June.3 The world was also less dependent on oil than in earlier shocks: it used about a third less energy for each dollar of output than twenty years before, and renewables had supplied most of the growth in electricity since 2010. Without these cushions, the IMF estimated, oil would have averaged about 110 dollars and world growth in 2026 would have been almost a percentage point lower.3

In mid-June a memorandum of understanding between Iran and the United States briefly reopened the strait, and prices collapsed, at one point to about 68 dollars a barrel, the IMF reports. The ceasefire did not last. In the IMF's words it was "replaced by a low-intensity conflict" that spread to the Red Sea, where attacks by the Houthis and other Iran-aligned militias hit the routes that Gulf producers had been using to get around Hormuz.3 The strait stayed mostly closed to Gulf oil until the end of August; in September Gulf crude exports recovered to almost nine-tenths of their pre-war level, partly because attacks in the Red Sea pushed shipments back through Hormuz. Attacks on Russian oil facilities, on land and in the Black Sea, added to the shortage, and the war damaged refineries that had made about a fifth of the refined fuel traded across borders, so prices of diesel and other products rose even more than those of crude.3 Brent rose again from August and averaged 123.9 dollars in October, the highest monthly average of the year. By the first weeks of November it had fallen back to about 96 dollars. The reports read for this chapter were written before that fall and do not explain it. European gas, meanwhile, kept rising, to 25.5 dollars per million British thermal units in September, 2.7 times its December level, as damage to Qatar's plants lasted and Europe's gas stores were near 15-year lows.3 That was still far below the peak of 2022, when Russia cut its supplies to Europe and the monthly price reached 70 dollars (Phase 7).

Inflation returns

The fall in inflation that had run since early 2024 came to an end. In July the IMF wrote that it "has stalled".5 In the United States consumer prices rose by 2.4 percent on a year earlier in January 2026 and 4.2 percent in May; the rate dipped as oil fell back in the summer, then rose to 3.7 percent in September (chart 9.2). In the euro area, which imports most of its energy, inflation rose from 1.7 percent in January to 4.0 percent in October, the highest since 2023. In the United Kingdom it was 3.6 percent in October. For the world as a whole, the IMF's October estimate of inflation in 2026 is 4.7 percent, up from 4.1 percent in 2025.3

The question was whether 2026 would repeat 2022, when an energy shock turned into a broad rise in prices and wages. The IMF thought the risk was real but smaller. Labour markets were less tight than in 2022 and inflation had been lower before the shock, but people remembered the cost-of-living crisis, and their expectations might react faster.2 By the summer, inflation excluding food and energy had stayed broadly stable and the IMF saw little sign that expectations were slipping.5 Food was the other worry. Gas is the main input in nitrogen fertiliser, and much of the world's fertiliser trade passes through the Gulf; the IMF warned in April of a jump in food prices if farmers cut back ahead of the planting season. In the event, the food prices it projected for 2026 were revised down, from a rise of 8 percent in July to 5 percent in October, partly because farmers in rich countries switched to crops that need less fertiliser. Farmers in poor countries had less room to adjust, and the IMF flagged the risk of hunger in low-income South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.253

Central banks turn again

At the start of the year most central banks expected to keep cutting rates, and the Bank of Japan to keep raising them slowly. The war changed that (chart 9.3). Within weeks markets that had expected cuts were expecting rises.6 The IMF's advice shifted with them. In April it said central banks could look through a temporary jump in energy prices as long as expectations stayed anchored. By July it was advising them to keep real interest rates (rates after inflation) steady, which "may imply raising nominal policy rates".5 The European Central Bank raised its deposit rate in June 2026 and again in September, to 2.5 percent. The Bank of Japan raised its rate twice, to 1.25 percent. The Federal Reserve, after holding its rate through the first eight months, raised it by a quarter of a point in September, to a midpoint of 3.88 percent. The Bank of England held its rate at 3.75 percent. Counting the world's 33 largest central banks, the IMF found that 14 had raised rates since the second quarter, against 2 in the first.7 Some emerging economies, like Brazil, whose rates were already very high, kept cutting.

Long-term interest rates rose further. The yield on ten-year US government bonds, 4.14 percent in December 2025, averaged 5.13 percent in October 2026, the highest monthly average since 2002. The IMF's financial-stability report put the rise down partly to expected short-term rates and partly to a higher term premium, the extra return investors demanded for holding long bonds as governments ran big deficits and AI companies borrowed heavily.7 Governments spent to shield households and firms from the energy shock. The IMF advised against broad subsidies and price caps, and in October it found that many of the measures taken since February were neither targeted nor temporary.23 It also projected that public debt in the advanced economies would keep rising, from 108 percent of GDP in 2025 to 115 percent in 2031.3

The AI cushion

What kept the world economy growing was the second great force of the period: the boom in investment in artificial intelligence (Phase 8). In the first quarter of 2026 the four biggest exporters of AI hardware (Taiwan Province of China, Korea, Thailand and Malaysia) grew far faster than the IMF had expected; Korea's economy grew at an annual rate of 7.5 percent, against a forecast of 1.8 percent.5 Share prices dipped when the war began and then recovered. After a fall of 5 percent between December and March, the technology-heavy Nasdaq index stood 14 percent above its December level in October, before falling back in November. World trade grew faster than expected, by an estimated 4.6 percent in 2026, much of it trade in chips and computers.3

The IMF was uneasy about this support. Its October report described the AI boom as a demand shock that comes early and adds to inflation, and its financial-stability report listed a fall in AI-related assets among the three biggest risks, pointing to rising debt and to "circular" financing deals in which AI firms invest in their own customers or suppliers.37 And the energy shock and the AI boom were not independent: data centres need a great deal of electricity, and the October report warned that energy constraints could slow the AI build-out itself.3

A split world

The world figures hide very different fortunes. The October report spoke of "a split global economy", and only 32 economies now accounted for 95 percent of world growth.3 The biggest losers were the Gulf oil and gas producers whose exports had to pass through Hormuz. In January the IMF had expected the Middle East and Central Asia to grow by 3.9 percent in 2026; in October it estimated a contraction of 0.3 percent (chart 9.4). The economies of Iraq, Kuwait and Qatar were estimated to shrink by about 15.6–16.7 percent, Saudi Arabia's, which could ship some oil by pipeline to the Red Sea, by 3.3 percent, and Iran's by 7.6 percent. Higher prices could not make up for oil that could not be shipped.3 Energy exporters outside the war zone gained from the high prices.5

Among the big economies, the United States was little touched: as a net exporter of energy it gained as much from higher prices as it lost, and the IMF estimates its growth in 2026 at 2.3 percent. The euro area, which imports most of its energy, did worse: the IMF cut its forecast from 1.3 percent in January to 0.9 percent in July, before raising it to 1.1 percent in October.3 China, despite slowing during the year, is estimated to have grown by 4.5 percent. The worst position, the IMF judged, was that of energy importers outside the technology supply chain: they paid more for fuel and food but gained nothing from the AI boom. Many were low-income countries, some of which faced fuel shortages because richer buyers outbid them for cargoes.3

How the forecasts moved

Because this chapter is written in the middle of events, its subject is not how the forecasts compared with the outcome, which is not yet known, but how they changed as the year went on (chart 9.5). The world growth forecast for 2026 barely moved. It was 3.3 percent in January, 3.1 percent in April, 3.0 percent in July and 3.1 percent in October; without the war, the IMF said in April, it would have raised it to 3.4 percent.2 The inflation forecast rose steadily, from 3.8 percent in January to 4.7 percent by July. Under the stable world total, the forecasts for individual regions moved sharply in opposite directions: down for the Gulf, up for the chip exporters and India.5

Behind every forecast was an assumption about the war. In April the IMF assumed that the conflict would be short and that the region's production and exports would be back to normal by mid-2026. In July it assumed that the strait would begin to reopen in mid-July and conditions return to normal by March 2027. In October it assumed that reopening would begin only in March 2027, with normal conditions by October 2027.253 Its assumed oil price for 2026 rose with each edition: 62 dollars a barrel in January, 82 in April, 89 in July and 96 in October.1253 In April the IMF also set out a severe scenario, with oil at about 110 dollars in 2026 and 125 dollars in 2027, that would have been "a close call for a global recession".2 So far that has been avoided, but the October report's downside scenario, in which the fighting lasts until mid-2027, would put oil at 130 dollars at the turn of the year.3 The October forecast of 3.4 percent growth in 2027 depends on the strait reopening.

India: an oil importer under strain

India went into 2026 in good shape. Consumer inflation had averaged just 2.1 percent in 2025-26, the Reserve Bank had cut its repo rate to 5.25 percent, the current-account deficit was about 0.6 percent of GDP, and foreign-exchange reserves were close to 700 billion dollars.89 The Budget presented on 1 February, four weeks before the war, planned a deficit of 4.3 percent of GDP and set aside almost nothing for petroleum subsidies.10 But India was exposed. It imported 88.6 percent of the crude oil it used, almost half of it from the Middle East. The Ministry of Finance estimated that about 93 percent of its cooking gas (LPG) came from the conflict region, as did over 80 percent of its imports of ammonia and sulphur, which are used to make fertiliser. About 9.2 million Indians lived and worked in West Asia (as Indian sources call the Middle East), and the region was the source of at least 35 percent of the money they and other migrants sent home.4

The shock arrived through all these channels at once. The price of the Indian crude basket, 71.2 dollars a barrel on 27 February, averaged 114.5 dollars in April.48 In March exports to the United Arab Emirates fell by 62 percent on a year earlier, foreign portfolio investors took out a net 13.1 billion dollars, and the rupee fell past 95 to the dollar during trading for the first time.49 Gas had to be rationed. Orders of 8 and 9 March gave households first call on LPG and piped gas and cut supplies to industry; commercial users at first received only 20 percent of their normal LPG.4

The government chose to absorb much of the oil shock itself rather than pass it on at once. On 27 March it cut the excise duty on petrol and diesel by 10 rupees a litre and held pump prices unchanged, while the state-owned oil companies took losses that the ministry put at about 26 rupees a litre on petrol and 82 rupees on diesel; an export levy kept diesel at home.114 The Ministry of Finance described the choice as "burden-sharing" between the government and fuel users. Cooking gas prices were raised from March and pump prices from mid-May, by 7.4 percent for petrol and 8.4 percent for diesel, and the Ministry of Finance proposed an Economic Stabilisation Fund of 57,381 crore rupees (about 574 billion rupees) to cover shocks without breaking its deficit target.48 The cost of the excise cut has not been published.

The Reserve Bank held its rate at 5.25 percent in April, June and August, treating the war as a supply shock, but acted to defend the rupee. In June it offered to bear the cost of hedging new foreign-currency deposits by Indians abroad (FCNR(B) deposits). The response was enormous: by the Ministry of Finance's count, about 133 billion dollars came in within three months, many times the 26 billion dollars of such deposits raised in a similar scheme in 2013 (34 billion including banks' overseas borrowing; Phase 4).84 Reserves, which had fallen to 671.6 billion dollars in mid-June, reached 765.9 billion by 18 September.4 The rupee, after averaging 90.03 to the dollar in December 2025, was at its weakest in July, at a monthly average of 95.86, and recovered a little afterwards (chart 9.7). Inflation, though, kept rising: on the new consumer price index (2024 = 100), from 3.2 percent in February to 4.4 percent in June, the first month above the 4 percent target after 16 months below it, and 4.8 percent in August. On 7 October the Reserve Bank raised its rate by a quarter of a point, to 5.50 percent, and said cuts were "off the table in the near term".8

So far India has come through better than its exposure suggested. Growth in April–June 2026 was 7.8 percent on a year earlier (on the new GDP series, with 2022-23 as base year, introduced in February 2026), and the Reserve Bank raised its forecast for 2026-27 to 7.1 percent in October, from 6.6 percent in June.8 The IMF's forecast for the year rose from 6.4 percent in January to 7.0 percent in October, the opposite of its forecasts for most energy importers.3 Its first upgrade, in April, owed something to a cut in the extra US tariff on Indian goods from 50 to 10 percent (Phase 8), which it judged outweighed the effect of the war.2 Money sent home held up: remittances reached a record 155.1 billion dollars in 2025-26, and net transfers in April–September 2026 were higher than a year before.49 The costs are in the external accounts and in prices. The current-account deficit in April–September 2026 was 25.8 billion dollars, against 18.0 billion a year earlier,9 and the IMF projects it at 1.5 percent of GDP for 2026-27, more than double the year before (chart 9.6), with consumer inflation of 5.0 percent. All of these figures for 2026-27 are early estimates or projections, and they depend, like everything in this chapter, on when the Strait of Hormuz reopens.

Key terms

Chokepoint
A narrow sea passage, such as the Strait of Hormuz or the Bab el-Mandeb at the mouth of the Red Sea, through which a large share of world trade must pass and which few routes can bypass.
Strategic petroleum reserve
Oil stocks held by governments, or by companies on their orders, to be released when supplies are cut.
Term premium
The extra return investors demand for lending for a long period rather than rolling over short-term loans; it rises with uncertainty about inflation and with heavy government borrowing.
Reference forecast
The name the IMF gave its central projection in April 2025 and April 2026, in place of "baseline", to stress that it rested on assumptions that might well not hold.
Net energy importer
A country that buys more energy from abroad than it sells, and so loses income when energy prices rise.
