Part III · Country lens 1 of 7

United States

The United States began the century as the engine of the world economy and ends this book still at its centre: its interest rates, its dollar and now its tariffs set conditions everywhere. In between it went through two recessions and two very different recoveries, cut interest rates to zero and bought trillions of dollars of bonds, ran up public debt to levels last seen after the Second World War and, from 2018, turned against open trade. This lens follows the American story across the book's nine phases and asks what it has meant for India.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Phase 3: The global financial crisis, Theme 3: Money and Theme 2: Trade

In brief

American growth averaged 2.7 percent a year from 2000 to 2007 and 2.4 percent in the 2010s, faster than the euro area's 1.4 percent. The housing bust of 2007–09 became a world financial crisis; the Federal Reserve held its policy rate near zero for seven years and bought bonds on a vast scale. The pandemic brought a deeper but much shorter recession, then the highest inflation in four decades and a rapid run of rate rises, followed by a soft landing. Public debt rose from 53.5 percent of GDP in 2001 to 124.4 percent in 2025, and the IMF projects more than 140 percent by 2031. From 2018, and on a far larger scale from 2025, the United States raised tariffs; in February 2026 the Supreme Court struck down the emergency-powers tariffs, and others took their place. The US share of world output has fallen as China and India have grown, but US interest rates still set the tone for borrowing costs worldwide. For India the United States is the largest export market, the biggest source of remittances and, in 2025, the source of a 50 percent tariff that was cut to 10 percent within a year.

Two busts, two recoveries

The century opened with the end of the dot-com boom (see Phase 1). Growth fell from 4.1 percent in 2000 to 1.0 percent in 2001 (chart US.1). The President's economic report of February 2002 described an economy that had stalled before the attacks of 11 September tipped it into recession, and still planned on "continuing the retirement of the Federal debt".1 The recovery that followed relied on record borrowing from abroad, and household saving fell to record lows. By the first quarter of 2005 the current-account deficit had reached 6½ percent of GDP. The IMF, in its annual review of the US economy (its Article IV consultation), judged the financial flows behind it unsustainable, but on housing it agreed with US officials that "house price stagnation was the most likely scenario", and it called the financial sector "exceptionally resilient".2 As late as July 2007 it expected "a soft landing" and found the system resilient to the early subprime losses.3 A year later, ten weeks before Lehman Brothers failed, it called the downturn the "first national-level housing bust in at least 40 years" but still leaned towards raising rates once recovery was established.4

The economy shrank by 2.6 percent in 2009 and recovered slowly. In 2010 the IMF found long-term unemployment at record levels.5 In 2013 it called that year's fiscal tightening "excessively rapid and ill designed",6 and in 2017 it summed up the expansion: "growth has been too low and too unequal".7 By 2019 the expansion was the longest on record, with unemployment the lowest since the late 1960s.8

The pandemic recession was deeper in its first months but much shorter: output fell 2.1 percent in 2020 and grew 6.3 percent in 2021. Then inflation took hold. Consumer prices rose 8.0 percent in 2022 after averaging 1.8 percent a year in the 2010s; the inflation measure the Fed targets peaked at 7.1 percent in mid-2022, the highest since the early 1980s.9 In June 2022 the IMF expected the economy "to narrowly avoid a recession" as rates rose.10 It did better than that. In 2024 the IMF described "a remarkable performance", with 16 million jobs created since the end of 2020 and no lasting scarring from the pandemic.9 Growth was 3.0 percent in 2024 and 2.3 percent in 2025; it averaged 2.7 percent a year in 2022–25, against 2.2 percent for advanced economies as a group, and the IMF's 2026 review credited strong productivity growth for setting the US "apart from its peers".11

The Fed and the price of money

The Federal Reserve's response to each shock has shaped borrowing costs worldwide (chart US.2). In November 2008 it announced purchases of mortgage-backed securities, and on 16 December 2008 it cut the federal funds rate target to a range of 0 to ¼ percent, promising to "employ all available tools". In March 2009 it raised its planned purchases of mortgage securities to $1.25 trillion and added $300 billion of Treasury bonds.12 The rate stayed below 0.25 percent for 85 months. In September 2012 a third round of quantitative easing, open-ended this time, added $40 billion a month of mortgage securities; the first rate rise came only on 16 December 2015.13 The Fed's balance sheet grew from $0.89 trillion at the end of 2007 to more than $4 trillion (chart US.3).

In March 2020 the Fed cut rates to zero again in two emergency moves and then promised to buy bonds "in the amounts needed".14 Its assets peaked at $8.95 trillion in April 2022. By then it had started raising rates, from March 2022, to a range of 5¼ to 5½ percent by July 2023, the 5.25 points of increases the IMF cited.159 The first cut came with a half-point move on 18 September 2024. After a further cut that December the range stood at 4¼–4½ percent, and three cuts in late 2025 took the range to 3½–3¾ percent.16 Then the 2026 energy shock intervened (see Phase 9): after holding for most of the year, the Fed raised its range to 3¾–4 percent on 16 September 2026, saying this would support "a timelier return" to its 2 percent inflation goal.17 Long-term rates rose too: the 10-year Treasury yield, which had fallen to 0.62 percent in July 2020, averaged 5.13 percent in October 2026, its highest monthly level since 2002.

A debt that keeps rising

America's fiscal position has worsened through good years as well as bad (chart US.4). General government debt was 53.5 percent of GDP in 2001, 87.1 percent in 2009 and 108.8 percent in 2019, before the pandemic took it to 132.6 percent. In 2010 the IMF warned that under current policies federal debt held by the public would rise from 64 to 95 percent of GDP by 2020, to "levels not seen since the years immediately following the Second World War".5 It got there: about 97 percent in fiscal 2023.9 In 2019, with unemployment at its lowest since the late 1960s, the IMF called US public debt "on an unsustainable path".8 The general government deficit averaged 7.6 percent of GDP in 2023–25, and the IMF projects deficits of 7 to 7½ percent and debt above 140 percent of GDP by 2031.11 In cash terms, Treasury figures show debt held by the public rising from $3.41 trillion in September 2000 to $32.90 trillion in November 2026.18

Contested question

Is US debt on a dangerous path?

Yes: deficits at full employment

The IMF has said so in every recent review: debt on "an unsustainable path" in 2019, and in 2024 and 2026 a needed adjustment of about 4 percent of GDP to reach a small primary surplus.8911 At its April 2026 review the IMF's Executive Directors also saw a risk of disorderly external rebalancing as the country's net foreign liabilities grow.11

No: growth and tariffs will fix it

The administration's 2026 economic report argued that the 2025 tax law, deregulation and tariff revenue would bring federal debt down to 94 percent of GDP in ten years, against 117 percent in its baseline.19

How the evidence weighs up. The two projections are not on the same measure: the report counts federal debt held by the public, the IMF general government gross debt. But they point in opposite directions. Past projections have tended to understate US debt: in 2019 the IMF expected general government debt of about 111 percent of GDP by 2028, and it was already 124.4 percent in 2025.811 With the 10-year yield above 5 percent in late 2026, the cost of carrying the debt is rising.

The turn against open trade

For most of the period the United States ran large trade deficits and championed open markets. That changed in 2018. A 25 percent tariff on steel took effect on 23 March 2018, on grounds of national security,20 and by 2019 tariffs on $200 billion of imports from China had been raised to 25 percent (see Phase 5).8 The IMF warned in 2019 that "tariff measures are likely to be ineffective" at reducing bilateral deficits.8 In 2024 it found that their cost was borne largely by US firms and consumers.9

2025 brought a far bigger shift (see Phase 8). Under emergency economic powers, an executive order of 2 April imposed an extra duty on imports from every trading partner, starting at 10 percent from 5 April, with higher country rates, India's at 26 percent. A further order of 31 July reset the rates: 25 percent for India and 15 percent for the European Union.21 On 20 February 2026 the Supreme Court ruled, 6–3, that the emergency-powers law "does not authorize the President to impose tariffs".22 The same day the President ended those tariffs and imposed a temporary 10 percent surcharge on imports for 150 days under a different law. In July 2026 tariffs of 10 to 12.5 percent on 60 economies were ordered under Section 301 of the Trade Act.23 The IMF expected the effective tariff rate to settle at 7 to 8.5 percent, and its Directors warned of sizeable spillovers to the rest of the world.11

Still the centre?

Measured at purchasing-power parity, the United States produced 20.4 percent of world output in 2000 and 14.6 percent in 2025 (chart US.5). China passed it on this measure in 2016, and India's share more than doubled, to 8.2 percent. America's financial weight is another matter: Theme 5 shows how US interest rates drive a global financial cycle in capital flows. The current-account deficit, at its widest in 2006 at 5.8 percent of GDP, was still 3.8 percent in 2025.

India and the United States

For India the United States matters as a market, as an employer of its people and as the source of financial shocks. It has long been India's largest export market. Exports to it grew from $11.5 billion in 2003-04 to $86.5 billion in 2024-25 (chart US.6), and India ran a trade surplus with the US of between about $17 billion and $21 billion a year from 2014-15 to 2018-19, while it ran a deficit with China of $53.6 billion in 2018-19.24 The US share of India's exports fell from 18.0 percent in 2003-04 to 10.1 percent in 2010-11, as exports to other markets grew faster, then climbed back to 19.8 percent in 2024-25 (chart US.7). The United States is also the biggest source of money sent home: the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI's) survey for 2023-24 found it supplied 27.7 percent of India's remittances, ahead of the United Arab Emirates (UAE).25

American shocks have reached India quickly. In 2008 foreign investors first poured money in, then pulled out $15.8 billion between February and June, and after Lehman's failure trade finance dried up; the 2008-09 Survey judged the impact "less severe" than elsewhere because India depended less on exports.26 In 2013 mere signals that the Fed would slow its bond purchases set off outflows and a sharp fall in the rupee, which averaged ₹63.75 to the dollar at its weakest in September; in July the RBI raised the rate on its marginal standing facility (its emergency overnight lending window for banks) by 2 percentage points, to 10.25 percent.27 The IMF had warned of exactly such spillovers that July, and US officials had been sceptical.6 In 2017 tighter scrutiny of US work visas worried India's software industry; the 2016-17 Survey argued that treating the movement of skilled people as an immigration issue harmed trade.28

The tariff turn of 2025 hit India hard and then eased. In February 2025 the two governments aimed to more than double bilateral trade to $500 billion by 2030 and to agree the first part of a trade deal by the autumn.29 Instead India faced a 26 percent tariff in April and 25 percent from August.21 On 6 August a further 25 percent was added because India was importing Russian oil, taking the total to 50 percent on most goods.30 The 2025-26 Survey said India had been "expected to be one of the early winners", so the penalty surprised many.25 In February 2026 a framework agreement cut the rate to 18 percent and removed the oil penalty, with India committing to stop buying Russian oil and saying it intended to buy $500 billion of American goods.3029 Two weeks later the Supreme Court's ruling removed the legal basis for that 18 percent; India then faced the temporary 10 percent surcharge and, from July 2026, a 10 percent Section 301 rate.23 A law signed in September 2026 threatened 100 percent tariffs on goods from the five largest buyers of Russian crude and gas; an order of 16 October waived them before they could apply.31 The rupee, ₹45.0 to the dollar in 2000, averaged ₹95.52 in October 2026.

Key terms

Federal funds rate
The overnight interest rate at which US banks lend to each other; the Fed sets a target range for it.
Quantitative easing
Large-scale purchases of bonds by a central bank, paid for with newly created money, to push down long-term interest rates.
Article IV consultation
The IMF's regular review of a member country's economy and policies.
Section 301
The part of the US Trade Act of 1974 that lets the government impose tariffs on countries whose trade practices it finds unfair.
