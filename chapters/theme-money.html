Money

At the start of the century central bankers believed they had mastered inflation. Within a decade they were fighting the opposite problem, with interest rates at zero and below and balance sheets swollen by bond purchases. Then inflation came back, faster than almost anyone expected, and they raised rates more quickly than at any time in forty years. This chapter follows the money: what central banks did, why, and how their thinking changed.

In brief

In 2000–2007 inflation in the advanced economies averaged 2.1 percent a year, close to the targets their central banks had set. The financial crisis pushed interest rates to zero: the Federal Reserve's stayed there for about 9 years in all, and the European Central Bank's deposit rate was below zero for 8. Central banks bought government bonds on a vast scale, and the Bank of Japan's assets came to exceed Japan's annual output. Through the 2010s the worry was inflation that stayed too low. Then, after the pandemic, prices surged: inflation in the advanced economies reached 7.3 percent in 2022. In that year 35 of the 39 central banks tracked by the Bank for International Settlements raised rates. Inflation came down without the deep recession many feared. By 2026 an energy shock was pushing prices up again, and central banks' independence was under political and fiscal strain. India adopted a formal inflation target in 2016; inflation has been lower and steadier since.

The age of the anchor

Inflation in the industrial countries averaged about 9 percent in the early 1980s; by the early 1990s it had settled at 2–3 percent a year and stayed there, close to the targets central banks had set, explicitly or implicitly.1 The tool was inflation targeting: a central bank, free of day-to-day political control, announces a number for inflation and moves its interest rate to hit it. If people believe the number, they set prices and wages accordingly, and the target becomes self-fulfilling. That belief is what economists call an anchor. In 2000–2007 inflation averaged 2.1 percent in the advanced economies and 6.9 percent in emerging and developing ones, which were bringing theirs down (chart T3.1).

Was it skill or luck? The IMF asked in 2006 how much credit belonged to globalisation. Cheap imports from China and elsewhere had helped: falling import prices cut inflation in the advanced economies by about half a percentage point in both 1998 and 1999. Global competition also seemed to make inflation less sensitive to how hard the domestic economy was running. But over the medium term, the IMF concluded, the anchor set inflation, and globalisation "cannot be relied upon" to keep it down once spare capacity ran out.1 Many emerging economies adopted inflation targets after the crises of the late 1990s showed the limits of fixed exchange rates. The IMF later found that their success owed more to domestic credibility, firmly anchored expectations, than to benign global conditions.2

Down to zero, and below

The financial crisis broke the routine (Phase 3). Central banks cut rates as far as they could. The Federal Reserve's reached a range of 0–0.25 percent in December 2008 and stayed there for seven years; the Bank of England's fell to 0.5 percent (chart T3.2). With the short-term rate at its lower bound, they turned to quantitative easing: creating money to buy government bonds and other assets, to push down long-term interest rates. The Federal Reserve's assets rose from 6.1 percent of US GDP at the end of 2007 to 19.1 percent in 2019; the European Central Bank's (ECB's) from 14.8 percent of euro area GDP to 38.5 percent; and the Bank of Japan's from 20.4 percent to 100.2 percent of Japan's GDP (chart T3.3).

Some went further. The ECB cut its deposit rate below zero in June 2014, so that banks paid to leave money with it, and took it to minus 0.5 percent by 2019. Switzerland, Sweden and Denmark did the same.3 The Bank of Japan announced negative rates on 29 January 2016 (they applied from 16 February) and, that September, a target of around zero for the ten-year government bond yield as well.3 The Bank for International Settlements, the central banks' own bank, warned in 2016 of diminishing returns: each new round of asset purchases moved bond yields less, and negative rates squeezed banks' margins. In Switzerland, banks initially raised mortgage rates to protect their profits.4

The puzzle of the decade was that inflation barely moved. Unemployment soared after 2008, yet prices did not fall much; then the economy recovered, and inflation stayed low. In the United States, core inflation, which leaves out food and energy, was below the Fed's 2 percent target in 97 percent of months from 2009 to 2020. The IMF's 2013 study, "The Dog That Didn't Bark", explained it by a much flatter Phillips curve, the link between slack and inflation, and by firmly anchored expectations: people expected 2 percent, so they set prices as if it would come. Its conclusion was that easy money carried little risk of inflation as long as central banks stayed independent.5 The Fed lifted its rate only in December 2015, to about 0.35 percent.4

The central banks redesigned their frameworks for this world. In August 2020 the Fed said that, because rates would hit the lower bound more often, it would aim for inflation that "averages 2 percent over time", letting it run moderately above 2 percent after periods below.6 In 2021 the ECB adopted a symmetric 2 percent target, under which undershooting was as undesirable as overshooting, and said that near the lower bound this might mean a period of inflation moderately above target.7 Both frameworks were built to fight the last war.

The surge

The pandemic brought the biggest monetary response yet (Phase 6). Rates went back to zero: by December 2020, 15 of the central banks in the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) data had policy rates at or below 0.25 percent. In 2020–2022 central bank balance sheets grew by more than 20 percentage points of GDP in Japan, the United Kingdom and the euro area, and by about 18 points in the United States, alongside government stimulus that averaged about 12 percent of GDP in the advanced economies and 4 percent in emerging markets.8 The Fed's assets peaked at 36.7 percent of GDP in 2021, the ECB's at 66.9 percent and the Bank of Japan's at 126.8 percent, in 2020.

When prices started rising in 2021, the IMF judged that they mostly reflected "transitory supply-demand mismatches" and that central banks could generally look through them.9 By mid-2022 global inflation had tripled from its pre-pandemic level.8 World inflation reached 8.7 percent in 2022, the highest in the period; in the advanced economies it went from 0.7 percent in 2020 to 7.3 percent. By April 2022 three-quarters of economies had inflation above 5 percent, and the BIS admitted it had not quite anticipated "the strength and persistence of the surge".10 US core inflation peaked at 5.6 percent in September 2022.

The two institutions told the story with different emphasis. The IMF stressed supply: demand had swung from services to goods during lockdowns and back again, colliding with bottlenecks, and then the war in Ukraine sent energy prices soaring (Phase 7). In Europe energy alone added more than 2.5 percentage points at the peak. It found little evidence that tight labour markets drove inflation at its peak, except in the United States after mid-2022.8 The BIS put demand first: a stronger rebound than expected, fuelled by huge stimulus and pent-up spending, which turned bottlenecks into lasting price pressure. It worried that the world might be "reaching a tipping point" from a low-inflation to a high-inflation regime, which tends to be self-reinforcing.10

The great tightening

Emerging-market central banks moved first. Brazil began raising rates in March 2021, a year before the Fed, as did Russia; Mexico followed in June and Chile in July (chart T3.4). The Bank of England moved in December 2021 and the Fed in March 2022; the ECB raised its deposit rate in July 2022, ending eight years of negative rates. Altogether 35 of 39 central banks in the BIS data raised rates in 2022. The Fed's rate went up by 5.25 percentage points, the Bank of England's by 5 and the ECB's by 4.5. The IMF called the rises unprecedented in speed, degree and breadth "in at least 40 years".11 The Bank of Japan, facing much lower inflation, stood apart.

The tightening worked better than feared. Before it, the BIS had estimated that the steeper rate paths in prospect could leave output 1.5–3 percent lower by 2025 than if rates stayed put.10 In the event, inflation fell faster than forecast in 2023–24, expectations did not come unanchored as in the 1970s, and there was no wage-price spiral. The IMF's 2024 review found that when bottlenecks are widespread and meet strong demand, the sacrifice ratio is low: tighter policy brings inflation down quickly at limited cost to output. Tightening three quarters earlier, it estimated, would have cut peak inflation by about 2 percentage points at a cost of 0.8 percent of GDP in 2022. Shrinking the balance sheets, quantitative tightening, had had minimal effects so far.8

The frameworks were rewritten again. In August 2025 the Fed dropped its promise to make up for past shortfalls in inflation and said it was "prepared to act forcefully" to keep expectations anchored.6 The ECB kept its 2 percent target but now defined symmetry as forceful action against large, sustained deviations "in either direction", expecting a more volatile inflation environment.7 Japan, the last holdout, ended negative rates and its yield target in March 2024, judging that a virtuous cycle of wages and prices had come into sight;3 its policy rate was 1.25 percent by October 2026.

Independence under strain

Central bank independence, the foundation of the 2000s consensus, came under pressure in 2025–26. In January 2026 the IMF reported that the dollar had come briefly under pressure after an investigation into the Federal Reserve chair was opened, and called central bank independence "paramount".12 The BIS warned in June 2026 that high public debt was straining the separation of fiscal and monetary policy, "long a cornerstone" of central bank independence and credibility, and that losses on the bonds central banks had bought were drawing criticism that could undermine their legitimacy.13

Then came a new shock. After the Strait of Hormuz was closed in early 2026, Brent crude rose by 67 percent to an intraday high of $120 in under two weeks (it went on to a daily peak of $138.21 in April; see Theme 8), and global inflation rose by half a percentage point within weeks (Phase 9). The BIS feared that, with the memory of 2021–23 fresh, expectations could come unanchored more quickly than in the past; on the other hand, labour markets were looser and policy rates higher than in 2022.13 The IMF projects inflation of 3.1 percent in the advanced economies in 2026. By the third quarter, 14 of 33 major central banks had raised rates since the second quarter, though the rises were modest.14

India: a target, at last

For most of the 2000s the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) had no single target. From 1998 it followed a "multiple indicator approach", watching a range of variables. That worked while inflation was moderate, averaging 4.7 percent a year in 2000-01 to 2007-08 (chart T3.5), but it lacked a clear anchor. After 2008 inflation took off, averaging 9.5 percent a year to 2013-14, with supply bottlenecks and sharp rises in farm support prices contributing. An RBI committee chaired by Urjit Patel, reporting in January 2014, noted that retail inflation had persisted at double-digit levels for six years. The committee recommended making inflation the anchor, measured by the consumer price index, with a target of 4 percent and a band of ±2 percentage points.15 The RBI raised its repo rate to 8.0 percent by March 2014.

The target became law in 2016. An amendment to the RBI Act made price stability the primary objective, "while keeping in mind the objective of growth", and handed rate decisions to a six-member Monetary Policy Committee, three from the RBI and three appointed by the government. The government set the target at 4 percent, with a tolerance band of 2–6 percent, from 5 August 2016. If inflation stays outside the band for three consecutive quarters, the RBI must explain to the government why, and what it will do.16 The government retained the target in 2021 and again, for 2026–2031, on 25 March 2026.17

The record is good, if uneven. Average inflation since inflation targeting began has been 4.9 percent, against 6.8 percent before, on the RBI's measure; the RBI describes the record as "hump-shaped", on target in the first three years and the last three and high in the middle.18 An RBI review in 2021 found that trend inflation had fallen from above 9 percent to about 4 percent.19 The new framework was not free of friction: in 2017 the Economic Survey argued that real interest rates were the highest in the recent past and that the scope for easing was "considerable", against the RBI's more cautious stance.20 During the pandemic the committee deliberately looked through high inflation to support growth, cutting the repo rate to 4.0 percent.18

The 2022 surge tested the framework. Inflation peaked at 7.8 percent in April 2022 and stayed above 6 percent for five quarters, triggering the first formal report to the government. The RBI raised the repo rate by 2.5 percentage points between May 2022 and February 2023, to 6.5 percent.1821 The episode was milder than elsewhere (chart T3.6): India's inflation was 6.6 percent in 2022-23, against an average of 9.6 percent in emerging and developing economies in 2022. The Economic Survey argued that India had chosen "not to overstimulate" during the pandemic.21 India had moved from being an inflation outlier in the early 2010s to one of the steadier emerging economies.

By 2025-26 inflation had fallen to 2.0 percent; the Economic Survey reported the lowest inflation in the current consumer price series, and the RBI cut its rate to 5.25 percent.22 The 2026 energy shock reversed that. As inflation rose again, on 7 October 2026 the committee raised the repo rate to 5.50 percent and changed its stance to "calibrated tightening".23 A decade after it was adopted, India's target faces the same question as the rest of the world's: how to respond to supply shocks that push prices up and growth down at once.

Key terms

Inflation targeting
A framework in which a central bank announces a numerical goal for inflation and sets interest rates to achieve it.
Zero lower bound
The point, at or near zero, below which central banks find it hard or costly to cut interest rates further.
Quantitative easing (QE)
Large-scale purchases of bonds by a central bank with newly created money, to lower long-term interest rates.
Quantitative tightening (QT)
Shrinking the central bank's balance sheet by letting bonds mature or selling them.
Negative interest rates
A policy rate below zero, so that banks pay to keep money at the central bank.
Phillips curve
The relationship between slack in the economy and inflation.
Sacrifice ratio
The output lost for each point by which inflation is brought down.
Central bank independence
The freedom of a central bank to set rates without government instruction.
