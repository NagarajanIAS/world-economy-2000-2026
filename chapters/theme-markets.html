Stock markets and capital flows

Share prices crashed three times in this period and recovered each time, ending it higher than ever and more dependent on a handful of American technology companies. Money flowing into emerging economies changed shape: the reserve hoards of the 2000s grew far more slowly, direct investment dwindled and portfolio money came and went with the mood in New York. This chapter follows the markets, the arguments over capital controls, and India's turn from foreign to home-grown investors.

2000–2026 · about 20 minutes · Read with Phase 1: The bust and the turn, Phase 3: The global financial crisis, Phase 6: Pandemic shock and response and Theme 3: Money

In brief

Stock markets fell hard in 2000–02, in 2008–09 and in 2020, and were shaken again by US tariffs in 2025 and by war in 2026. Each time they recovered. By October 2026 US share prices were about three and a half times their level of January 2000, and Indian share prices more than thirteen times. US shares are expensive by historical standards, and the market is more concentrated in a few technology giants than it was at the height of the dot-com bubble. American companies made up about half the value of the world's listed companies in 2024. Money flows to emerging economies have changed: the reserve build-up of the 2000s has slowed to a trickle, net direct investment has dwindled and portfolio money surges and retreats with global risk appetite. The IMF, once a champion of open capital accounts, now accepts controls in some circumstances. India's stock market has become one of the world's largest and is now owned more by Indian institutions than by foreign investors.

Three crashes and two scares

The century opened with a bust. The Nasdaq index, home of technology shares, averaged 4,803 in March 2000 and fell 74 percent to 1,242 by October 2002; it did not regain its March 2000 level until 2015 (Phase 1). By the end of 2002 share prices in the United States were at 76 on an index of January 2000 = 100, in Japan at 51 and in Germany at 45 (chart T5.1).

The boom that followed was strongest in emerging markets: China's share prices reached 394 in October 2007 and India's 366 that December. In April 2007 the IMF judged that strong growth, "led by China and India", underpinned financial stability; the fallout from bad US subprime loans had been limited, and its financial effects "may also be contained". It did warn that volatility was near historic lows, that investors were borrowing cheaply to chase yield, and that a reversal of flows into some emerging markets "cannot be ruled out".1 By October 2008 the selling of assets to repay debt had, in its words, "accelerated and become disorderly", and it expected losses on US loans and securities of about $1.4 trillion.2 From peak to trough, monthly average share prices fell 53 percent in the United States, 57 percent in Japan, 67 percent in China and 55 percent in India. The VIX, an index of expected volatility in US shares often called the fear gauge, averaged 63 in November 2008, its highest monthly average since the series began in 1990 (chart T5.2).

The pandemic brought what the IMF called "the fastest drop in history": the S&P 500 fell 20 percent from its peak in just 16 trading sessions, and foreign investors pulled more than $100 billion out of emerging-market shares and bonds, a record.3 Money market funds and even the US Treasury market seized up until the Federal Reserve stepped in.4 The VIX averaged 58 in March 2020. The recovery was fast: US share prices were at 250 at the end of 2021, against 204 at the end of 2019 (Phase 6).

Two scares followed. When the United States announced tariffs larger than expected in April 2025, volatility jumped to levels not seen since the pandemic and stock markets plunged; "unusually for a risk-off episode", the Bank for International Settlements noted, the dollar fell rather than rose, as investors hedged their US holdings. Markets recovered once the largest tariffs were postponed.5 When war broke out in the Middle East in 2026, world share prices fell 8 percent between February and mid-April; the IMF said markets had corrected "in an orderly manner so far", with the biggest falls in emerging economies that import energy (Phase 9).6

Expensive, and concentrated

How expensive are shares? One widely used gauge is Robert Shiller's cyclically adjusted price-earnings ratio, or CAPE: the price of US shares divided by the average of the previous ten years' earnings, both adjusted for inflation. It reached a record 44.2 in December 1999, fell to 13.3 in March 2009, and was 39.4 in November 2026 (chart T5.3). Its average since 1881 is 17.8, and in only 29 months since then has it been higher than in November 2026.7 The IMF, which uses forecasts of earnings rather than past earnings, reaches a milder verdict: in October 2025 it judged US shares overvalued, but by less than in the dot-com bubble.8 By April 2026 it found prices above fair value in most advanced economies, with the gap "well below historical peaks".6 Which verdict one accepts depends on the measure.

The IMF's bigger worry was concentration. In October 2025 information technology firms made up 35 percent of the S&P 500, as at the dot-com peak, but seven firms alone (Alphabet, Amazon, Apple, Meta, Microsoft, Nvidia and Tesla) made up 33 percent, and a measure of concentration was "substantially higher than during the dot-com bubble". If these firms failed to earn returns that justified their spending on artificial intelligence, the IMF warned, a sudden, sharp correction was possible. Investment in data centres and related equipment had supplied about 57 percent of US growth since late 2024.8 The seven firms' share of the index had risen from 20 to 30 percent between January and October 2024.9

By late 2026 the boom was being financed increasingly by debt. The Federal Reserve wrote that equity prices were high, "partly driven by market enthusiasm for super intelligence"; borrowing linked to advanced computing passed $680 billion in 2026 by October, about five times the 2023 figure, and the Fed compared the build-out of data centres to the telecoms investment cycle of the late 1990s.10 The IMF named an AI-related correction as one of three salient risks to financial stability, and counted 14 "circular" deals since 2023, in which AI firms invest in, lend to and buy from one another, involving about $300 billion of equity, $650 billion of debt and $1.4 trillion of commitments to buy.11

America's market and everyone else's

The United States has come to dominate the world's stock markets. By the World Bank's figures American companies made up 48.9 percent of the value of the world's listed companies in 2000. As other markets boomed in the 2000s the share fell to 33.0 percent in 2007; it then climbed back, to 43.3 percent in 2019 and 54.0 percent in 2024 (chart T5.4). Japan's share halved, from 10.2 percent in 2000 to 5.5 percent in 2024. It fell from 7.9 percent in 2019 even though Japanese share prices rose from 104 to 165 on the index of chart T5.1 between the end of 2019 and the end of 2024. China's peaked at 13.0 percent in 2021 and India's rose from 0.7 to 4.5 percent.

Against the size of the economy, the US market was worth 212 percent of GDP in 2024, against 147 percent in 2000; Japan's was 151 percent, China's 63 percent and Brazil's 30 percent (chart T5.5). This makes the rest of the world exposed to Wall Street. Foreigners' gains on their US shares had pushed their holdings up by nearly $6 trillion, the IMF noted in 2026, and US households held about 30 percent of their assets in shares, so a fall in US prices would spread widely.6

Capital flows: glut, cycle and control

In 2005 Ben Bernanke, then a Federal Reserve governor, offered an explanation for low long-term interest rates and the US current account deficit: a global saving glut. After the financial crises of the 1990s, he argued, emerging economies had turned from borrowers into "large net lenders", building up foreign-exchange reserves as war chests and investing them in US assets.12 Chart T5.6 shows the scale. The reserves of emerging and developing economies grew by 7.2 percent of their GDP in 2007 alone, and by about $3.9 trillion over 2003–08. It slowed sharply in the 2010s, and in 2015 and 2016 reserves fell.

In 2013 Hélène Rey of London Business School put the stress elsewhere: on a global financial cycle. Prices of risky assets worldwide move together, she showed, with a single global factor explaining about a quarter of the variation in returns, and capital flows are larger when the VIX is low; US monetary policy drives much of the cycle. A floating exchange rate, she concluded, does not by itself give a country control of its own monetary policy: that is possible only if the capital account is managed, directly or through rules on bank lending.13 The same year supplied a test. When Fed officials hinted in May 2013 that they would slow their bond purchases, the Bank for International Settlements (BIS) wrote, the news "caught many market participants by surprise"; US long-term yields rose a full percentage point by early July, emerging-market share prices fell 16 percent and investors pulled money out of emerging-market funds of every kind (Phase 4).14

The IMF's own position shifted. Its "institutional view" of 2012 still saw substantial benefits in free capital flows, but stated that there was "no presumption that full liberalization is an appropriate goal" for all countries at all times, and accepted capital flow management measures in some circumstances, as long as they were temporary and targeted and did not replace needed changes in economic policy.15 A review in 2022 went further, allowing some such measures to be used pre-emptively, before any surge of inflows, where borrowing in foreign currency threatened the financial system.16

Flows have since changed shape. Net direct investment into emerging and developing economies averaged 1.4 percent of their GDP in 2000–2025 but was only 0.3 percent in 2024. Portfolio flows have turned outward, with residents investing more abroad than foreigners invested at home: a net outflow of 1.2 percent of GDP in 2022. The IMF describes the pattern as "K-shaped", tilted towards debt and short-term bets on interest-rate differences rather than direct investment, and finds that just 25 of 101 emerging markets received more than 90 percent of all direct investment into emerging markets in 2022–24.17

From banks to funds

The 2008 crisis began in banks. Since then the weight has moved to non-bank financial institutions, such as investment funds, insurers and hedge funds, whose assets grew from 167 to 224 percent of world GDP between 2009 and 2023 while banks' rose only from 164 to 177 percent.18 The stress points have moved with them: money market funds and leveraged trades in Treasury bonds in March 2020;3 the abrupt unwinding of trades funded by borrowing cheaply in yen in August 2024, after the Bank of Japan raised its rate;9 and leveraged bond trades again in April 2025, when dealers reportedly reached the limits of what they could absorb.19

India: from foreign money to its own savers

For most of this period India's stock market moved with foreign money. In 2007-08 a surge of inflows brought the economy close to "the limits of its absorptive capacity", the Economic Survey wrote. The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) bought dollars and soaked up the extra rupees by selling special bonds (sterilisation), the government restricted the use of foreign borrowing by companies, and it described its commitment to fuller convertibility of the rupee as gradual and sequenced.20 Then the flow reversed. In 2008-09 foreign institutional investors took out $15 billion, after putting in $20.3 billion the year before, selling Indian shares to raise cash at home;21 Indian share prices fell 55 percent from peak to trough. Net portfolio investment swung from an outflow of $14.0 billion in 2008-09 to an inflow of $32.4 billion the next year (chart T5.7). In the 2013 taper tantrum the rupee was among five currencies that fell more than 10 percent against the dollar, and India lost more than $10 billion of reserves.14

Direct investment was steadier. Net foreign direct investment (FDI) averaged $36.9 billion a year from 2015-16 to 2021-22, then fell to $1.0 billion in 2024-25 as foreign owners took money home and Indian firms invested abroad. The Survey called the rise in repatriation "a success story as foreigners take profits".22 Gross inflows remained large, $81 billion in 2024-25, and net FDI recovered to $5.6 billion in April–November 2025 and $13.8 billion in April–August 2026.2324

The bigger change was at home. Demat accounts, needed to hold shares, rose to 22.5 crore (225 million) by March 2026,25 and regular monthly investment plans in mutual funds brought in over ₹28,000 crore (₹280 billion) a month on average in April–November 2025, seven times as much as in 2016-17.23 The market grew with them: the value of companies on the Bombay Stock Exchange rose from 26 percent of GDP at the end of March 2002 to 134 percent in March 2024 (chart T5.8), making India "the world's fifth-largest stock market".25 Indian institutions overtook foreign investors as owners of listed shares in early 2025.23

The test came in 2025-26. Foreign portfolio investors (FPIs) made India's "highest-ever FPI net equity outflows", $19.7 billion, blaming high valuations, slowing profits, the falling rupee, oil and a shift of emerging-market money towards AI chipmakers in Taiwan and Korea. Indian institutions bought a record ₹8.5 lakh crore (₹8.5 trillion) net, and the foreign share of listed companies fell to a 15-year low of 15.8 percent. The Nifty 50 index still fell 15.2 percent from its daily peak in early January 2026 (on the monthly averages of chart T5.1 the peak was in December 2025).25 Measured in dollars, India's gains look smaller: as the rupee fell from 43.59 to the dollar in January 2000 to 95.52 in October 2026, the index of 1,354 in rupees became 618 in dollars (chart T5.9).

Not all the new money was well spent. The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) found that 91.1 percent of individuals trading futures and options lost money in 2023-24, ₹1.81 lakh crore (₹1.81 trillion) in total over three years, while the profits went mostly to trading firms and foreign investors using algorithms.26 After it tightened the rules in November 2024, the number of active traders fell 18 percent in 2025-26, though SEBI did not claim the rules were the cause, and 87.7 percent still lost money.27 The 2023-24 Survey, published in July 2024, warned that India "can ill-afford the economy's over financialisation";28 the next two welcomed the rise in participation, the 2026 Survey calling it "portfolio diversification rather than displacement" of bank deposits.2923

Key terms

CAPE
Share prices divided by ten-year average earnings, both adjusted for inflation.
VIX
An index of the volatility in US share prices implied by option prices.
Foreign direct investment (FDI)
Investment that gives a lasting stake in a company.
Portfolio investment
Purchases of shares and bonds without a controlling or lasting stake.
Global financial cycle
The tendency of asset prices and capital flows worldwide to rise and fall together.
Capital flow management measures
Rules or taxes that limit flows of money into or out of a country.
Non-bank financial institutions
Funds, insurers, pension funds and other lenders and investors that are not banks.
Demat account
An electronic account in India for holding shares and other securities.
