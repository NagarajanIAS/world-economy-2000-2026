Part III · Country lens 2 of 7

China

China's rise runs through every phase of this book. It joined the World Trade Organization at the end of 2001 and grew at double-digit rates for a decade, became the world's largest exporter and, after 2008, kept growing by borrowing heavily. Since the mid-2010s growth has slowed, the property market has slumped and prices have stopped rising. This lens follows China across the book's nine phases and asks what its rise has meant for India.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Phase 2: The great boom, Theme 2: Trade and Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy

In brief

China's economy grew by an average of 10.3 percent a year from 2000 to 2011, 7.1 percent in 2012–19 and 5.4 percent in 2021–25, and the IMF expects about 3.8 percent a year to 2031. In dollars its output went from 11.9 percent of America's in 2000 to 63.6 percent in 2025. Growth rested on very high saving and investment, a large trade surplus in the 2000s and, after 2008, a credit boom that more than doubled total credit to companies, households and government relative to GDP. The IMF urged a shift from investment and exports towards consumption in almost every annual review; in its 2026 review it was still the first priority. Since 2021 a property slump and near-zero inflation have dominated. For India, China is the largest source of imports and of its trade deficit, a model it has struggled to copy, and a supplier of inputs it is trying, with limited success, to rely on less.

The workshop of the world

China became the 143rd member of the World Trade Organization (WTO) on 11 December 2001 (see Phase 1).1 Growth reached 12.7 percent in 2006 and 14.2 percent in 2007, on today's IMF figures (chart CN.1). The statistics bureau's first estimate for 2006 had been 10.7 percent, with exports up 27.2 percent and a trade surplus of $177.5 billion.2 In 2004 the IMF described an investment surge driven partly by local-government projects, with fixed investment reaching 44 percent of GDP in 2003, and hoped for "a soft landing".3

Behind the growth was an unusual pattern of saving. China's gross national saving rose to 51.0 percent of GDP in 2008, more than it invested, and the gap showed up as a current-account surplus that peaked at 9.8 percent of GDP in 2007 (chart CN.2). That surplus, and the reserves the central bank piled up, put China at the centre of the global imbalances debate. Its foreign-exchange reserves grew from $0.17 trillion in 2000 to a peak of $3.90 trillion in 2014. In 2004 the IMF found it "difficult to find persuasive evidence that the renminbi is substantially undervalued"; a year later it saw "increased undervaluation" and urged a move. The authorities saw "no convincing evidence" of undervaluation.4 The IMF's 2006 review asked for the economy to be "rebalanced away from heavy dependence on export-led growth".5

The currency had been held at about 8.28 yuan to the dollar. On 21 July 2005 the People's Bank of China revalued it by about 2 percent and began managing it against a basket of currencies (chart CN.4).4 It then rose gradually, to 6.84 to the dollar in July 2008, when it was re-pegged to the dollar.6 By the end of 2015 the central bank counted a rise of 27.46 percent against the dollar since the 2005 reform.7

Stimulus and the credit machine

China met the 2008 crisis with an infrastructure-heavy fiscal stimulus and what the IMF called "An extraordinary credit expansion": new bank loans equal to 31 percent of GDP in 2009 alone.6 Growth, 9.4 percent in 2009 and 10.6 percent in 2010, held up while the rich world shrank, and in 2010 the IMF said this policy response had "ensured that China has led the global recovery".6 It also flagged the cost. Local governments, barred from borrowing directly, had sharply increased their use of companies they owned, now known as local-government financing vehicles; and in the larger cities "a property bubble is beginning to inflate".6 Investment rose to 46.3 percent of GDP in 2011.

Credit kept growing faster than the economy (chart CN.3). Bank for International Settlements data show credit to non-financial companies rising from 88.4 percent of GDP at the end of 2008 to 151.8 percent in early 2016, and credit to households from 17.6 to 61.0 percent by early 2024. Including government, total credit to the non-financial sector went from 133 percent of GDP at the end of 2008 to 305 percent in early 2026. In 2013 the IMF wrote, "This pattern of growth is not sustainable and is raising vulnerabilities". It noted that total financing had risen by 60 percent of GDP in four years, warned that shadow-banking products could become a threat to the whole system, and estimated that augmented government debt, counting the financing vehicles, was 45 percent of GDP in 2012.8 In 2016 it warned that vulnerabilities were "still rising on a dangerous trajectory".9 In 2017 it estimated the excess of credit over its trend at about 25 percent of GDP.10

The strain showed in 2015 (see Phase 5). The yuan had reached its strongest, 6.05 to the dollar, in January 2014. On 11 August 2015 the central bank changed how it set the daily reference rate, tying it to the previous day's market close.7 The yuan ended the year 5.77 percent lower against the dollar. Capital outflows in 2015 and early 2016 exceeded the current-account surplus and drew down reserves; the IMF judged that the August change had "seemingly reinforced" expectations of further falls.9 The IMF called the currency broadly in line with fundamentals from 2016 to 2019.11

Property, deflation and a slower path

Local governments had long relied on selling land for revenue, which made the property market central to public finances.8 The market turned in 2021. In August 2020 the authorities had begun "imposing stringent benchmarks (the “three red lines”)" on developers' borrowing, and many developers fell into financial distress.12 Real estate investment fell by about a fifth in 2022, a year of COVID restrictions in which growth was 3.1 percent (see Phase 7); in December 2022 the IMF wrote that "the real estate crisis remains unresolved".13 It was still falling in 2025, by 17.5 percent.14 By the IMF's 2026 review the contraction was in its fourth year: housing investment had fallen from 12.3 percent of GDP in 2020 to 6.1 percent, more than half of private developers were still in distress, and land sales had fallen from 2.4 percent of GDP in 2020 to 0.7 percent.15

Prices stopped rising. Consumer price inflation was 0.2 percent in 2023, 0.2 percent in 2024 and 0.1 percent in 2025, and the broadest measure of prices in the economy fell three years running.15 The IMF had expected core inflation of 2 percent in 2023 and headline inflation of 1.3 percent in 2024.1316 Prices began to rise in 2026, but mainly because of energy: producer prices were up 5.7 percent in October on the year, consumer prices 0.7 percent (see Phase 9). The statistics bureau itself called "the domestic imbalance between strong supply and weak demand" acute.17 Slow price growth has pushed down the real exchange rate, which in October 2026 was 92.9 against a peak of 107.3 in March 2022 (2020 = 100). The IMF judged it undervalued by 12 to 21 percent in 2025, and said real depreciation had lifted the current-account surplus.15

Public debt has risen fast (chart CN.5). General government gross debt was 28.8 percent of GDP in 2007 and 98.6 percent in 2025, with a deficit of 7.9 percent of GDP, and the IMF projects 122.1 percent by 2031. On the IMF's augmented measure it was 126.6 percent of GDP in 2025 and heading for 153.7 percent in 2030.15 The population is now falling: it was 1,404.89 million at the end of 2025, 3.39 million fewer than a year earlier (see Theme 9).14

Contested question

Can China grow by consuming more?

It must: the old model is exhausted

The IMF's 2026 review put the "transition to a consumption-led growth model, away from overreliance on exports and investment" first, arguing that state-led, debt-financed investment and industrial policy had weakened productivity and created excess supply. It found "the policy steps remain modest relative to the scale of the challenges".15 The World Bank noted that social protection spending, about 11 percent of GDP, is about half the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) average, which keeps households saving.18

It can grow by innovating

The authorities expect growth to average 4.2 percent a year to 2035, enough to meet their goal of reaching the level of a mid-level developed economy. The recommendations for the 2026–30 Five-Year Plan stress innovation and self-reliance alongside domestic demand.15

How the evidence weighs up. The record favours the IMF's diagnosis but also shows how slowly it has been acted on. Investment was still 38.9 percent of GDP in 2025, against 34.9 percent in India and 21.6 percent in the United States. Of 2025's 5.0 percent growth, 1.6 points came from net exports and 2.6 from consumption.14 The current-account surplus has widened again, to 3.7 percent of GDP. The IMF's projection of about 3½ percent growth by 2030 is well below the authorities' aim.15

Trade conflict

China's export success became a political target. The United States put tariffs on most Chinese goods in 2018–19 (see Phase 5 and the US lens). The "Phase One" agreement signed on 15 January 2020 required China to buy, over 2020 and 2021, at least $200 billion more of American goods and services than in 2017.19 The conflict escalated far further in 2025 (see Phase 8). US tariffs on Chinese goods peaked at over 100 percent in April; a one-year truce from October 2025 suspended part of them and China's export controls on rare earths until November 2026.15 American imports from China fell 29 percent in 2025, and China's share of US imports fell to 9.3 percent, from more than 20 percent before 2018. China remained the world's largest exporter, with 14.4 percent of world merchandise exports.20 Its exports grew 17.6 percent in yuan terms in the first ten months of 2026.17

In 2025 growth was 5.0 percent, which met the government's target, and the IMF's 2026 review called the economy "remarkably resilient despite facing multiple shocks".15 The World Bank reports a target of 4.5–5 percent for 2026; the IMF projects 4.5 percent, slowing to 3.3 percent by 2031.18 China's output per head at purchasing-power parity rose from 7.3 percent of America's in 2000 to 32.5 percent in 2025.

India and China

For India, China is three things: a supplier, a source of a large trade deficit and a model it has not been able to follow. Imports from China rose from $4.1 billion in 2003-04 to $113.4 billion in 2024-25 and, on provisional figures, $131.6 billion in 2025-26 (chart CN.6). China's share of India's imports rose from 5.2 percent in 2003-04 to 15.7 percent in 2024-25 (chart CN.7). In 2011-12 China displaced the United Arab Emirates (UAE) as India's largest trading partner, and the 2012-13 Survey noted the "growing trade deficit of India with China": $39.4 billion in 2011-12, up from $28 billion.21 By 2016-17 China accounted for 47.3 percent of India's trade deficit, against 19.9 percent in 2011-12.22 In 2024-25 India exported $14.25 billion of goods to China, 14.5 percent less than a year earlier and only 3.3 percent of its exports, and imported $113.45 billion, a gap of about $99 billion.23

The gap between the two economies has widened. In 2000 China's output per head at purchasing-power parity was 1.4 times India's; by 2025 it was 2.5 times. India has grown faster since 2021, 7.9 percent a year in 2021–25 against China's 5.4 percent, but it has not repeated China's manufacturing-led path. The 2014-15 Survey observed that, unlike in China, "manufacturing has not been this escalator in India", blaming distortions in labour, capital and land markets.24 India now invests almost as large a share of its output: 34.9 percent of GDP in 2025, against China's 38.9 percent.

The pandemic turned dependence into a policy issue. The 2020-21 Survey said COVID-19 had "exposed the excessive dependence of Indian pharmaceutical industry on China" for drug ingredients, and production-linked incentive (PLI) schemes were launched for these and other sectors.25 Press Note 3 of 17 April 2020 required investment from any country sharing a land border with India, China among them, to get government approval.26 Yet imports from China rose from $65.2 billion in 2020-21 to more than twice that by 2025-26, and the 2024-25 Survey reported that "India sources 75 per cent of lithium-ion batteries from China". It noted that China held nearly 80 percent of the world's battery-making capacity and about 90 percent of rare-earth processing.27 The risk was not hypothetical: the 2025-26 Survey recorded China's tightening of export licences for rare earths and magnets.23

The Surveys have since shifted towards engagement. The 2023-24 Survey argued that "the world cannot completely look past China, even as it pursues China plus one": as firms moved production out of China, India "may not be an immediate beneficiary", and of its two options, promoting investment from China would serve it better than joining Chinese supply chains.28 There were early gains: Apple assembled about $14 billion of iPhones in India in 2023-24, 14 percent of its global output, and India's electronics trade with the United States moved from a $0.6 billion deficit in 2016-17 to an $8.7 billion surplus.28 The 2025-26 Survey accepted that "global manufacturing is anchored in China’s scale and integrated industrial systems", which no tariff wall can override, and recommended lowering India's cost of capital rather than more protection. It reported that over ten years India had overtaken China as the leading destination for investment in Asia after the United States, while China's share of India's imports rose to 16.4 percent in April–November 2025.23

Key terms

Local-government financing vehicle
A company set up by a Chinese local government to borrow and invest in infrastructure and property on its behalf, outside its official budget.
Augmented government debt
The IMF's wider measure of China's public debt, adding the borrowing of local-government financing vehicles and government funds to the official figures.
Shadow banking
Lending by institutions and products outside the regulated banking system, such as trust companies and wealth-management products.
Rebalancing
A shift in the sources of a country's growth, here from investment and exports towards household consumption.
Production-linked incentive (PLI) scheme
An Indian government programme, begun in 2020, that pays firms incentives linked to their extra output in chosen manufacturing sectors.
