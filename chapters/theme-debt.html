Debt and fiscal policy

Governments borrowed heavily twice in this period, to fight the financial crisis and then the pandemic, and never paid the debt back down. For a decade it hardly seemed to matter, because interest rates were lower than economic growth. Now rates are higher, the interest bill is rising and new demands, from defence to ageing, are pressing on budgets. This chapter follows the debt, the arguments about it and India's attempt to put a ceiling on its own.

In brief

Government debt in the advanced economies was about 70 percent of GDP in 2007. The financial crisis took it to 102 percent by 2012 and the pandemic to 119 percent in 2020; it has not come down much since. In emerging and developing economies debt fell in the 2000s to 33 percent of GDP in 2008, then more than doubled, to 76 percent in 2026, led by China. For the 2010s, low interest rates made this look affordable, and some economists argued that debt might cost nothing. Since 2022 rates have risen and interest bills are climbing: the IMF projects that world government debt will reach 100 percent of GDP by 2029. India's debt is above the emerging-market average and about a quarter of its government revenue goes on interest; in 2025 it adopted a target to cut the central government's debt to about 50 percent of GDP by 2031.

Two jumps and a climb

The story of public debt since 2000 is told by two steps and a slope (chart T4.1). In the boom years before 2008 the debt of the advanced economies held steady at about 68–70 percent of GDP. The financial crisis then added 32 percentage points in five years, as recession cut tax revenue and governments rescued banks and launched stimulus programmes (Phase 3). The pandemic added another 18 points in a single year. In neither case did the ratio go back to where it had been.

The emerging and developing economies told a different story. Through the 2000s their debt fell, to 32.8 percent of GDP in 2008. Then it began to climb, and has not stopped. China's government debt, by the IMF's count, rose from 27 percent of GDP in 2008 to 104 percent in 2026, partly because of a change in how the IMF counts the borrowing of local government financing vehicles, companies set up by local governments to fund infrastructure.1 The IMF now puts world government debt at 93.9 percent of GDP in 2025, rising to 100 percent by 2029, "a level previously reached only in the aftermath of World War II".1

The big economies differ widely (chart T4.2). Japan's debt peaked at 229 percent of GDP in 2020. Greece's reached 210 percent the same year, after a decade of crisis, bailouts and a restructuring of its debt to private creditors (Phase 4), and has since fallen to 136 percent. US debt, about 65 percent of GDP before the crisis, was 127 percent in 2026 and is projected to reach 145 percent by 2031. The number of economies with debt above 100 percent of GDP rose from 13 in 2007 to 19 in 2026.

Stimulus, austerity and the multiplier

When the financial crisis struck, the consensus was clear. In November 2008 the IMF said that with monetary policy struggling, "broad-based fiscal stimulus is likely warranted" wherever governments had room to borrow.2 The US deficit widened to 13.2 percent of GDP in 2009 and the advanced economies' combined deficit to 8.8 percent (chart T4.3).

By 2010 the mood had turned. The euro crisis made debt look dangerous, and many European governments announced large, multiyear cuts (Phase 4). Forecasters assumed that these would cost little growth. They were wrong. In October 2012 two IMF economists, Olivier Blanchard and Daniel Leigh, compared the forecasts made in 2010 with what happened. Countries that planned bigger cuts had done systematically worse than forecast: each extra percentage point of GDP of planned consolidation went with about 1 percent less output than expected. The forecasts had assumed a fiscal multiplier of about 0.5; the real multipliers had been between about 0.9 and 1.7.3 With interest rates already at zero, central banks could not offset the cuts, and households and firms short of cash spent less when their incomes fell. The authors stressed that their results did not mean consolidation was undesirable, only that its short-term cost had been underestimated.3

The lesson was applied in the pandemic. Governments spent on a scale never seen in peacetime: by September 2020 fiscal measures worldwide amounted to $11.7 trillion, close to 12 percent of global GDP, and by March 2021 to $16 trillion.4 The US deficit reached 14.1 percent of GDP in 2020 and the advanced economies' 10.2 percent. The deficits did not close afterwards. The United States ran a deficit of 7.1 percent of GDP in 2025 with its economy, in the IMF's words, operating near full capacity, and the IMF projects deficits of about 8 percent a year to 2031; it notes that such deficits would be unprecedented in peacetime with no slack in the economy.1 China's deficit, close to zero in 2007, peaked at 9.6 percent of GDP in 2020 and has stayed between about 6 and 8 percent since.

When debt seemed free

Why did debt not cause more alarm in the 2010s? Because borrowing was cheap. What matters for the debt ratio is the gap between the interest rate a government pays and the growth rate of nominal GDP, known as r minus g. If the rate is below growth, the ratio of debt to GDP tends to fall on its own, as long as the primary deficit, the deficit before interest, is not too large. In the United States the 10-year Treasury yield averaged 2.4 percent a year in 2010–2019, against nominal growth of 4.1 percent (chart T4.4).

In his presidential address to the American Economic Association, published in 2019, Blanchard drew the radical conclusion. Safe interest rates below growth were, he argued, "more the historical norm than the exception": in the United States in 1950–2018 the 10-year rate averaged 5.6 percent and nominal growth 6.3 percent; if the future was like the past, governments could roll over their debt without ever raising taxes to pay it, and "public debt may have no fiscal cost". He was careful: the paper was not, he said, an argument for more debt, and the future might be different.5 India's Economic Survey adopted the argument two years later (see below).

The future was different. When inflation surged in 2021–22, nominal growth jumped and debt ratios fell back from their 2020 peaks, but central banks raised rates (Theme 3). The US 10-year yield rose from 1.44 percent in 2021 to 4.3 percent in 2025, close to nominal growth of 5.0 percent. As old debt is refinanced at higher rates, the interest bill climbs. The IMF reports that interest payments rose from 2 percent to nearly 3 percent of world GDP in four years; US interest payments are projected to approach 5 percent of GDP by 2031.1 Measured against government revenue, US net interest rose from 7.6 percent in 2019 to 10.4 percent in 2025 and is projected at 16.5 percent in 2031 (chart T4.5). The Bank for International Settlements (BIS) warned in 2023 that high debt can hold monetary policy hostage, a condition known as fiscal dominance: a central bank may hesitate to raise rates if doing so could set off a crisis in the government bond market.6

What brings debt down? The IMF examined the record in 2023 and found that the average fiscal consolidation "has a negligible effect on debt ratios", because cuts slow growth and are often offset by transfers to state companies, hidden liabilities and currency falls. Consolidations succeed more often, more than three times in four rather than half the time, when the economy is growing, when debt is high and, in rich countries, when they rely on spending cuts rather than tax rises.7 In 2024 it estimated that stabilising debt with confidence would need budget tightening of 3–4½ percent of GDP on average, more than governments have managed in the past, and it noted that its own debt projections have been too optimistic, by about 6 percentage points of GDP three years ahead.8 New demands keep arriving: by April 2026 sixteen EU members used the escape clause in the bloc's fiscal rules to spend more on defence, spending the IMF judged "unlikely to be transitory".1

A new wave in the developing world

In poor countries, the 2000s began with relief. After the debt-relief schemes known as HIPC and MDRI, under which creditors forgave much of what the poorest countries owed, low-income countries' debt fell steeply between 2000 and 2010.9 Zambia's government debt fell from 261 percent of GDP in 2000 to 19 percent in 2008; Sub-Saharan Africa's average fell from 58 to 21 percent (chart T4.6).

It did not last. In a study published in 2020 the World Bank described a "fourth wave" of debt in developing economies, which began in 2010 and was "the largest, broadest, and fastest growing" of the four since 1970; the first three had all ended in crises. More of the borrowing came in riskier forms: from private bondholders and from lenders outside the Paris Club of creditor governments, notably China, sometimes with collateral and secrecy clauses.9 When the pandemic struck, the Group of Twenty (G20) suspended debt service for poor countries; the scheme deferred $12.9 billion of payments in 2020–21, but "only one private creditor participated".10

Distress followed. Zambia's debt reached 140 percent of GDP in 2020; Sri Lanka's reached 116 percent in 2022 and Ghana's 93 percent. The G20 had created a Common Framework for restructuring in 2020, and the IMF called for it to be made more predictable.7 Ghana reached agreement with its official creditors in December 2023 and Zambia in June 2024, and Sri Lanka, outside the framework, also in June 2024. The World Bank's 2026 debt report found that the flow of money had turned: developing countries had paid out more in debt service than they received in new loans in 2022–24, but in 2025 they received $45.4 billion more than they paid, as private lenders returned. Their external debt reached a record $9.4 trillion, and more than 60 percent of low-income countries were at high risk of or already in debt distress.11

India: from deficit rules to a debt anchor

India entered the century with high debt and large deficits. The combined fiscal deficit of the centre and the states was 9.8 percent of GDP in 2001-02, and their combined liabilities rose to 84.7 percent of GDP in 2003-04 (chart T4.7). The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management (FRBM) Act of 2003 set out to change this: its rules required the centre to cut its deficit to 3 percent of GDP and eliminate its revenue deficit by March 2009.12 Growth and buoyant revenue did most of the work; by one estimate two-thirds of the adjustment came from revenue. The centre's deficit fell to 2.6 percent of GDP in 2007-08 and the debt ratio fell steadily, though the Comptroller and Auditor General found that oil and fertiliser bonds issued off the budget had flattered the figures.12

The crisis broke the rules. In 2008-09 the budgeted deficit of 2.5 percent of GDP became 6 percent in the revised estimates, and 7.8 percent counting the off-budget bonds.12 The combined deficit rose to 9.5 percent of GDP in 2009-10 (chart T4.8). It came down slowly through the 2010s, and the debt ratio reached its low of 66.6 percent in 2014-15. In 2017 a review committee chaired by N.K. Singh proposed a new framework built around debt: a ceiling of 60 percent of GDP for the centre and states together, 40 percent for the centre and 20 percent for the states, and an independent fiscal council. The Chief Economic Adviser dissented, preferring a target for the primary balance.13 The 60 and 40 percent ceilings, to be reached by 2024-25, were written into the Act in 2018, along with an escape clause allowing the deficit to exceed its target by up to 0.5 percent of GDP in emergencies.14

Then came the pandemic. The centre's deficit reached 9.2 percent of GDP in 2020-21 and the combined deficit 13.1 percent; combined liabilities jumped to 89.2 percent of GDP. The Economic Survey 2020-21, published in January 2021, argued that India need not fear this. Citing Blanchard, it said the interest rate India's government paid had been below the growth rate "by norm, not by exception", because India grew fast; spending to support growth would therefore lower debt ratios, not raise them, and it called this "not a call for fiscal irresponsibility".15 The 2017 committee had warned the other way: interest rates and growth tend to rise and fall together, so faster growth should not be a reason to relax.13

The government chose a middle path. In 2021 it replaced fixed annual targets with a glide path to a deficit below 4.5 percent of GDP by 2025-26, a move it later described as a shift from rigid "fiscal rules" to flexible "fiscal standards".16 The target was met: the centre's deficit was 4.4 percent of GDP in 2025-26 in the revised estimates, and the budget for 2026-27 aims for 4.3 percent.17 The 2018 ceilings were not: the centre's debt was 57.1 percent of GDP in 2024-25, against the 40 percent target.16 The budget of February 2025 therefore made debt itself the anchor: from 2026-27 the deficit will be set each year so that the centre's debt falls to "about 50±1 per cent" of GDP by March 2031, from 56.1 percent in 2025-26.1617 The Sixteenth Finance Commission's recommended path, with the centre's deficit falling to 3.5 percent by 2030-31, would bring the centre's debt to 47.6 percent and combined debt to 73.1 percent of GDP.18

The weak point is the interest bill. India's general government net interest payments have taken 24.5 percent of its revenue on average since 2014, against 6.9 percent in the average emerging and developing economy (chart T4.9). The IMF counts this as a risk, but a contained one: the debt is long-dated, at fixed rates, in rupees and held mostly at home, and it rates the risk of sovereign stress as moderate. It projects general government debt falling to 77 percent of GDP by 2030-31 and has urged that the 50 percent target be made more ambitious, extended to the states and backed by an independent fiscal body; the government has defended its anchor.19 The states are the new pressure point: their combined deficit rose from 2.6 percent of GDP in 2021-22 to 3.2 percent in 2024-25, and cash transfers to households, mostly to women, cost them about ₹1.7 lakh crore (₹1.7 trillion) in 2025-26, and about half of the states paying them run revenue deficits, borrowing to cover day-to-day spending.20 The Survey stresses the progress: capital spending has risen to about 3 percent of GDP, and general government debt, on the IMF's measure, has fallen by about 7 points of GDP since 2020.20

Key terms

General government gross debt
Everything owed by all levels of government, before subtracting financial assets.
Primary balance
The budget balance leaving out interest payments.
Interest rate–growth differential (r – g)
The interest rate on government debt minus nominal GDP growth; when negative, debt ratios tend to fall.
Fiscal multiplier
The change in output caused by a change in government spending or taxes.
Fiscal consolidation
Cutting the budget deficit by raising taxes or cutting spending.
Debt restructuring
A renegotiation in which creditors accept less than they were owed.
Debt anchor
A numerical ceiling or target for the debt ratio that guides annual deficit targets.
FRBM Act
India's Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act of 2003, which sets its fiscal targets.
