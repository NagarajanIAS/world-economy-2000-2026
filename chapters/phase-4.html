Uneven recovery

The world economy bounced back in 2010, but the rich countries recovered slowly and the euro area fell into a second crisis. Governments cut deficits while central banks pushed money into the system, and when the US Federal Reserve hinted in 2013 that it would slow down, the emerging economies felt it first.

January 2010 – June 2014 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: The global financial crisis · Next: Slow growth and slowbalisation

What happened

World output grew by 5.3 percent in 2010, after the recession of 2009. Then growth settled lower, at about 3.4 percent a year in 2012 and 2013. The advanced economies grew by only 1.9 percent a year from 2010 to 2014; the emerging and developing economies by 5.7 percent. In the euro area the crisis moved from banks to governments. Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus needed rescue loans, Greece restructured its debt, and borrowing costs in Italy and Spain rose until the European Central Bank promised in July 2012 to do "whatever it takes". Most rich-country governments cut their deficits from 2010, and in 2012 IMF economists found that forecasters had underestimated how much this would cost in lost output (the fiscal multiplier). Central banks held rates near zero and bought bonds. In 2013 the Fed's talk of slowing its purchases set off the taper tantrum: long-term rates jumped and the currencies of countries with large current account deficits, India among them, fell sharply.

A recovery at two speeds

In April 2010 the IMF projected that world output would rise by about 4¼ percent that year, after a contraction of about ½ percent in 2009, and it called the recovery "multispeed": weak in the advanced economies, strong in the emerging ones.1 The outcome was better than that forecast, but the rebound did not last. By April 2012 the IMF wrote of "a major setback during 2011", and in October 2013 it described global growth as "in low gear".2

The gap between the two groups is clearest in the level of output (chart 4.1). By 2014 US real GDP was 109 percent of its 2007 level, Germany's and Britain's 106 percent, and Japan's 101 percent. Italy's was still 91 percent, and Spain's and Portugal's had fallen to 92 percent at their lowest in 2013. Greece's output in 2013 was 73 percent of its 2007 level, a fall of more than a quarter. Over the same years China's real GDP rose to 181 percent of its 2007 level and India's to 153 percent. China's growth, though, slowed from 10.6 percent in 2010 to 7.5 percent in 2014, and by October 2013 the IMF saw China and many other emerging economies "coming off cyclical peaks".2

The euro crisis

The euro area's second crisis began in Greece. Early in 2010 the Greek government's borrowing costs began to climb towards levels it could not sustain (chart 4.2).3 In May 2010 the other euro-area countries and the IMF agreed a three-year rescue of €110 billion: €80 billion in bilateral loans from euro-area governments and €30 billion from the IMF. The European Stability Mechanism's account of the programme gives €52.9 billion lent through the Greek Loan Facility and €20.1 billion from the IMF.3 The same month the European Central Bank (ECB) began buying the bonds of governments under pressure, through its Securities Markets Programme, and offset the effect on the money supply.4 In June 2010 the euro-area governments created a temporary rescue fund, the European Financial Stability Facility. It lent to Ireland, whose package was approved in November 2010 after the cost of rescuing its banks shut it out of the bond market, and to Portugal, whose package followed in May 2011.5

The IMF had warned in April 2010 that worries about Greece "could turn into a full-blown and contagious sovereign debt crisis".1 That is what happened. The spread between Greek and German ten-year yields, about 2.8 percentage points in January 2010, averaged 27.4 points in February 2012 (chart 4.2). Portugal's peaked at 12.0 points in January 2012 and Ireland's at 9.7 in July 2011. In the second half of 2011 the pressure reached Italy and Spain. Italy's spread peaked at 5.2 points in November 2011, Spain's at 5.6 in July 2012. Investors in German bonds, meanwhile, accepted a yield of 1.24 percent in July 2012.

The core of the problem was a loop between banks and governments, which the IMF later urged Europe to "sever".6 Banks held their own governments' bonds, so doubts about a government weakened its banks; governments stood behind their banks, so bank losses weakened the government. In December 2011 the ECB offered banks unlimited three-year loans (longer-term refinancing operations, or LTROs) at a fixed rate.7 In February 2012 Greece agreed a second programme with the euro-area fund and the IMF. In March it restructured its debt to private creditors: about 97 percent of privately held Greek bonds were exchanged, with a 53.5 percent cut in their face value (the IMF later put the reduction in privately held debt at 60 percent; see the euro area lens).3 In July 2012 Spain obtained loans to recapitalise its banks, and Cyprus followed with a programme in April 2013.8

The turn came from the central bank. On 26 July 2012, speaking in London, ECB President Mario Draghi said that "within our mandate, the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro", and added: "believe me, it will be enough."9 In September the ECB set out Outright Monetary Transactions: it would buy the short-term bonds of a country that had agreed a programme with the rescue funds, with no preset limit.10 Spreads fell. By June 2014 Spain's was 1.5 points and Italy's 1.7. The IMF had argued since 2012 that the euro area needed a banking union and some fiscal integration, "on the principle that more area-wide insurance must come with more area-wide control".11 By April 2014 it was still calling for an independent Single Resolution Mechanism and common backstops to complete the banking union.6

Austerity and the multiplier

The stimulus of 2009 had left large deficits, and in 2010 the consensus turned to cutting them. The IMF said in April 2010 that medium-term consolidation strategies were "urgently needed", and in October that "fiscal adjustment needs to start in 2011".1 Governments did not wait. Between 2010 and 2013 the structural budget balance of the advanced economies improved by 3.8 percent of potential GDP (chart 4.3). Greece tightened by 15.4 points, Portugal by 5.9 and Spain by 5.7. The United States tightened as much as Spain, by 5.7 points, as a payroll tax cut expired, taxes rose, caps on discretionary spending took effect and automatic spending cuts (the "sequester") began in 2013. In May 2013 the Fed chairman, Ben Bernanke, told Congress that federal fiscal policy had become "significantly more restrictive".12 Britain tightened by 3.8 points and Germany by 3.4. Japan barely tightened at all.

Yet public debt kept rising, because output kept disappointing. Greece's general government debt went from 105 percent of GDP in 2007 to 183 percent in 2014 despite the restructuring; Spain's from 36 to 104 percent. The question was how much a cut in the deficit cut output: the fiscal multiplier. If the multiplier is small, consolidation is cheap. If it is large, cutting the deficit shrinks the economy so much that the ratio of debt to GDP may barely fall.

In October 2012 the IMF published a short box by its chief economist, Olivier Blanchard, and Daniel Leigh. For 28 European and other advanced economies, they compared the fiscal tightening planned for 2010–11 in the April 2010 forecasts with how far growth later fell short of those forecasts. Countries that planned more consolidation had larger shortfalls: each 1 percent of GDP of planned tightening went with growth about 1 percentage point below forecast. Forecasters had assumed multipliers of about 0.5; the box concluded that since the global financial crisis the actual multipliers had been "in the 0.9 to 1.7 range". The reasons it gave were spare capacity, monetary policy stuck at the zero lower bound, and many countries tightening at once.13 The IMF's advice shifted with it. In April 2012 it had already said that countries with fiscal room "can reconsider the pace of consolidation". By April 2013 it held that "fiscal adjustment needs to progress gradually".14

The cost showed up in jobs (chart 4.4). Euro-area unemployment rose from 7.6 percent in 2007 to 12.2 percent in 2013. In Spain it reached 26.1 percent and in Greece 27.5 percent, both in 2013. American unemployment, which had peaked at 10.0 percent in October 2009, fell steadily, to 6.1 percent by June 2014. German unemployment fell below its pre-crisis level, to 4.7 percent in 2014.

Money at zero

With fiscal policy tightening, central banks carried the load. The Fed held its policy rate near zero throughout and kept buying bonds. In September 2012 it began a third round of quantitative easing, buying mortgage-backed securities at 40 billion dollars a month and promising to continue until the outlook for the labour market improved "substantially"; it later added Treasury bonds at 45 billion a month.15 Its balance sheet grew from 2.26 trillion dollars in January 2010 to 4.35 trillion in June 2014. Japan renewed fiscal and monetary stimulus under the programme that became known as Abenomics, which the IMF judged risky given Japan's public debt.14 The ECB moved later and in both directions. It raised its deposit rate to 0.75 percent in July 2011, in the middle of the crisis, before reversing. In June 2014 it cut its deposit rate below zero, to −0.10 percent.16

Low rates in the rich countries pushed investors towards higher yields elsewhere. In April 2011 the IMF noted the risk of overheating in many emerging economies; measures to manage capital inflows, it wrote, "can play a role" but are "not a substitute for macroeconomic tightening".17 By October 2013 it judged that capital flows to emerging markets may have been driven "more by low yields in advanced economies" than by their own prospects.18

The taper tantrum

In May and June 2013 the Fed began to talk about slowing its purchases. At his press conference on 19 June, Bernanke said that if the economy developed as expected the Committee would "moderate the monthly pace of purchases later this year", and end them around the middle of 2014.19 Markets reacted strongly. The US ten-year Treasury yield, which had averaged 1.76 percent in April 2013, averaged 2.81 percent in September. The IMF called it "a surprisingly large tightening" of US monetary conditions, and noted that emerging-market local bond yields had jumped by roughly three times what its own scenario of US tightening had implied.18

The hardest hit were economies that relied on foreign money to finance large current account deficits. Between April 2013 and September, its weakest month on average, the Indian rupee lost 15 percent of its value against the dollar (chart 4.5). By December the Brazilian real had also fallen by 15 percent and the South African rand by 12 percent. The IMF warned in October 2013 that some emerging economies "could even face severe balance of payments disruptions", and urged them to let exchange rates adjust while using reserves to smooth "excessive volatility".18 In the event the Fed did not begin to taper in September. In December 2013 it announced that from January it would buy 35 billion dollars of mortgage securities and 40 billion of Treasuries a month, 10 billion less in all.20 The ten-year yield, at 2.90 percent in December, barely moved; the real and the rand weakened further in early 2014, while the rupee had already recovered part of its loss. By April 2014 the IMF reported that the recovery had "broadly strengthened", led by the advanced economies, while growth in many emerging economies had disappointed.6

India: impact and response

India came out of 2008–09 fast. In February 2011 the Economic Survey found that "the turnaround has been fast and strong", and forecast growth of 9 percent (plus or minus a quarter point) for 2011-12. It judged a second global downturn "a very low probability event".21 On today's figures growth was 8.5 percent in 2010-11. Then it fell: to 5.2 percent in 2011-12 and 5.5 percent in 2012-13. On the series used at the time the slump looked worse still: 4.5 and 4.7 percent in 2012-13 and 2013-14. In 2014 the Survey noted that India had last grown below 5 percent two years running "a quarter of a century ago".22 Each February's forecast missed. The 2012 Survey expected 7.6 percent for 2012-13; the 2013 Survey expected 6.1–6.7 percent for 2013-14.2324

The first problem was inflation. Wholesale inflation peaked at 11 percent in April 2010, and the Survey read it as driven by demand, not just by the monsoon.21 Consumer inflation for industrial workers stayed high: 10.4 percent in 2010-11 and 10.3 percent in 2012-13. The Reserve Bank raised its repo rate 13 times between March 2010 and October 2011, by 3.75 percentage points in all, from 4.75 to 8.5 percent.2324 With hindsight, the 2014 Survey judged that the post-crisis stimulus had "proved to be excessive and fostered high inflation".22

The second problem was investment. The 2013 Survey traced the slowdown to the rate rises, weaker exports to the rich countries, and stalled projects held up by land acquisition, coal linkages and environmental clearances. "Lack of growth and slowdown in investment are feeding into each other," it wrote.24 Firms that had borrowed heavily in the boom could not finish their projects or repay, and banks' gross non-performing loans rose from 2.36 percent of credit in March 2011 to 3.90 percent in March 2014.22 By 2015 the Survey called this a "balance sheet syndrome with Indian characteristics".25

The third problem, and the one that made India a casualty of the taper tantrum, was the external balance. Saving fell faster than investment. Households, facing high inflation and weak equity returns, put more of their savings into gold.24 With Brent crude averaging about 111 dollars a barrel in 2011, India's oil import bill rose from 106.0 billion dollars in 2010-11 to 164.0 billion in 2012-13, and gold imports reached 1,078 tonnes in 2011-12.2322 The current account deficit widened to 88.2 billion dollars in 2012-13, or 4.8 percent of GDP on today's GDP figures (chart 4.6). The Survey of the time, on the figures then available, called its 4.7 percent a "historic peak".22 The government raised the import duty on gold in steps, from 2 percent before the 2012-13 Budget to 10 percent during 2013-14.2422 Fiscal policy was a further weakness: the Centre's deficit, 4.8 percent of GDP in 2010-11, slipped to 5.7 percent in 2011-12 against a budget of 4.6, and a new roadmap promised 3 percent by 2016-17.24

So when the Fed signalled in May 2013 that it might slow its purchases, money left India's bond market quickly. The 2014 Survey dates the signal to 22 May, which it ties to the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC); that was the day of Bernanke's testimony to Congress, and the clearer signal came at his press conference of 19 June and notes that countries with large current account deficits saw the biggest outflows.22 The rupee fell to a record low of 68.36 per dollar on 28 August 2013; on monthly averages it lost 15 percent of its dollar value between April and September (chart 4.5). In October the IMF cut its forecast for India's growth in 2013 to 3¾ percent.18 The 2015 Survey later called this "a near-crisis during July/August 2013", and its index of macroeconomic vulnerability ranked India the most vulnerable of the countries it compared in 2012.25

The response came in two stages. In mid-July 2013 the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) raised the rate on its emergency lending window (the marginal standing facility) by 2 percentage points, to 10.25 percent, and capped banks' access to its funds, squeezing the supply of rupees. Raghuram Rajan, who had warned of the risks of the pre-2008 boom as the IMF's chief economist (Phase 2), became RBI Governor on 4 September 2013. From 20 September the squeeze was unwound and replaced by conventional rate rises, taking the repo rate back to 8 percent by January 2014 (it had been cut to 7.25 percent between April 2012 and May 2013, on BIS data).22 The government curbed gold imports, and the RBI opened a special swap window for banks' foreign-currency deposits from non-resident Indians (FCNR(B) deposits) and their overseas borrowing, through which 34 billion dollars was raised.22 Gold imports fell to 664 tonnes in 2013-14, the current account deficit to 32.3 billion dollars (1.7 percent of GDP), and reserves recovered to 304.2 billion dollars by March 2014. When the Fed actually began tapering, the Survey noted, "the rupee became resilient".22 The 2015 Survey drew the lesson that a current account deficit keeps costing money, through payments on the foreign capital that finances it, even when financing is easy.25

Key terms

Sovereign spread
The extra yield a government pays over a benchmark, such as German Bunds.
Fiscal consolidation
Cutting a budget deficit through higher taxes or lower spending.
Structural balance
The budget balance with the effects of the business cycle removed.
Fiscal multiplier
How much output changes for each unit change in government spending or taxes.
Outright Monetary Transactions
The ECB's 2012 offer to buy the bonds of governments in a rescue programme, without a preset limit.
LTRO
A longer-term ECB loan to banks; in 2011–12, for three years.
Taper tantrum
The 2013 jump in yields and fall in emerging-market currencies after the Fed signalled slower bond purchases.
Banking union
Common supervision and resolution of euro-area banks.
