The global financial crisis

How losses on American mortgages of modest total size became a run on the world's financial system, a collapse in trade, and the deepest downturn in the advanced economies since the Second World War.

August 2007 – December 2009 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: The great boom · Next: Uneven recovery

What happened

In August 2007 investors stopped trusting securities built from US subprime mortgages, and banks stopped trusting each other. For a year the strain looked containable: central banks lent freely, and the IMF still expected the world to grow by almost 4 percent in 2009. Then in September 2008 the US government took over Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, Lehman Brothers failed and AIG was rescued, all within ten days. Credit froze, world trade volumes fell by 10.2 percent in 2009, and advanced-economy output shrank by 3.4 percent. Interest rates near zero, a Federal Reserve balance sheet that more than doubled, large budget deficits and public capital for banks stopped the slide by mid-2009. China and India kept growing; Mexico, Russia, Japan and Germany were hit harder than the United States, where the crisis began.

From subprime to the money markets, 2007

In April 2007 the IMF's World Economic Outlook judged that the world economy was still well set for continued robust growth in 2007 and 2008. It expected the US slowdown, then centred on housing, to stay a mostly American affair, because spillovers from a mid-cycle slowdown are usually small.1 Its sister report, the Global Financial Stability Report, noticed that credit quality in the subprime segment was deteriorating. It warned that losses could spread to structured mortgage products held by investors around the world. But it concluded that none of the risks it had identified would, on its own, threaten financial stability.2

That judgement rested on a reading of securitisation that turned out to be wrong in an important way. Spreading mortgage risk across many holders was supposed to make the system safer. In practice, much of the risk had been bought with borrowed short-term money by banks and by bank-sponsored vehicles outside the regulated banking system. When the value of mortgage securities became uncertain, nobody could tell which institutions held the losses. On 9 August 2007 BNP Paribas suspended redemptions from three investment funds because it could not value their assets. The next day the Federal Reserve said it would supply reserves to keep its policy rate near its 5.25 percent target, and a week later it cut its discount rate.3

The TED spread, the premium banks paid to borrow dollars over the yield on Treasury bills, measures the fear that set in. In the first half of 2007 it averaged 0.50 percentage points. It jumped in August and never returned to its earlier calm before 2009 (chart 3.1). In September the UK authorised the Bank of England to support Northern Rock, a mortgage lender that had funded itself in wholesale markets. It was nationalised in February 2008.4

The IMF's October 2007 Outlook cut its 2008 world growth forecast by almost half a percentage point and said the risks were "firmly on the downside". It still described 4¾ percent world growth as solid.5 Central banks treated the problem as one of liquidity. In December 2007 the Fed opened dollar swap lines with the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Swiss National Bank so that European banks, which had borrowed dollars to buy American securities, could get funding.6 That diagnosis was partly right: there was a run on short-term funding. But beneath the run lay real losses, and liquidity alone could not repair them.

Bear Stearns to Lehman: the run becomes a panic, 2008

In March 2008 Bear Stearns, a Wall Street investment bank, ran out of funding and was absorbed by JPMorgan Chase with financing from the New York Fed.7 The IMF's April 2008 Outlook now spoke of "a major financial crisis". It forecast a mild US recession and world growth of 3.7 percent in 2008, and it put the chance of a global recession at one in four. By the IMF's rule at the time, that meant world growth of 3 percent or less. It saw "a broad credit squeeze" but "not yet a full-blown credit crunch".8 Over the summer, oil was still rising. Brent crude peaked at 143.95 dollars a barrel on 3 July 2008, which squeezed household incomes just as credit tightened (Brent data: FRED DCOILBRENTEU).

September 2008 broke the pattern of containment. On the 7th the US government placed Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, the giant housing-finance companies, into conservatorship. On the 15th Lehman Brothers filed for bankruptcy. On the 16th the Fed authorised up to 85 billion dollars of lending to AIG, an insurer that had sold protection on mortgage securities to banks around the world.9 Letting Lehman fail told markets that no institution was safe, and the run spread from investment banks to money-market funds, commercial paper and foreign banks. The TED spread reached its daily peak of 4.58 points on 10 October, and the VIX index of expected stock-market volatility hit 80.9 on 20 November. On 3 October Congress passed the Emergency Economic Stabilization Act, creating the 700 billion dollar Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP).10

The IMF's October 2008 Outlook, finished in the middle of this, described the most dangerous financial shock in mature financial markets since the 1930s. It also made an unusual admission: in hindsight, lax macroeconomic and regulatory policies may have let the world economy run beyond its speed limit.11

The great trade collapse

The financial panic reached the real economy through three channels at once. Banks cut lending to protect their capital. Households and firms, seeing wealth fall and the future darken, stopped buying cars, machinery and houses. And trade finance and global supply chains seized up. The S&P Composite stock index, measured as a monthly average, fell 51 percent from its October 2007 peak to March 2009. Over similar windows the Euro Stoxx 50 fell 55 percent and the Nikkei 225 57 percent (Shiller; ECB; FRED NIKKEI225). US house prices, on the Case-Shiller national index, ended 27 percent below their July 2006 peak when they bottomed in February 2012 (FRED).

Spending on durable goods is concentrated in internationally traded products, so trade fell much faster than output. The IMF's January 2009 update said that global output and trade had "plummeted" in the final months of 2008.12 Its April 2009 Outlook estimated that real GDP in the advanced economies had fallen at an annualised rate of about 7½ percent in the fourth quarter of 2008, and that emerging economies had contracted by about 4 percent.13 On today's data, world trade volumes fell 10.2 percent in 2009, while world GDP fell 0.4 percent (chart 3.2). World GDP per person fell in 2009 for the first time since 1991, which is how this book marks a global recession.

The collapse was not evenly spread (chart 3.3). Output in the advanced economies fell 3.4 percent in 2009. Japan (−5.9 percent) and Germany (−5.5 percent), both big exporters of machinery and cars, fell further than the United States (−2.6 percent). Among emerging economies, Mexico (−6.3 percent), tied to US manufacturing, and Russia (−7.8 percent), hit by the oil price collapse, did worse than any large advanced economy. Brent fell to 33.73 dollars on 26 December 2008. China grew 9.4 percent. India, whose figures run by fiscal year, slowed to 3.1 percent in 2008-09 on today's data but never contracted, and grew 7.9 percent in 2009-10 (see India: impact and response). So the emerging economies as a group still grew 2.6 percent (all IMF World Economic Outlook, October 2026 database).

Was this decoupling? Only in part. Countries that depended on exports of manufactures or commodities, or on foreign bank funding, were dragged down with the advanced economies. The April 2009 Global Financial Stability Report (GFSR) expected net private capital flows to emerging markets to turn negative in 2009, against refinancing needs of about 1.6 trillion dollars.14 Large economies with big domestic markets, limited reliance on foreign funding and room for policy came through best. China's growth owed much to a credit-fuelled investment push, whose consequences belong to later chapters.

The rescue: central banks, treasuries and the G20

Policy changed in two stages. Before Lehman, central banks cut rates and lent against a widening range of collateral. After Lehman, they acted on a scale without precedent in peacetime. The policy rates of the Fed, the ECB and the Bank of England fell from 5.25, 4.00 and 5.75 percent in July 2007 to 0.12 (the effective rate within the Fed's target range), 1.00 and 0.50 percent by June 2009 (Bank for International Settlements policy rates). Once rates approached the zero lower bound, the Fed turned to its balance sheet. In November 2008 it announced purchases of agency debt and mortgage-backed securities. On 16 December it set a target range of 0 to 0.25 percent. In March 2009 it expanded its purchases and added up to 300 billion dollars of Treasury bonds, the start of what came to be called quantitative easing.15 The Fed's assets in December 2008 were 2.6 times their July 2007 level. The ECB's were 1.7 times, mostly through lending to banks, while the Bank of Japan, already near zero rates, barely expanded (chart 3.4).

Governments added two kinds of support. First, they put capital into banks and guaranteed their debts, which the IMF's January 2009 update argued was the precondition for any recovery. It called for loss recognition, triage of weak institutions, capital injections and carving out bad assets.12 Second, they let deficits widen, partly automatically as tax receipts fell and partly through discretionary stimulus. The general government deficit of the advanced economies went from 1.3 percent of GDP in 2007 to 8.8 percent in 2009. The US deficit went from 2.9 to 13.2 percent. Advanced-economy gross public debt rose from 70.1 percent of GDP in 2007 to 101.8 percent in 2012 (IMF WEO). That inheritance shaped the decade that followed.

Coordination mattered too. The IMF's April 2009 Outlook noted some improvement in sentiment since the Group of 20 meeting in early April.13 By October 2009 the IMF judged that strong public intervention had "all but eliminated" fears of a global depression. It now expected world output to have contracted by about 1 percent in 2009 and to grow about 3 percent in 2010.16 The recovery came faster than the October 2009 forecast implied: world growth in 2010 was 5.3 percent on today's data. Unemployment was slower to turn. The US rate peaked at 10.0 percent in October 2009, up from 4.4 percent in May 2007 (FRED). In Spain, where a housing boom had also burst, unemployment more than doubled from 8.2 percent in 2007 to 17.9 percent in 2009 (IMF WEO).

India: impact and response

India's banks held almost none of the securities at the centre of the crisis. In February 2008 the government's Economic Survey judged that India has remained relatively insulated from this crisis, and credited the country's gradual approach to financial reform. Its bigger worry was the opposite problem: too much foreign money. Net capital inflows had risen to about 9 percent of GDP in 2007-08, foreign exchange reserves reached 309.7 billion dollars by end-March 2008, and the rupee had strengthened to 39.3 per dollar in January 2008, squeezing exporters.1718

The shock arrived through the capital account, not the banks. The 2008-09 Survey later described how the first effect of the subprime crisis was positive: foreign institutional investors poured money in between September 2007 and January 2008, feeding the decoupling debate. Then they sold Indian shares to raise cash at home.18 Net foreign portfolio investment swung from an inflow of 27.4 billion dollars in 2007-08 to an outflow of 14.0 billion in 2008-09. Indian share prices fell 55 percent from December 2007 to March 2009, and the rupee weakened to 51.1 per dollar, so that a dollar cost 30 percent more rupees (chart 3.5). The Reserve Bank sold dollars to slow the fall, and reserves ended March 2009 at 252.0 billion dollars. Indian companies that could no longer borrow abroad turned to domestic banks just as liquidity tightened. After September 2008 exports fell too, and merchandise exports dropped from 185.3 billion dollars in 2008-09 to 178.8 billion in 2009-10.1819

Policy turned abruptly. Until the late summer of 2008 the Reserve Bank of India was fighting inflation, driven by oil and food prices: wholesale price inflation peaked at 12.8 percent in August 2008, and the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) raised its repo rate to 9.00 percent. After Lehman it reversed course. It cut the repo rate to 5 percent by March 2009, cut banks' cash reserve ratio from 9 to 5 percent, and added credit lines for exporters, housing, small firms and infrastructure.18 By April 2009 the repo rate was 4.75 percent (BIS policy rates). On top of spending already committed (the Sixth Pay Commission's arrears and a farm-loan waiver), the government announced three stimulus packages between December 2008 and February 2009: cuts in excise and service tax, more plan spending, and help for particular sectors. The 2008-09 Survey put the total fiscal stimulus at about 3.5 percent of GDP.18 On today's figures, the Centre's fiscal deficit rose from 2.6 percent of GDP in 2007-08 to 6.1 percent in 2008-09 and 6.6 percent in 2009-10. Adding the states, the combined deficit went from 4.1 to 9.5 percent.19

How badly was India hit? Less than most, but how much less depends on which GDP series you use. In February 2009 the statistics office's advance estimate for 2008-09 growth was 7.1 percent. The series in use until 2014 put it at 6.7 percent. The current series uses 2011-12 as its base year, with new methods carried back to earlier years (a back series). It puts 2008-09 growth at 3.1 percent, which turns the year from a pause into a sharp downturn. The IMF now uses this back series.19 On any series, India never contracted, and growth recovered to 7.9 percent in 2009-10 and 8.5 percent in 2010-11.

The recovery had a cost. In 2010 the Survey judged that the stimulus had worked because it put money in people's hands through tax cuts and rural employment spending.20 Three years later the Survey's view was harsher. The stimulus had been large, and together with supply bottlenecks it fed the high inflation that forced the RBI to tighten. Falling saving then widened the current account deficit.21 That story belongs to Phase 4 and the 2013 "taper tantrum". For India, 2008 was a sudden stop in foreign capital and a collapse in confidence, not a banking crisis. The lessons it drew were about capital flows, reserves and fiscal room more than about bank regulation.

Why did it happen? Competing explanations

Contested question

Nearly everyone agrees on the proximate cause: a US housing and credit boom ended in losses on mortgage-related securities held, with heavy leverage, by institutions that funded themselves short-term. They disagree about why the boom happened and what turned losses into a systemic crisis.

A global saving glut

In 2005 Ben Bernanke argued that a surge of saving in emerging and oil-exporting economies, much of it invested in safe US assets, explained both the US current account deficit and low long-term interest rates. He saw housing as one channel through which low rates worked.23 In 2006 the US deficit was 5.8 percent of GDP; China's surplus reached 9.8 percent in 2007 (IMF WEO). The FCIC dissent by Hennessey, Holtz-Eakin and Thomas called these global capital flows, together with a worldwide repricing of risk, essential causes of the credit bubble.24

Monetary policy too loose

John Taylor argued that the Fed held rates well below what his rule implied in the years before the boom. He showed estimates suggesting that rule-based policy would have produced a much smaller housing boom and bust, and that housing booms in Europe were largest where policy deviated most.25 Bernanke and Greenspan disputed this. Their points: rule-of-thumb comparisons depend on how the rule is built, and short-term policy rates explain little about why long-term mortgage rates were also low.24

Failures of regulation and of firms

The majority of the US Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission concluded that the crisis was avoidable. They blamed widespread failures of regulation and supervision, of governance and risk management at major firms, excessive borrowing, collapsing mortgage standards, over-the-counter derivatives and credit rating agencies. They judged that Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac contributed but were not a primary cause.26

Government housing policy

In a separate dissent, Peter Wallison argued that US housing policy, especially the affordable-housing requirements imposed on Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, was the indispensable cause. In his account it lowered lending standards and created millions of weak mortgages that defaulted when the bubble deflated.27

How the evidence weighs up. The explanations are not exclusive, and the IMF's own October 2008 view combined them: macro conditions created the fuel, and regulatory gaps let it accumulate.11 The saving-glut and loose-money stories explain cheap credit but not why it ended up in leveraged, short-funded holdings of complex securities. Germany and Japan also had low rates and current account surpluses without a comparable domestic banking collapse. The housing-policy account struggles with the fact that booms and busts in Spain, Ireland and the UK happened without US housing mandates. That leaves the structure and supervision of finance as the common factor. Low rates and capital inflows worked through it, without being sufficient on their own.

Key terms

Subprime mortgage
A loan to a borrower with a weak credit record or little documented income.
Securitisation
Pooling loans and selling securities backed by their payments, often in tranches of differing risk.
TED spread
Three-month dollar LIBOR minus the three-month Treasury bill rate: a gauge of fear about bank solvency.
Liquidity versus solvency
Short of cash versus short of value. Panics blur the two; the cure for each differs.
Swap lines
Central banks lending each other their currencies, so that dollars reach banks abroad.
Quantitative easing
Central bank purchases of bonds with new reserves to lower long-term rates once policy rates reach zero.
Repo rate
The rate at which the Reserve Bank of India lends overnight to banks against government securities: its main policy rate.
Global recession
In this book, a year in which world real GDP per person falls (1991, 2009 and 2020 in the WDI data).
