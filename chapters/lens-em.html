Part III · Country lens 6 of 7

Other emerging markets

Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Türkiye, South Africa, Russia and the Gulf states entered the century as the great hopes of the developing world. They shared a commodity boom, a crisis in 2009 and a sell-off in 2013. After that their paths split: Indonesia and Türkiye kept catching up with the rich world, Brazil stood still, Mexico and South Africa fell back, and Russia and the Gulf rose and fell with the price of oil and with war.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Theme 1: Growth and convergence, Theme 3: Money, Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy and Theme 8: Energy, commodities and climate

In brief

Together the seven economies of this lens (Brazil, Mexico, Indonesia, Türkiye, South Africa, Russia and Saudi Arabia) produced 13.8 percent of world output at purchasing-power parity in 2000 and 13.4 percent in 2025, while India's share rose from 3.9 to 8.2 percent.1 Over those years Türkiye's income per person rose from 27 to 48 percent of the US level and Indonesia's from 11 to 20 percent; Brazil's ended where it began, and Mexico's, South Africa's and Saudi Arabia's fell. Brazil, Mexico and South Africa ended the period far more indebted than they began it, and Türkiye's inflation reached 72.3 percent in 2022.

The BRICs and the boom, 2000–2008

The idea of a new class of economies came from a bank. In November 2001 Jim O'Neill of Goldman Sachs calculated that Brazil, Russia, India and China, which he called the BRICs, produced 23.3 percent of world output at purchasing-power parity, and argued that the Group of Seven (G7) should make room for them.2 Brazil's election year of 2002 brought market pressure, a falling currency and a US$30.4 billion IMF loan;3 its central bank raised the policy rate to 26.5 percent in February 2003.4 Türkiye, whose economy shrank 5.5 percent in 2001, borrowed US$16 billion from the IMF in 2002, with a target of 35 percent inflation for that year.5

Then the commodity boom (see Phase 2) lifted almost all of them. Growth averaged 7.2 percent a year in Russia from 2000 to 2007, 5.6 percent in Türkiye, 5.1 percent in Indonesia, 4.3 percent in South Africa and 3.6 percent in Brazil, but only 2.3 percent in Mexico (chart EM.1). Türkiye brought inflation down from 55.0 percent in 2000 to 8.6 percent in 2004.

The oil exporters' finances improved fastest. Saudi Arabia's current-account surplus reached 28.1 percent of GDP in 2005 (chart EM.3), and its government, which owed 86.7 percent of GDP in 2000, had cut its debt to 2.1 percent of GDP by 2013 (chart EM.6). Russia's debt fell from 55.9 percent of GDP in 2000 to 8.0 percent in 2007. Others saved too: Indonesia's debt fell from 87.4 percent of GDP in 2000 to 23.0 percent in 2012, and Türkiye's from 75.6 percent in 2001 to 29.5 percent in 2013. In 2007 the IMF found Indonesia much less vulnerable than at the time of the Asian crisis ten years earlier.6

Crisis, tantrum and the oil collapse, 2008–2016

The global crisis showed how far the boom had depended on the world outside. In January 2009 the IMF expected Mexico's economy to shrink by 0.3 percent that year; by April it expected a fall of about 3¾ percent, and gave Mexico a new kind of precautionary loan, a Flexible Credit Line, worth about US$47 billion.78 Output fell 6.3 percent. Russia, hit by what the IMF called "the dual shocks" of falling oil prices and capital outflows, saw its reserves fall from US$598 billion in August 2008 to US$375 billion in March 2009, and its economy shrank 7.8 percent.9 Of the seven, only Indonesia kept growing, by 4.7 percent. The recovery was quick: Brazil grew 7.5 percent in 2010.

The next test came from the Federal Reserve. When it hinted in May 2013 that it would slow its bond purchases, the taper tantrum (see Phase 4) fell hardest on five economies that the World Bank later listed as the Fragile Five: Brazil, India, Indonesia, Türkiye and South Africa.10 The IMF judged that Indonesia had been "one of the most seriously affected"; its rupiah lost 17 percent against the dollar in 2013.11 Brazil, India and Türkiye raised interest rates.12

Then the price of oil halved. For Russia it came on top of the sanctions imposed after its actions in Crimea in 2014. The Bank of Russia let the rouble float and on 16 December 2014 raised its key rate from 10.5 to 17 percent;1314 inflation reached 15.5 percent in 2015 and the economy shrank 2.0 percent. Saudi Arabia, whose oil provided about 87 percent of government revenue in 2014 on the IMF's figures, needed an oil price of US$106 a barrel to balance its budget that year (its fiscal break-even oil price), up from US$69 in 2010.15 Its budget deficit reached 14.9 percent of GDP in 2015, on the IMF's later figures, and its current account, a surplus of 26.9 percent of GDP in 2008, turned to a deficit of 7.9 percent in 2015.

Brazil had the deepest fall. A slowdown from 2011 turned into what the IMF called "its deepest recession in decades", worsened by a corruption investigation centred on the oil company Petrobras and by the impeachment of President Dilma Rousseff in August 2016.16 Output shrank 3.5 percent in 2015 and 3.3 percent in 2016, inflation reached 9.0 percent, and the central bank held its rate at 14.25 percent from July 2015 (chart EM.5). The government planned to hold federal spending flat in real terms, but public debt rose from 59.6 percent of GDP in 2013 to 93.3 percent in 2025, and the IMF projects 105.3 percent by 2031.

Seven paths, 2016–2026

After 2016 the seven stopped moving together. Measured against the United States, income per person rose in Türkiye and Indonesia and fell in Mexico, South Africa and Saudi Arabia (chart EM.2).

Türkiye grew fastest but at the price of high inflation. In 2018 the IMF saw clear signs of overheating after a stimulus-driven rebound from the slump that followed the coup attempt of 2016.17 Between January and September 2018 the lira lost 37.2 percent of its value against a basket of currencies, and the central bank raised its rate to 24 percent.18 In 2021, after the government replaced the central bank's governor, the bank cut rates from 19 percent while inflation was rising, calling the price pressures transitory.19 Inflation averaged 72.3 percent in 2022 (chart EM.4). From June 2023 a new team raised the rate from 8.5 percent to 15 percent and, by March 2024, to 50 percent; the IMF called it "a bold policy shift".2021 Inflation was still 34.9 percent in 2025, and the rate was 36 percent in October 2026.22

South Africa stagnated. Its growth averaged 1.7 percent a year from 2010 to 2019, income per person fell, and in 2018 the IMF wrote that the economy "has decoupled from the global growth recovery".23 A new government that year promised to fight "state capture", the use of public resources by politically connected people, and the state power company Eskom needed emergency financing. Power cuts, known as load-shedding, came on almost 160 days in 2022.24 Public debt rose from 24.0 percent of GDP in 2008 to 78.4 percent in 2025. By 2026 the cuts had been suspended since May 2025, the Treasury expected debt to stabilise, and the government had adopted a lower inflation target of 3 percent.25

Mexico tied itself more closely to the United States through the United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement of July 2020, which the IMF thought would help Mexico gain from moving supply chains back to North America, though it worried about the partial reversal of the 2013 energy reform.26 The 2025 tariffs mostly spared it: by July 2025 the effective US tariff on Mexican goods was about 5 percent, because most exports qualified for the agreement's preferences.27 Growth was only 0.5 percent in 2025.

Indonesia was the steadiest, growing about 5 percent a year apart from 2020 and 2021. It suspended its legal ceiling on the budget deficit, 3 percent of GDP, during the pandemic and met it again a year early, in 2022.2829 It banned exports of nickel ore from 2020 to force processing at home; nickel exports rose from US$4.5 billion in 2019 to US$19.6 billion in 2022, though the IMF urged it not to extend such restrictions.29 By July 2025 it was a member of the BRICS group.30

Russia and the Gulf lived by oil and war. After Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, the Bank of Russia raised its rate from 9.5 to 20 percent;13 the IMF expected output to fall 8.5 percent, but it fell only 1.4 percent, and growth reached 4.9 percent in 2024 before slowing to 1.0 percent in 2025.31 The IMF has published no review of Russia since February 2021.32 Saudi Arabia launched Vision 2030 in 2016 to diversify away from oil, aiming to raise non-oil revenue from SAR 163 billion to SAR 1 trillion,33 and planned a 5 percent value-added tax from the start of 2018.34 The war of 2026 (see Phase 9) closed the Strait of Hormuz to much of the Gulf's oil: the IMF now projects that Saudi Arabia's economy will shrink 3.3 percent in 2026 and the United Arab Emirates' 3.9 percent.35

Contested question

Are "emerging markets" still a group?

Yes: a bloc with a voice

O'Neill's idea became a club. The BRICS countries, by then including South Africa, signed the agreement founding the New Development Bank in 2014, with US$100 billion of authorised capital;36 invited Argentina, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates (UAE) to join in 2023;37 and admitted Indonesia by 2025.30 In 2013 markets treated five very different economies as one trade, and they still face the same global forces: the dollar, US interest rates and commodity prices. On today's data the four original BRICs produced 33.6 percent of world output in 2025.

No: the label hides more than it shows

Most of the BRICs' rise was China and India. The seven economies of this lens produced 13.8 percent of world output in 2000 and 13.4 percent in 2025; their combined weight did not grow. Their income levels range from 18 percent of the US level in South Africa to 82 percent in Saudi Arabia, and their inflation in 2025 from 1.9 percent in Indonesia to 34.9 percent in Türkiye. Oil exporters and oil importers gain and lose from the same shock.

How the evidence weighs up. As a financial category the group still works: in 2008 and in 2013 money left these economies together. As an economic group it does not. Over the quarter-century only Türkiye and Indonesia narrowed the gap with the rich world by much, and they did so with very different policies, one with the highest inflation of the seven and the other with some of the lowest. The label says little about any one of them.

India among the emerging markets

India was the "I" in O'Neill's BRICs.2 By 2025 it produced 8.2 percent of world output at purchasing-power parity, more than any of the seven economies in this lens and well over twice as much as the largest of them, Russia, at 3.4 percent. Yet India remains the poorest of the group: its income per person was 13 percent of the US level in 2025, against 18 percent in South Africa.

In 2013 India was one of the Fragile Five. Against a basket of its trading partners' currencies, the rupee fell 14.8 percent between April and September 2013, about as much as the Brazilian real, the Turkish lira and the South African rand (chart EM.7). The World Bank found that India had the second-largest currency fall and loss of reserves in the group.38 The Economic Survey of 2021-22 later celebrated India's transformation "from being among the Fragile Five" to the world's fourth-largest holder of reserves.39 Its 2022-23 successor drew a less flattering lesson: optimism about the BRICS had drawn in foreign money and fed a credit boom that ended in the crisis of 2013.40

India has been an active member of the BRICS club. The agreement of 2014 that founded the New Development Bank gave India its first president and placed the bank's headquarters in Shanghai.36 India's closest economic ties among the emerging markets, however, are with the Gulf. India has received more money from workers abroad than any other country every year since 2006: US$150.7 billion in 2025, 16.3 percent of the world total and more than twice as much as Mexico, the second-largest recipient (chart EM.8). The Gulf states long supplied most of it. When oil prices fell in 2014–16, fewer Indian workers went to Saudi Arabia and the UAE and private transfers to India fell.41 By 2023-24 the pattern had changed: the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI's) survey found that the United States sent 27.7 percent of India's remittances and the UAE 19.2 percent, a shift towards skilled workers in rich countries.42

India also measures itself against the other emerging markets. The Economic Survey of 2023-24 found that from 2021 to 2023 India's inflation strayed on average 1.8 percentage points from its target, less than Mexico's (3.4), Brazil's (3.9) or Russia's (4.8), though more than South Africa's (1.3).43 Their policies reach Indian kitchens too. India imports 60 percent of its edible oil,44 including palm oil from Indonesia, one of its main suppliers, and when Indonesia and Malaysia put taxes on palm-oil exports in 2020, palm-oil prices in India rose.4546

Trade with the Gulf has deepened too. India's agreement with the UAE, signed on 18 February 2022, came into force on 1 May that year, and in July 2022 the RBI allowed trade to be invoiced and settled in rupees.47 The UAE has been among India's three largest trading partners for two decades and in 2023-24 was its second-largest export market.48 In April–November 2022, China, the UAE, the United States, Russia and Saudi Arabia together supplied 40 percent of India's imports.49 That reliance on Gulf oil and Gulf earnings made the war of 2026 a direct risk for India (see Phase 9).

Key terms

BRICS
Brazil, Russia, India, China and South Africa, a group of large emerging economies whose leaders meet at annual summits; it invited six more countries to join in 2023, and Indonesia was a member by 2025. The original "BRICs", without South Africa, was a Goldman Sachs label of 2001.
Fragile Five
Brazil, India, Indonesia, Türkiye and South Africa, the economies whose currencies came under the heaviest pressure in the taper tantrum of 2013.
Fiscal break-even oil price
The oil price at which an oil exporter's government budget would balance.
Flexible Credit Line
An IMF loan, given to Mexico in 2009, that countries with strong policies can draw on without the usual conditions, and that most treat as insurance.
