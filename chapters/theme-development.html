Part II · Theme 6 of 9

Development

In 2000 more than a third of humanity lived in extreme poverty; by 2015 fewer than one in seven did, and by 2024 child deaths had halved. Then progress stalled, years before the pandemic, and the world's poorest people became concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa. This chapter follows poverty, health, schooling, inequality and aid, the World Bank's changing idea of what development is, and India's fast fall in poverty and the long argument over how to measure it.

2000–2026 · about 20 minutes · Read with Theme 1: Growth and convergence, Phase 2: The great boom and Phase 6: Pandemic shock and response

In brief

On the World Bank's current measure, the share of the world's people in extreme poverty fell from 36.2 percent in 2000 to 13.4 percent in 2015, as China, India and other large economies grew fast. Since then the fall has nearly stopped: in 2026 about one person in ten is still extremely poor, and most of them live in Sub-Saharan Africa. Health improved more steadily. Deaths of young children halved, and life expectancy rose by about six years despite the pandemic. Almost all children now start school, but in poorer countries most of them do not learn to read properly. Aid from rich countries nearly tripled in real terms between 2000 and 2023, then fell by almost a quarter in 2025, the biggest drop on record. India's poverty fell steeply on new surveys, but there was no official consumption survey for eleven years, and how to measure Indian poverty and inequality is still disputed.

The great escape, and the stall

In September 2000 the world's leaders met at the United Nations and promised to spare no effort to free people from extreme poverty, "freeing the entire human race from want". Among the targets they set for 2015 were to halve the share of people living on less than a dollar a day, to get every child through primary school, and to cut deaths of children under five by two-thirds and maternal deaths by three-quarters.1 These became the Millennium Development Goals. The World Bank's World Development Report for 2000/01 counted 1.2 billion people living on less than $1 a day, and calculated that halving poverty would need a faster decline than the 1990s had managed.2

The 2000s did far better than that. Measured against today's international poverty line of $3.00 a day, the share of the world's population in extreme poverty fell from 36.2 percent in 2000 to 21.0 percent in 2010 and 13.4 percent in 2015 (chart T6.1). The number of extremely poor people fell by about 1,233 million in fifteen years. Most of the fall came in East Asia, where the rate fell from 46.6 percent in 2000 to 6.1 percent in 2015, and later in South Asia. The engine was growth in large economies: the Bank's 2016 report credited the convergence of incomes "spurred by rising incomes in populous countries such as China and India" with the first narrowing of global inequality since the industrial revolution.3

Then the fall slowed. The Bank reckoned that extreme poverty had fallen by about one percentage point a year from 1990 to 2015, but by only about half a point a year in 2015–17.4 The pandemic made things worse. In 2020 the number of extremely poor people rose by more than 70 million, "the largest one-year increase since global poverty monitoring began", and the incomes of the world's typical person fell for the first time since 1990.5 In the World Bank's data the rate rose from 11.1 percent in 2018 to 11.4 percent in 2020, and was 10.1 percent in 2024.

The verdict in the Bank's October 2026 report is blunt. About 811 million people, one in ten, live below $3.00 a day in 2026, "a rate unchanged since 2019"; "The slowdown predates the pandemic". Had the pace of 2000–13 continued, 243 million more people would have escaped. The target of cutting extreme poverty to 3 percent by 2030, which the Bank adopted in 2013, is out of reach, and the report warns that the 2020s may become the first decade in more than a generation without real progress.6 The UN's estimate for 2026 is slightly higher, at about 826 million.7

How the poor are counted

"Extreme poverty" is a line drawn by the World Bank, based on the national poverty lines of the poorest countries and converted into dollars of equal buying power using purchasing-power parity exchange rates. When new price surveys arrive, the line moves. It was $1 a day in 2000,2 $1.90 by 20163 and $2.15 by 2022;5 in June 2025 it became $3.00 a day in 2021 prices. The revision was not just a matter of prices: the poorest countries' own poverty lines had risen, reflecting what the Bank called "better information on the cost of basic needs". The new line raised the count for 2022 from 713 million to 838 million, and Sub-Saharan Africa's from 448 to 559 million.8 Counts from different editions should therefore never be compared, and this chapter uses the current line throughout. For middle-income countries the Bank also publishes higher lines, now $4.20 and $8.30 a day. Measured against $8.30, 46.2 percent of the world's people were still poor in 2024, against 70.6 percent in 2000.

The two main reports disagree on whether progress resumed after the pandemic. In 2024, on the old line, the Bank reported extreme poverty at 8.5 percent, for the first time below its 2019 level, though it still called the 2020s "a lost decade".9 Its 2026 report, on the new line, says the rate has not moved since 2019.6

Poverty moves to Africa

As Asia's poor escaped, Africa's numbers grew. The share of people in extreme poverty in Sub-Saharan Africa fell from 62.4 percent in 2000 to 45.4 percent in 2015, but its population grew so fast that the number of poor people rose, from about 425 million to 580 million in 2024. Outside the region, the number fell from about 1,806 million to 242 million. So Africa's share of the world's extremely poor people rose from 19 percent in 2000 to 47 percent in 2015 and 71 percent in 2024 (chart T6.2). In the Middle East and North Africa, where conflict in Syria and Yemen pushed poverty up after 2015, the rate has risen since 2015, from 11.3 to 14.3 percent.4 The Bank expects 87 percent of the extremely poor to live in Sub-Saharan Africa, in countries affected by conflict, or both, by 2030. Most of those who have left extreme poverty since 2000 have not gone far: they live between $3.00 and $8.30 a day, "one shock away from falling back".6

From Millennium Goals to Sustainable Goals

The Millennium Goals expired in 2015 and were followed by the Sustainable Development Goals for 2030, with 169 targets.10 [unverified: the UN's final 2015 report on the Millennium Goals could not be fetched, so this chapter does not score them goal by goal.] The UN's progress reports are sobering. In 2025 only 35 percent of the targets were on track or making moderate progress, and 18 percent had gone backwards since 2015; "We face a global development emergency", the Secretary-General wrote.10 The 2026 report found extreme poverty "just 3 percentage points below its 2015 level". It also listed real gains since 2015: nearly a billion more people with safe drinking water, internet use up from 40 to 74 percent of the world, and social protection covering more than half the world's people for the first time.7

Health: the clearest success

Health is where progress has been steadiest. The world's under-five mortality rate fell from 76.7 deaths per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 37.4 in 2024, a fall of 51 percent (chart T6.3). The number of young children who died each year fell from 10.1 million to 4.9 million; the World Health Organization calls child survival "one of the success stories of global public health".11 The fall was fastest in South Asia (70 percent) and East Asia (67 percent). Sub-Saharan Africa halved its rate too, from 150 to 71, but it is still almost twice the world average. Maternal deaths fell from 328 per 100,000 live births in 2000 to 197 in 2023, still nearly three times the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target.11

Life expectancy at birth rose from 67.7 years in 2000 to 72.9 in 2019 (chart T6.4). The pandemic cut it to 71.2 in 2021; the World Health Organization (WHO) estimates 22.1 million excess deaths in 2020–23, about three times the deaths reported, and says COVID-19 "erased nearly a decade of progress" before life expectancy recovered by 2022–23.11 In Sub-Saharan Africa it rose from 51.6 to 62.8 years. The WHO warns, though, that progress slowed after 2015 (Phase 6).

Schooling without learning

Getting children into school was a Millennium Goal, and largely achieved: the world's primary completion rate rose from 82 percent in 2000 to 88 percent in 2024, and Sub-Saharan Africa's from 53 to 69 percent. Whether children learned was another matter. "Schooling is not the same as learning", the World Bank's 2018 World Development Report declared, the first ever devoted to education; in Kenya, Tanzania and Uganda three-quarters of third-graders could not understand a simple sentence.12 The Bank and the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) then began to measure learning poverty, the share of ten-year-olds who cannot read and understand a simple text. In low- and middle-income countries it was 57 percent in 2019, and 86 percent in Sub-Saharan Africa. School closures in the pandemic, which lasted 273 days on average in South Asia, may have pushed it to about 70 percent in 2022, wiping out all the gains since 2000; that figure is a simulation, not a measurement.13

Inequality: between countries and within them

Inequality between countries fell as poorer countries grew faster than rich ones (Theme 1). Inequality within countries is harder to judge, and the two main sources disagree. The World Bank, using household surveys, counted 70 economies with a high Gini index (above 40) in 2000 and 42 in 2024.6 Brazil is a striking case: its Gini fell from 58.4 in 2001 to 50.3 in 2024 (chart T6.5). South Africa's remains very high, at 54.1 in 2022, while the United States' edged up from 40.1 to 41.8. The World Inequality Lab, which combines tax records with national accounts, reaches a darker conclusion. In its 2022 report, the gap between the richest tenth and the poorest half within countries "almost doubled, from 8.5x to 15x" since 1980.14 By its 2026 estimates the richest tenth of the world's people take 53 percent of global income and the poorer half 8 percent.15 Part of the difference is method: surveys tend to miss the richest households, as the World Bank itself acknowledges.16 In 2006 the Bank had argued that greater equity was "doubly good" for the poor, because it helps both growth and the poor's share of it.17

Aid: up for two decades, then a record fall

Official development assistance from the rich countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development's (OECD's) Development Assistance Committee rose from $83 billion in 2000 to a peak of $232 billion in 2023, in 2024 prices, about 2.8 times as much (chart T6.6). As a share of the donors' national income, aid rose from 0.22 percent to 0.38 percent. Then it fell, by 23 percent in 2025 alone, to $163 billion. The UN called this "the largest annual decline ever recorded", returning aid to about its 2015 level.7 The World Inequality Lab notes that rich countries' net income from investments in poorer ones is about three times what they give in aid.15

What the World Bank thought development was

The titles of the World Bank's annual World Development Report trace how ideas moved. The 2000/01 report, Attacking Poverty, drew on interviews with more than 60,000 poor people and treated poverty as more than low income, and proposed three ways of attacking it: opportunity, empowerment and security.2 Later reports turned to equity (2006), jobs (2013), learning (2018) and data (2021). The 2024 report, The Middle-Income Trap, was about how middle-income countries could become rich, and said that the sense that three decades had been great for development came from "abysmally low expectations".18 The 2026 report, on artificial intelligence, opens by noting that "development progress has dipped to a 75-year low".19 In 2024 the Bank also renamed its poverty report Poverty, Prosperity, and Planet, adding the planet to its title.9

India: the fastest fall, and the fight over measuring it

By the World Bank's account India achieved "one of the fastest poverty declines in the world". Its GDP per head, adjusted for prices, rose 3.1 times between 2000 and 2024. On the $3.00 line the share of Indians in extreme poverty fell from 27.1 percent in 2011-12 to 5.3 percent in 2022-23 and 2.6 percent in 2023-24, from about 342 million people to 37 million (chart T6.7). Below $4.20 a day, the line the Bank uses for lower-middle-income countries, the share fell from 57.7 to 16.1 percent, "lifting an estimated 496 million people out of poverty". But 80.0 percent of Indians still lived on less than $8.30 a day.16

How fast poverty fell, and when, is uncertain, because India went eleven years without an official consumption survey. The last official poverty estimate, 21.9 percent on the national Tendulkar line, was for 2011-12.16 In the meantime the World Bank, noting that India "has not published official survey data on poverty since 2011", estimated Indian poverty from a private household survey run by the Centre for Monitoring Indian Economy.5 When the Household Consumption Expenditure Survey for 2022-23 arrived, with a new questionnaire and a different way of recording spending, the Bank rebuilt its Indian series and dropped the interim estimates as not comparable.8 It says part of the further fall in 2023-24 reflects "better measurement" as well as real gains.16

The government's own headline measure is not money at all. NITI Aayog's multidimensional poverty index, which counts deprivations in health, schooling and living standards, fell from 55.3 percent in 2005-06 to 24.9 percent in 2015-16 and 15.0 percent in 2019-21, in the years with national health surveys. Its widely quoted claim that 24.82 crore (248 million) people escaped poverty compares two years, 2013-14 and 2022-23, for which the figures were estimated by extending trends, not measured.21 The Economic Survey 2025-26 cites the World Bank, NITI Aayog and academic estimates, one of which puts poverty on the Tendulkar line at 2.3 percent in 2023-24, but no official monetary poverty estimate of the government's own.22

Inequality is even more contested. The Bank's Gini index for India, based on consumption surveys, fell to 23.0 in 2023-24; its earlier briefs, based on the private survey, had put it at about 33 to 36. The Bank warns that the surveys "are likely to understate inequality" because they miss richer households.16 The World Inequality Lab, using income and tax data, has called India "among the most unequal countries in the world".14

Health gains are less disputed. India's under-five mortality rate fell from 92 per 1,000 live births in 2000 to 27 in 2024, a fall of 71 percent, taking it below the world average (chart T6.8). Maternal deaths fell from 362 to 80 per 100,000 births. But the pandemic hit hard: World Bank data show life expectancy falling by 3.5 years between 2019 and 2021, twice the world's fall, before recovering to 72.2 years. Over a third of young children (35.5 percent in 2020) were still stunted.23

Schooling shows the world's pattern sharply. More than 95 percent of rural children aged 6 to 14 have been enrolled in school for nearly twenty years,24 but the 2018 World Development Report noted that in rural India nearly three-quarters of third-graders could not do a two-digit subtraction.12 The Annual Status of Education Report (ASER) survey of 2024 found a recovery after the pandemic: 23.4 percent of third-graders in government schools could read a second-grade text, the highest since the survey began, yet only a third of all third-graders could do a subtraction with borrowing.24 The government's PARAKH survey also showed a "promising recovery post-COVID".23

Key terms

International poverty line
The World Bank's measure of extreme poverty, $3.00 a day at 2021 prices since June 2025.
Gini index
A measure of inequality from 0 (everyone equal) to 100 (one person has everything).
Millennium Development Goals
Targets for 2015 drawn from the UN's Millennium Declaration of 2000.
Sustainable Development Goals
The UN's goals for 2030, with 169 targets, which followed the Millennium Goals.
Learning poverty
The share of ten-year-olds who cannot read and understand a simple text.
Multidimensional poverty
Poverty measured by deprivations in health, education and living standards.
Official development assistance
Grants and cheap loans from governments to promote development in poorer countries.
