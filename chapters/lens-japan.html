Japan

Japan entered the century as the rich world's warning: a country that had let a bubble burst into years of falling prices, weak growth and rising public debt. It spent the next two decades fighting deflation with one new central-bank tool after another. In the 2020s inflation finally returned, though less because of those tools than because of a weak yen, dear imports and a shrinking supply of workers. Japan also has the oldest population of any large economy.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Phase 5: Slow growth and slowbalisation, Theme 3: Money, Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy and Theme 9: Demography and labour

In brief

Japan's economy grew by an average of 1.5 percent a year from 2000 to 2007, 1.3 percent in the 2010s and 1.4 percent in 2021–25. Consumer prices fell in 9 of the 13 years from 2000 to 2012. The Bank of Japan kept its policy rate at or below 0.5 percent for the whole period until December 2025, ran negative rates from 2016 to 2024 and increased its balance sheet almost fivefold after 2012. Gross public debt peaked at 228.8 percent of GDP in 2020. Inflation returned in 2022, averaging 2.9 percent a year in 2022–25, the yen fell to its weakest since 1986, and after ending negative rates in March 2024 the Bank raised its rate five times, to 1.25 percent. People aged 65 and over make up 30.0 percent of the population. India's dollar GDP is projected to overtake Japan's in 2027; India is the largest borrower from Japan's aid agency, and in 2017-18 Japan was one of India's top three sources of foreign investment.

Deflation and a false dawn

Japan began the century still paying for the bubble of the late 1980s. Consumer prices fell in 9 of the 13 years from 2000 to 2012 (chart JP.1), and unemployment peaked at 5.4 percent in 2002. With its policy rate already near zero (the zero lower bound), the Bank of Japan switched in March 2001 to a target for the reserves banks held with it, a form of quantitative easing, and promised to keep it until core inflation was stably at or above zero.1 The IMF's 2005 review found that "Mild deflation persists", called conquering it the priority, and judged the debt ratio to be on "an unsustainable path".2

By 2006 the mood had turned. Banks had worked through their bad loans, and the IMF wrote that Japan's post-bubble legacies "have essentially been resolved and deflation has ended".3 In March 2006 the Bank judged that the conditions of its commitment were fulfilled and returned to an interest-rate target, and in July it raised the rate to 0.25 percent, its first rise since the zero-rate period, and to 0.5 percent in February 2007 (chart JP.2).14 The government's annual report spoke of overcoming deflation as "now in sight".5 The IMF's 2007 review noted that the Bank had chosen "a very gradual pace of tightening", and that low Japanese rates had encouraged capital outflows that weakened the yen,6 the yen carry trade.

Two external shocks

The global financial crisis reached Japan through trade rather than through its banks. In July 2008 the IMF thought Japan "appears headed for a modest slowdown" and projected growth of 1.5 percent and export growth of 4.6 percent in 2009.6 Instead exports fell by almost a quarter7 and output by 5.9 percent. The IMF's 2009 review called it "the most severe recession in decades": "The global recession has derailed Japan’s export-led boom."8 Prices fell again, by 1.3 percent in 2009 and 0.7 percent in 2010. The Bank cut its rate to 0.1 percent in December 2008.

Then, on 11 March 2011, the earthquake and tsunami "brought Japan’s nascent recovery to a halt". The IMF put the damage to the capital stock at 3–5 percent of GDP, and power shortages and broken supply chains cut industrial production by 15 percent in March.7 The yen rose, reaching its strongest monthly average, 76.6 to the dollar, in October 2011 (chart JP.4). Public debt, 150.4 percent of GDP in 2007, kept rising, and the IMF urged a higher consumption tax and, in 2012, wrote: "A fiscal adjustment of 10 percent of GDP over the next decade is necessary".79

Abenomics and the two-year target

In 2013 the government's programme, known as Abenomics, promised "three arrows": aggressive monetary easing, flexible fiscal policy and structural reform. In January 2013 the government and the Bank of Japan issued a joint statement in which "the Bank sets the price stability target at 2 percent".10 In April the Bank launched quantitative and qualitative monetary easing: the Bank would double the monetary base and its holdings of government bonds and equity funds in two years, and aim for 2 percent inflation "with a time horizon of about two years".11 The IMF saw "a unique opportunity to end decades-long deflation and sluggish growth", but warned that "all three arrows need to be launched for the policies to succeed".12

The yen fell from 78.1 to the dollar in September 2012 to 123.7 in June 2015, and the Nikkei share index rose 2.3-fold over the same period. Prices rose too, but much of the rise in 2014 came from raising the consumption tax from 5 to 8 percent that April: inflation was 2.7 percent that year on the IMF's figures of the time, but 1.2 percent excluding the tax.1314 The Bank's Board had forecast core inflation of 1.9 percent for fiscal 2015; the outcome was 0.0, and by April 2016 the Bank was forecasting 2 percent "during fiscal 2017".15 In 2016 it went further, adopting a negative interest rate of −0.1 percent in January and, in September, yield curve control: a target of about zero for the ten-year government bond yield.16 The ten-year yield fell to -0.28 percent in August 2019.

The Bank's balance sheet grew from ¥158 trillion at the end of 2012 to a peak of ¥765 trillion in 2024 (chart JP.3): from about 31 percent of a year's output to a peak of about 127 percent in 2020, far larger relative to output than the Federal Reserve's or the European Central Bank's (see Theme 3). Still the IMF's verdicts cooled: Abenomics was "gaining traction" but progress had been "uneven" (2014); it needed "a significant policy upgrade to regain traction" (2016); and it "has not yet achieved a durable exit from deflation" (2017).131417 The second tax rise, to 10 percent, was postponed and took effect in October 2019.1819 Inflation averaged 0.6 percent a year from 2013 to 2021.

Contested question

Did Abenomics work?

Yes, in part

Unemployment fell to 2.4 percent by 2019, more people joined the labour force, and consumer prices rose in most years. The IMF's 2022 review credited Abenomics with helping Japan exit deflation and raising labour-force participation.20 The Bank's own 2024 review concluded that, on balance, "the overall effect on the Japanese economy so far appears to have been positive", with no evidence that it had held back bank lending.21

No: the target was missed

The promise was 2 percent in about two years; inflation averaged 0.6 percent over the next nine. The same Bank review admitted that easing "did not have as large an upward effect on prices as originally expected", partly because expectations proved hard to shift, and that it harmed the functioning of the bond market.21 The IMF said Abenomics "fell short on the deep reforms needed to raise productivity".20

How the evidence weighs up. Abenomics ended falling prices and tightened the labour market, but it did not produce the 2 percent inflation it promised. When inflation did arrive, in 2022, the Bank's own review attributes it mainly to import prices and labour markets made tight partly by ageing.21 Whether easing laid the ground for that, by keeping the economy near full employment, cannot be settled from the data alone.

Inflation returns

In March 2022 the IMF projected inflation of 1.0 percent for that year and 0.8 percent for 2023.20 It was 2.5 and 3.3 percent, and a year later the IMF noted that "Headline inflation has recorded levels not seen in four decades".22 While other central banks raised rates fast in 2022 (see Phase 7), the Bank of Japan held its rate at −0.1 percent, and the yen fell from 103.8 to the dollar in December 2020 to 147.1 in October 2022 (chart JP.4).

On 19 March 2024 the Bank ended negative rates and yield curve control, saying they had "fulfilled their roles", and set the overnight rate at around 0 to 0.1 percent.23 It then raised the rate to 0.25 percent in July 2024, a move that helped unwind yen carry trades in early August, 0.5 percent in January 2025 and 0.75 percent in December 2025.24 The 2026 energy shock (see Phase 9) and a falling yen, which reached 162.3 to the dollar in July 2026, its weakest since 1986, were followed by further rises: to 1 percent in June and 1.25 percent in September 2026, when the Bank cited oil prices and the weak yen among the pressures on producer prices. At the October meeting one member voted for 1.5 percent, arguing that "the situation had shifted to a new phase".25 The ten-year bond yield averaged 3.05 percent in September 2026, the first month above 3 percent since 1996.

Wages, the missing piece for decades, are now moving. The IMF's 2026 review found that "nominal wages are rising at a historic pace", but also "a 6-percent fall in real wages since 2022", and expects inflation to settle at 2 percent in 2027.26 It judged that "Monetary policy accommodation is appropriately being withdrawn".26

Debt and demography

The finance ministry itself says Japan has "the highest level debt-to-GDP ratio in the world" (chart JP.5).27 Gross debt rose from 118.5 percent of GDP in 2000 to 228.8 percent in 2020, against 137.1 percent for Italy and 124.4 percent for the United States in 2025. Debt service takes 25.6 percent of the central government's budget for fiscal 2026, and outstanding government bonds are projected to reach ¥1,145 trillion by the end of that year.27 The ratio has fallen since 2020, to 204.8 percent in 2025, as nominal GDP has grown with inflation; part of the fall reflects a new IMF definition that counts debt at face value after netting out holdings within government, and a revision that raised nominal GDP by about 4 percent.26 The Bank of Japan has begun to shrink its holdings of government bonds, from 91 percent of GDP in June 2024 to about 80 percent in January 2026,26 so more of the debt must find private buyers just as interest rates rise.

Behind slow growth lies demography. Japan's population fell from 127.8 million in 2011 to 123.3 million in 2025 on IMF figures, and the fertility rate was 1.15 births per woman in 2024. The share of people aged 65 and over rose from 17.4 percent in 2000 to 30.0 percent in 2025, the highest of any country with more than 10 million people (chart JP.6), while the working-age share fell from 68.2 to 58.8 percent. The IMF's 2018 review said ageing and shrinking "will dominate economic policy making in coming decades"; its 2019 review cited official projections that the population would shrink by more than a quarter over 40 years.2818 Measured per person, Japan's performance looks better than the headline: real GDP per head rose 22.0 percent from 2000 to 2025, against 40.3 percent in the United States.

India and Japan

For India, Japan is above all a financier. Japan's cumulative official development assistance to India reached ¥7.66 trillion by fiscal 2022, almost all of it in loans, and JICA, Japan's aid agency, says "India has the largest scale of ODA loan projects among all JICA projects".29 In 2022 the two governments set a goal of ¥5 trillion of Japanese public and private investment in India over five years; this chapter could not find a published tally of how much has arrived.29 India also holds Japan's largest bilateral currency swap line, of US$75 billion, which the IMF described in 2020 as by far the largest of Japan's arrangements with Asian central banks.18 Private investors followed: in 2009 Japanese firms ranked India the second most promising country for overseas business after China, and the 2017-18 Survey listed Japan, with Mauritius and Singapore, as India's top three sources of foreign direct investment.3031

Trade has been less of a success for India. The India–Japan Comprehensive Economic Partnership Agreement, in force from August 2011, covers more than 90 percent of trade and was India's first such agreement with a developed country.32 Since then India's imports from Japan have risen from US$6.7 billion in 2009-10 to US$18.9 billion in 2024-25, while its exports rose from US$3.6 billion to US$6.2 billion, so the deficit grew from about US$3.1 billion to about US$12.7 billion.3033 The gap was still widening in 2025-26: in April–November, imports from Japan were 13.4 percent higher than a year earlier and exports to Japan 2.8 percent lower.33 The Economic Survey of 2015-16 found in one test that "the Japan FTA has had a significantly negative effect on exports", and the 2019-20 Survey that the Japan and Korea agreements had hurt India's manufactured exports.3435

The flagship project is the Mumbai–Ahmedabad high-speed railway, using Japanese bullet-train technology. When it was agreed in December 2015, Japan offered loans for 81 percent of the cost, repayable over 50 years at 0.1 percent interest, and the Survey expected "Thirty-five trains per day each way" by 2023.34 The cost has since been revised to ₹1.08 lakh crore, and the line was 47 percent complete in October 2024 and over 55 percent complete in October 2025.36

Indian policymakers have also read Japan as a warning. The 2016-17 Survey noted that India's bad-loan problem had not led to stagnation of the kind seen in Japan after its bubble burst, and the 2020-21 Survey argued for public investment to avoid "a low wage-growth trap, as has happened in Japan during the last two decades".3738 The two economies are moving in opposite directions. Japan's dollar GDP was 10.8 times India's in 2000; on the IMF's projections India's passes it in 2027 (chart JP.7), although much of the change in Japan's dollar figures reflects the yen. Only 7.4 percent of Indians are 65 or older, against 30.0 percent of Japanese. The rupee's value against the yen has swung widely: a hundred yen cost 33.08 rupees in June 2007, 71.10 at the yen's dearest in November 2020 and 60.46 in October 2026 (chart JP.8).

Key terms

Deflation
A sustained fall in the general level of prices, which raises the real burden of debts and can make households and firms put off spending.
Quantitative and qualitative monetary easing
The Bank of Japan's programme from April 2013: very large purchases of government bonds and other assets, aimed at 2 percent inflation in about two years.
Yield curve control
A central-bank target for a long-term interest rate, here the Bank of Japan's 2016–24 target of about zero for the ten-year government bond yield.
Negative interest rates
A policy rate below zero, so that banks pay to keep money with the central bank.
Consumption tax
Japan's value-added tax on goods and services, raised from 5 to 8 percent in 2014 and to 10 percent in 2019.
