The great boom

Four years of the fastest world growth in a generation, led for the first time by the emerging economies, and paid for in part with cheap money, rising house prices and debts that the rest of the world was happy to finance.

January 2003 – July 2007 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: The bust and the turn · Next: The global financial crisis

What happened

After the dot-com bust and the shock of 11 September 2001, the world economy picked up in 2003 and then ran hot. World output grew by an average of 4.9 percent a year from 2003 to 2007, against 3.1 percent from 1990 to 2002. The emerging and developing economies grew by 7.3 percent a year, China by 11.7 percent and India by 7.9 percent. Their demand drove up the prices of oil, metals and food. The US Federal Reserve held its policy rate at 1 percent for a year, house prices rose across much of the rich world, and American households borrowed against them. The United States ran a current account deficit that reached 803 billion dollars in 2006, financed by surpluses in China, Germany, Japan and the oil exporters, many of which piled up foreign exchange reserves. The IMF called it a "purple patch" and warned, year after year, that the imbalances could unwind suddenly. When the crisis came in 2007, it came from somewhere else: the American mortgage market.

The fastest growth in a generation

In April 2003 the IMF expected the world economy to grow by 3.2 percent that year. The war in Iraq had just begun, SARS (severe acute respiratory syndrome) was spreading in Asia and the equity markets had not recovered from the collapse of 2000–02. A year later it put 2003 growth at 3.9 percent, and it described the outlook as "among the rosiest we have seen for a decade".1 That pattern repeated for four years: each April the IMF projected growth for that year, and each following April the outcome turned out higher (chart 2.6 below). By April 2006 Raghuram Rajan, the IMF's chief economist, wrote that the world was in "an extraordinary purple patch", its third year of growth well above trend, with sub-Saharan Africa heading for its best performance in over 30 years.2

What was new was where the growth came from. The advanced economies grew by 2.8 percent a year from 2003 to 2007, about their usual pace; the United States by 3.0 percent, the euro area by 2.2 percent and Japan by 1.7 percent. The emerging and developing economies grew more than twice as fast (chart 2.1). On the IMF's figures China, India and Russia together accounted for two-thirds of the upward revision to world growth in 2005.2 In September 2006 the IMF reported that in both China and India, gains in efficiency (total factor productivity) contributed more to growth than the accumulation of physical or human capital, and that in India's case services as well as manufacturing had led the way.3 Cheaper imports from these economies also helped keep inflation low in the rich countries: the IMF estimated in 2006 that globalisation had cut advanced-economy inflation by about a quarter of a point a year.2

Cheap money and the housing boom

The boom began with a deliberate decision to keep money cheap. Concerned about the risk of an undesirable fall in inflation, the Federal Reserve cut its target for the federal funds rate to 1 percent in June 2003 and held it there until June 2004. It then raised the rate in quarter-point steps to 5.25 percent by June 2006 (BIS policy rates). The rise in short-term rates did little to long-term ones. In February 2005, by which time the Fed had raised its target by 150 basis points (1.5 percentage points), Alan Greenspan told Congress that long-term rates had trended lower, that "glacially increasing globalization" could not explain it, and that the behaviour of world bond markets remained "a conundrum". The ten-year Treasury yield averaged 4.73 percent in June 2004, when the Fed began tightening, and 4.00 percent a year later. Greenspan also noted that American households were saving only about 1 percent of their income, and he added a warning: people who live through long periods of stability "are prone to excess".4

Low long-term rates fed straight into housing, because most mortgages are priced off them. On the Case-Shiller national index, US house prices rose 85 percent from January 2000 to their peak in July 2006 (FRED). Households borrowed against the rising value of their homes, and household debt in the United States climbed from 69.4 percent of GDP at the start of 2000 to 98.4 percent by the end of 2007. America was not alone. In the United Kingdom it went from 63.1 to 94.3 percent, and in Spain from 43.1 to 81.9 percent (BIS; chart 2.2). In Germany household debt fell over the same years.

The IMF saw the risk early, though it framed it as a cyclical danger, not a systemic one. In April 2003 it found that 40 percent of past housing booms had ended in busts, with prices falling by 25–30 percent on average and lost output of about 8 percent of GDP spread over two to three years.5 In September 2004 it noted that house prices moved together across countries, about 40 percent of their movement being explained by global factors such as world interest rates, so a correction in one place was likely to coincide with corrections elsewhere.6 By April 2006 it called the US housing market "a key uncertainty" and warned that, with households saving little, a weaker housing market could trigger an abrupt fall in consumer spending.7 What it did not foresee was that the losses would land on the banks, not just on homeowners and builders.

Global imbalances

The phrase that dominated economic diplomacy in these years was global imbalances. The US current account deficit widened from 445 billion dollars in 2002 to 803 billion in 2006, or 5.8 percent of GDP. On the other side of the ledger, China's surplus rose from 35 billion dollars to 353 billion in 2007 (9.8 percent of its GDP); Germany's from 36 to 226 billion; and Japan's from 109 to 212 billion. Oil exporters such as Saudi Arabia, whose surplus went from 12 to 103 billion dollars between 2002 and 2006, joined them as oil prices climbed (IMF World Economic Outlook; chart 2.3).

Two stories competed to explain these numbers. The first, which the IMF tended to stress, put the cause in the deficit countries. In April 2005 Rajan traced the US deficit to the investment boom of the late 1990s, and argued that when that boom ended the deficit should have shrunk. It did not, because the United States adopted expansionary fiscal policies and the Fed kept money very loose, so house prices kept rising and household saving kept falling.8 The second, set out by Ben Bernanke, then a Fed governor, in March 2005, put the cause abroad. After the crises of the 1990s, he argued, emerging economies had switched from borrowing on world markets to lending, by building reserves and paying down debt. Oil exporters added their windfalls. This global saving glut pushed down world interest rates and found its way into American assets and, through low mortgage rates, into American houses. He saw "no reason why the whole process should not proceed smoothly", though he allowed that a disorderly adjustment was always possible.9

Much of the surplus countries' saving passed through their central banks. China's reserves rose from 298 billion dollars at the end of 2002 to 1,546 billion at the end of 2007; Japan's from 470 to 973 billion; Russia's from 48 to 479 billion; and India's from 72 to 277 billion (World Bank, including gold). In effect, Bernanke observed, governments were channelling their citizens' saving into US Treasury securities.9 As early as September 2003 Kenneth Rogoff, Rajan's predecessor at the IMF, judged that Asian reserve building up to 2001 could be explained by fundamentals, but that the run-up since then was "much harder to rationalize". He urged Asian countries to let their currencies rise, for their own sake and to share the burden of closing the US deficit.10 China took a first step on 21 July 2005, revaluing the renminbi by 2.1 percent and moving to a managed float against a basket of currencies. The currency moved little against the dollar at first, and a bill to put tariffs on Chinese goods unless the renminbi rose won wide support in the US Congress.11

Rajan's view hardened as the imbalances grew. In September 2005 he described a world in which rich countries consumed while emerging markets supplied and financed them as "a temporary and effective response to crises, which now needs to be reversed". The worry was that easy finance had muted the usual warning signals: inflation, interest rates and exchange rates were not "flashing", so when they did change they might change abruptly.12 In the event the imbalances did not unwind through a dollar crash. They shrank in 2009, when the crisis cut American imports (chart 2.3).

Oil, metals and food

Fast growth in large, industrialising economies is resource-hungry growth. The IMF's energy price index rose 2.7-fold between 2002 and 2007, its metals index 3.9-fold and its food index by half (chart 2.4). Brent crude averaged 25.11 dollars a barrel in 2002 and 97.33 dollars in 2008, the peak year of the commodity boom (IMF WEO). In September 2004, as oil jumped on strong demand, thin spare capacity and threats to supply, Rajan drew a lesson: the long-expected effect of the growth of China "and, to a lesser extent, India" on world energy markets had arrived, and "that future is now upon us".13 The rich economies absorbed the shock better than in the 1970s; in the United States, Greenspan noted in 2005, energy had become a much smaller share of business costs than thirty years before.4 For oil-importing developing countries, India among them, it was a heavier burden. Exporters of oil and metals in Africa, Latin America, the Gulf and Russia enjoyed a windfall; in September 2006 the IMF advised the metals exporters not to commit to spending that would be hard to reverse, because supply would catch up and prices were likely to fall.14

Risk spread thin: the new finance

Behind the housing boom stood a transformation of banking. Banks no longer had to hold the loans they made. They could pool mortgages and other loans into securities (securitisation), slice the securities into tranches of differing risk, and sell them to investors around the world, or insure against default using credit default swaps. The official view in the mid-2000s was that this made the system safer. In April 2006 the IMF's Global Financial Stability Report noted a "growing recognition" that spreading credit risk from banks to a wider range of investors had made the banking system and the financial system as a whole "more resilient", with fewer bank failures and steadier lending. It added caveats: these markets had grown fast in benign conditions and had not been tested by a severe downturn, secondary-market liquidity was thin in places, and much of the risk now sat with less-regulated institutions, raising the "potential for unpleasant surprises".15

The IMF had made a similar point about credit booms in April 2004. Rajan's foreword observed that "the roots of every bust lie in the boom that preceded it", and the report found that true credit booms, as distinct from normal credit growth in a fast-growing economy, invariably ended in financial crises with large losses of output.16 In September 2006, after a sharp but short sell-off in May and June, the Global Financial Stability Report (GFSR) judged the turbulence "modest in scale", but it listed two risks that would soon prove decisive: a sharper US slowdown with a rapid weakening of housing, and a lack of liquidity in markets for new and complex instruments such as structured credit.17 In April 2006 Rajan had put the mood in one line: "These are the best of times but they are also the most dangerous of times".18

US house prices peaked in mid-2006 and began to fall. By early 2007 rising defaults on subprime mortgages were unsettling markets, but the IMF's April 2007 outlook still saw the world "well set for continued robust growth".19 The boom's last months are the opening of the next chapter.

India: impact and response

For India these were, by the government's own reckoning, the best years since independence. In February 2008 the Economic Survey called 2003-08 "perhaps, the best ever five year growth performance in the history of independent India", and noted that at the new pace average income would double in a decade.20 Its language had climbed year by year. In 2004 it hoped India was moving to "a sustained growth phase at a higher rate"; in 2005 it saw "a possible ratcheting up of the trend rate of growth"; in 2007 it declared that the economy had "decidedly 'taken off'".21

The boom was driven by investment. On today's figures, fixed investment rose from 28.3 percent of GDP in 2003-04 to 35.8 percent in 2007-08, and domestic saving from 29.6 to 37.8 percent. The Surveys of the time reported a corporate "Capex" boom: private corporate investment rose from 5.4 percent of GDP in 2001-02 to 14.5 percent in 2006-07.20 Bank credit grew from 30.1 to 48.2 percent of GDP between 2003-04 and 2007-08. Merchandise exports tripled, from 52.7 billion dollars in 2002-03 to 163.1 billion in 2007-08, and services, led by software, did better still: in 2005 the Survey saw them "eclipsing the performance of merchandise exports".21 Imports from China rose from 4.1 to 27.1 billion dollars between 2003-04 and 2007-08.

How fast India grew depends on which official series you use (chart 2.5). The Surveys of the time, on the 1999-2000 base, reported growth of 9.4 and 9.6 percent in 2005-06 and 2006-07.20 The later 2004-05 series put the five years from 2003-04 at an average of 8.7 percent, with three successive years above 9. The current series (2011-12 base, with a back series for these years) puts the average at 7.9 percent and 2006-07 at 8.1. On any measure it was India's fastest sustained growth.

India's link to the global imbalances ran through the capital account. Its current account was close to balance, but foreign money poured in: net capital inflows rose from 6.0 billion dollars in 2002-03 to 62.0 billion in 2007-08, when portfolio investment brought in 27.4 billion and foreign direct investment (FDI) 15.9 billion. The 2008 Survey judged that inflows had been "far in excess" of what was needed to finance the current account.20 The Reserve Bank bought the surplus dollars, and reserves rose from 76.1 billion dollars in March 2003 to 309.7 billion in March 2008. Buying dollars creates rupees, so the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) had to sterilise, by selling government bonds. When it began to run out of bonds, the government and the RBI created the Market Stabilisation Scheme in March 2004: the government issued extra bills and bonds and parked the proceeds with the RBI. The scheme's ceiling, ₹60,000 crore (₹600 billion) at the start, had been raised to ₹2.5 lakh crore (₹2.5 trillion) by 2007-08, and the Survey put the fiscal cost of sterilisation in that year at ₹8,200 crore (₹82 billion).22 In 2007 it called these "problems of plenty".21

Even so the rupee rose, from an average of 48.4 per dollar in 2002-03 to 40.3 in 2007-08, and the government announced relief for exporters hurt by it. The Survey described the dilemma as the familiar trilemma: the search for a balance between an open capital account, an independent monetary policy and a flexible exchange rate, which a country cannot have in full all at once.20 Monetary policy tightened. The Reserve Bank first raised its reverse repo rate in October 2004; its policy rate in the BIS series, 6.0 percent in October 2004, had reached 7.75 percent by March 2008, and banks' cash reserve ratio was raised in steps to 7.5 percent by November 2007.20 Fiscal policy tightened too, under the FRBM Act of 2003, which required the central government to eliminate its revenue deficit (borrowing to pay for current rather than capital spending) by 2007-08.23 The Centre's fiscal deficit fell from 5.8 percent of GDP in 2002-03 to 2.6 percent in 2007-08, and the combined deficit of the Centre and states from 9.4 to 4.1 percent. The commodity boom was the main cost: India's oil import bill rose from 17.6 billion dollars in 2002-03 to 79.6 billion in 2007-08.

The Surveys watched the world closely. From 2004 they warned that the US deficits could have a "disorderly resolution" that would upset currency markets and capital flows to emerging economies.22 By 2007 they judged such risks, "for the present", to "be limited". In February 2008, with the subprime crisis under way, the Survey expected only a moderate world slowdown and wrote that "the situation of excess inflows is likely to remain".20 India thus entered the crisis with large reserves and a smaller fiscal deficit. But it had come to depend on foreign portfolio money, and that money was the first thing to leave (Phase 3).

Key terms

Global imbalances
Large, persistent current account deficits in some economies matched by surpluses in others.
Foreign exchange reserves
Foreign assets held by a central bank, used to steady the currency and as insurance against a sudden stop in capital inflows.
Global saving glut
Bernanke's term for the excess of desired saving over investment outside the United States, which he argued pushed down world interest rates.
Securitisation
Pooling loans and selling securities backed by their payments, often in tranches of differing risk.
Credit default swap
Insurance-like contract that pays out if a borrower defaults.
Commodity boom
A sustained, broad rise in the prices of energy, metals and food.
Sterilisation
Offsetting the domestic money created when a central bank buys foreign currency.
FRBM Act
India's 2003 fiscal responsibility law setting deficit targets for the central government.
