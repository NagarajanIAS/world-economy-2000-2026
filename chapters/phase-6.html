Pandemic shock and response

A virus shut down much of the world economy in a few weeks of 2020. Output fell further than in any recession since the 1930s, and governments and central banks answered with support on a scale never seen before. The recovery that followed was fast but uneven, and by the end of 2021 it had run into shortages and rising prices.

January 2020 – December 2021 · about 20 minutes · Previous phase: Slow growth and slowbalisation · Next: Inflation, tightening, war and energy

What happened

In the early months of 2020 the COVID-19 pandemic spread around the world, and from about mid-March governments ordered people to stay at home. Shops, offices, factories and borders closed. In the second quarter of the year output was 9.1 percent below its level at the end of 2019 in the United States, 14.0 percent below in the euro area and 22.2 percent below in Britain. The IMF called it the Great Lockdown. Financial markets panicked in March, money fled emerging markets, and the price of US oil briefly fell below zero. Governments spent and lent on a scale never seen in peacetime, and central banks cut rates and bought bonds in vast quantities: the US Federal Reserve's balance sheet doubled. World output fell by 2.7 percent in 2020 on today's figures, the deepest fall in the IMF's records since 1980, and grew by 6.7 percent in 2021 as economies reopened and vaccines arrived. The rich countries recovered fastest; poorer ones, with less money to spend and slower access to vaccines, lagged. By late 2021 shortages of chips, containers and workers were pushing prices up. India imposed one of the strictest lockdowns, suffered one of the deepest falls in output and a devastating second wave in 2021, and then rebounded.

The Great Lockdown

China, where the virus was first detected, was the first to lock down: the IMF estimated that its output in the first quarter could have been about 8 percent lower than a year earlier.1 By March the virus had reached Europe and North America. Lockdowns were most intense and widespread from about mid-March to mid-May 2020.2 Travel, hotels, restaurants and entertainment stopped almost entirely. "There is a de facto shutdown of a significant portion of the economy," the IMF wrote in April. In the United States more than 6.6 million people claimed unemployment benefits in the fourth week of March, against about 280,000 two weeks earlier.1 The US unemployment rate jumped from 3.5 percent in February to 14.8 percent in April.

Output fell with startling speed (chart 6.1). In the second quarter of 2020 real GDP was 6.9 percent below its level of the last quarter of 2019 in Japan, 10.6 percent in Germany, 11.0 percent in Brazil and 22.2 percent in Britain. The International Labour Organization, quoted by the IMF, estimated that the hours lost in the second quarter were equivalent to more than 300 million full-time jobs.2 Unusually for a recession, consumption and services fell sharply: people could not spend, even when they had the money.

The IMF's April 2020 report, titled The Great Lockdown, projected that world output would shrink by 3 percent in 2020, "much worse than during the 2008–09 financial crisis", and it had cut its forecast by more than 6 percentage points in three months. Its chief economist, Gita Gopinath, wrote that the world would very likely experience "its worst recession since the Great Depression".3 She also explained why this crisis was "like no other": the shock was huge, nobody knew how long it would last, and because the slump was largely the result of measures needed to contain the virus, the usual remedy of stimulating spending was harder to apply and, in the sectors most affected, not even desirable.3

Panic in the markets

Financial markets took fright from late February. Share prices fell sharply, the gap between the interest rates paid by risky and safe borrowers widened, and new issues of high-yield bonds and leveraged loans (debt of riskier, heavily indebted firms) essentially stopped. Investors rushed for cash and safe assets, and short-term dollar funding markets came under strains the IMF found reminiscent of 2008; even the market for US Treasury bonds, normally the deepest in the world, stopped working smoothly.4 For emerging markets it was "the perfect storm". Foreign investors pulled more than 100 billion dollars of portfolio investment out of them after 21 January, the sharpest reversal on record both in dollars and relative to their output, and the dollar rose about 8½ percent in real terms.41

Oil collapsed. The OPEC+ group of producers (the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries and other oil exporters) failed to agree on output cuts on 6 March, just as demand vanished, and the price of crude fell by about 65 percent between mid-January and the end of March.1 Brent, 64 dollars a barrel on average in January, averaged 18 dollars in April. On 20 April, with storage tanks filling up, the price of US crude for delivery the following month fell to −36.98 dollars a barrel: sellers paid buyers to take it away. OPEC+ then cut output by 9.7 million barrels a day for May and June.5

Whatever it takes, everywhere

The policy response was faster and larger than in 2008. Central banks cut rates to zero or close to it, lent freely to banks and markets and bought government and corporate bonds. On 15 March the Fed and other major central banks announced coordinated action on swap lines, the arrangements through which they lend each other dollars, and on 19 March the Fed extended them to nine more central banks.1 Many emerging-market central banks bought bonds for the first time.5 The Fed's balance sheet grew from 4.17 trillion dollars in February 2020 to 8.72 trillion in December 2021; the European Central Bank's (ECB's) from 4.68 trillion euros to 8.51 trillion (chart 6.3). The Fed also moved to a framework of flexible average inflation targeting, under which it would tolerate inflation above its target for a time after a period below it.5

Governments did even more. They spent on health, supported wages and incomes, deferred taxes and lent to and guaranteed loans for firms. The US CARES Act of March 2020 alone contained about 2 trillion dollars, almost 10 percent of GDP, of tax, spending and lending measures.6 By September 2020 the IMF counted fiscal actions worldwide of 11.7 trillion dollars, close to 12 percent of world GDP, half of it extra spending or forgone revenue and half loans, guarantees and capital injections; by March 2021 the total was 16 trillion dollars.78 The European Union agreed a €750 billion recovery fund.5 The general government deficit of the advanced economies widened from 2.9 percent of GDP in 2019 to 10.2 percent in 2020; that of the emerging and developing economies from 4.3 to 8.4 percent (chart 6.2).

The gap in firepower was large. The IMF estimated that the advanced economies had taken discretionary measures worth more than 9 percent of GDP by October 2020, plus another 11 percent in loans, guarantees and other liquidity support; emerging and developing economies managed about 3.5 percent and a little over 2 percent.5 Central banks' bond buying made it easier for rich countries, and some emerging ones, to borrow. In many emerging markets, and especially in low-income countries, the money was simply not available.7 Public debt rose everywhere: in the advanced economies from 101 percent of GDP in 2019 to 119 percent in 2020. Average public debt worldwide reached "an unprecedented 97 percent of GDP in 2020", the IMF reported.8 The support worked, at least in the short run. "Massive policy support has prevented worse outcomes," the IMF concluded in October 2020: there was no repeat of the financial collapse of 2008.5

A recovery split by vaccines

Economies bounced back as soon as they reopened. The IMF's October 2020 report, A Long and Difficult Ascent, found that the world was "climbing out from the depths" faster than expected, led by China, which had reopened in early April and was projected to grow about 10 percent over 2020 and 2021 together. It also warned that the pandemic would reverse decades of progress against poverty, with close to 90 million more people falling into extreme poverty in 2020.5 On today's figures China grew by 2.3 percent in 2020, one of very few large economies to grow at all, while the euro area shrank by 6.0 percent, the United States by 2.1 percent and the emerging and developing economies as a group by 1.8 percent.

As vaccines were rolled out in 2021, rich countries moved fastest. By July 2021 the IMF wrote that "vaccine access has emerged as the principal fault line" in the recovery, dividing almost all advanced economies, which could expect to return to normal later that year, from the rest.9 In October 2021 about 58 percent of people in the advanced economies were fully vaccinated, against about 36 percent in emerging markets and less than 5 percent in low-income countries. The IMF proposed vaccinating at least 40 percent of every country's population by the end of 2021.10 Output followed the same divide. The United States regained its pre-pandemic level of output in the first quarter of 2021, the euro area in the third quarter, Britain only at the end of the year. The world economy grew by 6.7 percent in 2021, the fastest in the IMF's records, which begin in 1980, but sub-Saharan Africa grew by only 3.9 percent.

Scarring

Deep recessions can leave lasting damage: firms that close do not reopen, workers lose skills or leave the workforce, and investment that was postponed is never made. Economists call this scarring. In October 2020 the IMF expected potential output in the ten largest advanced economies to stay about 3.5 percent below its pre-pandemic path over the medium term, and in the ten largest emerging markets about 5.5 percent below.5 A year later it judged that the advanced economies would regain their pre-pandemic path by 2022, largely because of US policy support, while all other groups would remain below it.10

That forecast was broadly right (chart 6.4). Comparing today's figures with the growth the IMF projected in October 2019, before anyone had heard of the virus, world output in 2024 was about 3.2 percent below the pre-pandemic path, and that of the emerging and developing economies about 5.2 percent below. The advanced economies ended slightly above it, though partly because the IMF had expected them to grow slowly anyway. The scars fell mostly on poorer countries.

Shortages and the first signs of inflation

The recovery brought a problem of its own. Spending shifted from services, still restricted, to goods, which households bought with money saved during lockdowns and with government transfers. Supply could not keep up. In October 2021 the IMF described the bottlenecks: microchip production was still short of demand, shipping containers were stranded away from their usual routes, and the blockage of the Suez Canal, restrictions at ports in China's Pearl River Delta and congestion at Los Angeles and Long Beach had lengthened delivery times. Oil prices in 2021 were expected to be close to 60 percent above their low 2020 level; Brent averaged 74 dollars a barrel in December 2021.10 In July 2021 the IMF had judged that rising prices mostly reflected "transitory supply-demand mismatches" and that central banks could generally look through them.9 By October it still expected inflation to subside in 2022, but warned that "inflation risks are skewed to the upside".10 What happened next is the subject of Phase 7.

India: lockdown, collapse and rebound

India announced a 21-day national lockdown on 24 March 2020 and extended it until 31 May. It was among the most stringent anywhere, scoring the maximum on the Oxford stringency index. The Economic Survey of January 2021 defended the decision: facing great uncertainty about the virus, it argued, India had chosen to protect lives first in the expectation that livelihoods could be restored later, and the early lockdown had "transformed the short-term trade-off between lives and livelihoods into a win-win".11 The economic cost was immediate. Output in April–June 2020 was 23.9 percent lower than a year earlier.11 Measured against the end of 2019, and adjusted for the season, India's fall was the deepest of the large economies in chart 6.1: 23.8 percent.

The fiscal response was smaller than in rich countries and tilted towards guarantees and in-kind help. A first relief package in March 2020 was followed by the Atma Nirbhar Bharat ("self-reliant India") packages from May. The Survey put the support from the government and the Reserve Bank together at 29.87 lakh crore rupees (about 30 trillion rupees; a lakh crore is a trillion), 15 percent of GDP, of which the government's share was 9 percent.11 About 80 crore (800 million) people received 5 kilograms of free food grain a month on top of their normal rations, cash went directly to the bank accounts of 42 crore people, and banks lent to small firms under a fully guaranteed credit scheme of up to 3 lakh crore rupees.11 Revenue collapsed with output. The Centre's fiscal deficit rose from 4.6 percent of GDP in 2019-20 to 9.2 percent in 2020-21, and the combined deficit of the Centre and the states to 13.1 percent (chart 6.5). The Survey argued that this was affordable: because India's growth rate has normally exceeded the interest rate on its debt, "growth leads to debt sustainability in the Indian context", and counter-cyclical spending in a crisis was "not a call for fiscal irresponsibility".11

The Reserve Bank cut its repo rate from 5.15 percent to 4.40 percent on 27 March 2020 and to 4 percent on 22 May, lowered banks' cash reserve ratio and allowed borrowers to defer loan repayments from March to August 2020; new insolvency cases were suspended for a year.11 When food prices pushed consumer inflation above the 6 percent upper limit of its target range, to 7.6 percent in October 2020, the Monetary Policy Committee held rates, judging the causes to be "essentially supply shocks that should dissipate over time".11 The same Survey warned against keeping emergency relief for banks too long, recalling that forbearance (letting banks avoid classifying troubled loans as bad) after 2008 had lasted seven years and left a legacy of bad loans: "when an emergency medicine becomes a staple diet, it can be counterproductive".11

The external accounts swung into surplus as imports collapsed. Foreign portfolio investors had pulled out 15.92 billion dollars in March 2020 and the rupee touched 76.86 to the dollar in April, but money then poured back in, drawn by the flood of global liquidity.11 The current account showed a surplus of 0.9 percent of GDP in 2020-21, which the Survey described as the first annual surplus in 17 years, and reserves rose from 478 billion dollars in March 2020 to 607 billion in March 2022.1112

The Survey of January 2021 judged that India had avoided a second wave. It came in April–June 2021, "more severe from a health perspective" than the first, with more than 400,000 new cases a day at its peak in May.12 This time the restrictions were left to the states, and the economic damage was much smaller. The January 2022 Survey again described the response as a "barbell strategy": safety nets for the vulnerable combined with an "agile" approach that adjusted policy as high-frequency data came in.12 Growth in 2020-21 was first estimated at −7.7 percent, the fourth contraction since 1960-61, then revised to −7.3 percent and later to about −6.6 percent; on today's figures it was −5.8 percent, followed by growth of 9.7 percent in 2021-22.111213 The rebound left output well below the path India had been on before the pandemic: in 2024-25 it was about 10.2 percent below the level implied by the IMF's October 2019 projections, a larger gap than for emerging economies as a whole.

Key terms

Lockdown
Government orders closing workplaces, schools and shops and restricting movement to slow the spread of a disease.
Scarring
Lasting damage to an economy's productive capacity from a deep recession.
Swap line
An arrangement under which one central bank lends its currency to another, used in 2008 and 2020 to ease dollar shortages abroad.
Average inflation targeting
A rule under which a central bank aims for inflation to average its target over time, letting it run above target after a period below.
Barbell strategy
The Economic Survey's name for India's pandemic policy: safety nets for the most vulnerable at one end, and flexible policy adjusted to incoming data at the other.
