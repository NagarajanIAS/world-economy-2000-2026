Euro area

The euro was launched in 1999 as a bet that a single currency could bind Europe's economies together. For its first decade the bet seemed to be paying off. Then the 2008 crisis exposed what the good years had hidden, and from 2010 a run on the debts of weaker members threatened to break the currency apart. It survived, thanks above all to its central bank, but at a heavy cost in jobs and output in the south. This lens follows the euro area across the book's nine phases and asks what it has meant for India.

2000–2026 · about 15 minutes · Read with Phase 4: Uneven recovery, Theme 3: Money and Theme 4: Debt and fiscal policy

In brief

The euro area grew by an average of 2.3 percent a year from 2000 to 2007, 1.4 percent in the 2010s and 0.9 percent in 2023–25, against 2.4 and 2.7 percent in the United States. Its first decade hid large gaps between members: Germany ran big current-account surpluses while Greece, Portugal and Spain ran big deficits. When the 2008 crisis ended the flow of credit, the gaps became a sovereign debt crisis. Greece needed three rescue programmes; unemployment reached 27.5 percent in Greece and 26.1 percent in Spain. The European Central Bank's promise in 2012 to do "whatever it takes" ended the panic, and was followed by negative interest rates and large bond purchases. After the pandemic the EU borrowed jointly on a large scale, then faced its highest inflation since the euro began. For India, the euro area is a large export market whose crises slowed Indian growth, a maker of rules such as the carbon border tax, and, since January 2026, the partner in a concluded free trade agreement.

A confident first decade

The euro began well by most measures (chart EA.1). Inflation averaged 2.2 percent a year from 2000 to 2007, close to the European Central Bank's aim. Governments in Greece, Italy, Portugal, Spain and Ireland could borrow for ten years at almost the same rates as Germany: in June 2007 the gaps, or spreads, were between 0.06 and 0.24 percentage points (chart EA.2). In 2004 the IMF found area-wide fundamentals "seem relatively sound", though potential growth had weakened, and four members, France and Germany among them, had breached the 3 percent deficit limit, leaving the fiscal rules suspended in practice.1 In 2007 it wrote, "The outlook is the best in years."2 In 2008 the European Commission marked the euro's tenth anniversary by calling it "a major success".3

The IMF and the Commission also saw the problem. Inflation had been persistently higher in some members than others, eroding their competitiveness. The IMF warned in 2006 that the resulting divergences "might take a painfully long time to unwind",4 and the Commission noted that they had "led to accumulated competitiveness losses and large external imbalances".3 By 2007 Germany had a current-account surplus of 6.5 percent of GDP, while Spain ran a deficit of 9.4 percent, Portugal 9.6 percent and Greece 14.1 percent. The Commission later judged that Greece's growth over the decade, close to 4 percent a year, "was based on unsustainable drivers".5

From the financial crisis to the euro crisis

The European Central Bank (ECB) raised its rates in July 2008, taking the deposit rate to 3.25 percent; its president, Jean-Claude Trichet, said "the euro area does not suffer from major imbalances".6 The IMF called monetary union "a distinct and promising success" that summer and expected growth of 1.2 percent in 2009.7 Instead output fell 4.5 percent. On 8 October 2008 the ECB cut rates in a move coordinated with five other central banks.8

The crisis turned into a sovereign debt crisis in 2010. Greece's deficit had reached 13.6 percent of GDP in 2009 and its debt 115 percent; in May 2010 the IMF wrote that "The Greek economy is teetering due to heavy public debt".9 Euro-area governments and the IMF lent €110 billion: €80 billion in bilateral loans and €30 billion from the Fund.5 The IMF projected that Greek debt "would peak at 149 percent of GDP in 2013" and fall to 120 percent by 2020.9 Ireland's deficit reached 32.2 percent of GDP in 2010. The IMF called the crisis "a wake-up call for the euro area", in which divergences "have been allowed to fester".10 New rescue funds were created in 2010, the European Financial Stability Mechanism and Facility,10 and later a permanent European Stability Mechanism.

Spreads kept widening (chart EA.2). Greece's peaked at 27.39 percentage points in February 2012, Portugal's at 12.03 in January 2012 and Ireland's at 9.71 in July 2011; Italy's reached 5.19 points in November 2011 and Spain's 5.55 in July 2012. A second Greek programme in March 2012 brought total euro-area lending to €164.5 billion and swapped €197 billion of privately held bonds,11 cutting their value by 60 percent, on the IMF's later measure (the cut in face value was 53.5 percent; Phase 4).12 In July 2012 the IMF wrote that the crisis was "raising questions about the viability of the monetary union itself", and that "the first priority is a banking union".13

"Whatever it takes"

On 26 July 2012 the ECB's president, Mario Draghi, told an audience in London that "the ECB is ready to do whatever it takes to preserve the euro. And believe me, it will be enough."14 In September the ECB set out the details of Outright Monetary Transactions, purchases of the short-term bonds of countries in a rescue programme, for which "No ex ante quantitative limits are set"; it required a rescue programme from the European Stability Mechanism or its predecessor.15 Spreads fell: Italy's from 4.60 points in June 2012 to 3.24 in December, and by 2014 the IMF found them back near pre-crisis levels.16

The damage to jobs was already done (chart EA.3). Euro-area unemployment peaked at 12.2 percent in 2013, with 27.5 percent in Greece and 26.1 percent in Spain, while Germany's kept falling. Then inflation sank. "Inflation has been too low for too long," the IMF wrote in 2014.16 In June 2014 the ECB cut its deposit rate below zero, to −0.1 percent, and in January 2015 it began buying €60 billion of bonds a month, "to address the risks of a too prolonged period of low inflation".1718 Prices fell 0.6 percent in the year to January 2015 (chart EA.4). The deposit rate stayed negative for 97 months, reaching -0.50 percent, and Germany's ten-year bond yield was below zero for 38 months. The ECB's balance sheet grew from €2.01 trillion in September 2014 to €8.83 trillion at its peak in mid-2022 (chart EA.5).

Greece's crisis flared once more in 2015. A third programme of up to €86 billion was agreed in August and completed in August 2018.11 Meanwhile the euro area as a whole moved from external deficit to surplus: its current account went from 0.1 percent of GDP in 2011 to 3.6 percent in 2016,1619 and Germany's surplus peaked at 8.9 percent of GDP in 2016. In 2019 the IMF warned of "a prolonged period of anemic growth and inflation".19

Contested question

Did austerity work in Greece?

No: too much, too fast

The IMF's own review of its 2010 loan found that "Market confidence was not restored", that the debt had to be restructured anyway, and that the projections were "too optimistic": real GDP in 2012 was 17 percent below 2009, against a projected fall of 5½ percent, and unemployment was 25 percent against 15.12 Research at the IMF found that fiscal multipliers, the effect of budget cuts on output, had been substantially larger than forecasters assumed early in the crisis.20

Yes, in the end

By 2025 Greece ran a budget surplus of 1.7 percent of GDP, its debt had fallen from 209.9 percent of GDP in 2020 to 146.6 percent, its spread over Germany was 0.99 points and unemployment was 8.9 percent. The IMF's 2025 review saw debt firmly on a downward path and EU recovery funds driving investment.21

How the evidence weighs up. Both views are true of different periods. Greece's finances did recover, but its real output in 2025 was still 13.8 percent below its 2007 level, while Spain's was 18.7 percent above. The debt the IMF expected to peak at 149 percent of GDP in 2013 was 209.9 percent in 2020.

Pandemic, energy and inflation

The pandemic brought two departures. In March 2020 the ECB launched a €750 billion emergency purchase programme, saying it "will not tolerate any risks to the smooth transmission of its monetary policy".22 And the EU agreed to borrow jointly: the NextGenerationEU recovery fund, set at €806.9 billion, to be repaid from 2028 to 2058.23 Output fell 6.0 percent in 2020 and grew 6.4 percent in 2021.

Inflation caught forecasters out. In December 2021 the IMF projected 3.0 percent for 2022 and expected inflation then to "remain below the ECB’s inflation target".24 It was 8.4 percent, peaking at 10.6 percent in October 2022, as Russia's invasion of Ukraine drove up gas prices (see Phase 7 and Theme 8); the IMF later called it "the largest terms of trade loss in several decades".25 The ECB raised rates from July 2022, adding a Transmission Protection Instrument to stop "unwarranted, disorderly" rises in members' borrowing costs,26 and took its deposit rate to 4.00 percent by September 2023. It began cutting in June 2024 and reached 2 percent in June 2025.27 New EU fiscal rules took effect in April 2024, with a single target for each government's net spending.28 From 2025 the rules' escape clause let 19 members spend more on defence, and in 2026 it was extended to energy security for some.29

The 2026 energy shock (see Phase 9) reversed the easing. The ECB raised its deposit rate to 2.25 percent in June and 2.5 percent in September, warning that "inflation is set to remain well above target for an extended period".30 Inflation was 4.0 percent in October 2026, and the Commission expects 3.2 percent for the year and growth of 1.2 percent.31 Public debt has drifted up (chart EA.6). France's rose from 65.5 percent of GDP in 2007 to 115.5 percent in 2025, with a deficit of 5.1 percent, and in October 2026 its spread over Germany, 1.47 points, was wider than Spain's or Portugal's.

The deeper problem is slow growth. Germany grew by an average of 0.3 percent a year in 2019–25, and the IMF's 2026 review pointed to "Europe’s persistently weak potential growth that reflects subdued productivity growth and aging".32 Mario Draghi, now reporting for the Commission in 2024, called the gap with the United States "an existential challenge" and said that "a minimum annual additional investment of EUR 750 to 800 billion is needed".33

India and the euro area

For India, Europe has been a market, a source of shocks and a setter of rules. In 2000-01 Europe took 25.9 percent of India's exports; by 2012-13 its share had fallen to 18.7 percent, and the Survey that year noted that "India has been fairly successful in diversifying its export markets".34 The share then recovered. In 2024-25 India exported $75.9 billion of goods to the European Union, 17.3 percent of its exports, and 22.5 percent to Europe as a whole; it imported $60.7 billion from the EU, a surplus of about $15 billion.35 India's economy, measured in dollars, grew from 7.2 percent of the euro area's in 2000 to 21.7 percent in 2025, and the IMF projects 30.0 percent by 2031 (chart EA.7).

The euro crisis hit India at a bad moment. The Survey of February 2011 forecast growth of 9 percent in 2011-12 and called a second-dip recession in the rich world "a very low probability event".36 By the next year's Survey the environment had "turned sharply adverse in September 2011 owing to the turmoil in the eurozone"; export growth fell from 40.6 percent in the first half of 2011-12 to −0.5 percent in November 2011, and the Survey named the euro-area crisis among the global causes of India's slowdown.37 Growth in 2011-12 was 6.2 percent on the figures of the time (5.2 percent on today's series).38 The 2012-13 Survey summed up: "The fallout for the Indian economy has been a sharp deceleration in exports".34 In 2015 it still listed "possible turmoil if the viability of the Eurozone were to come into question" among India's main external risks.39 The rupee has fallen against the euro over the period, from an average of about ₹46 per euro in 1999 to ₹106.96 in October 2026 (chart EA.8).

Europe now affects India as much through its rules as through its demand. The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, which charges importers for the carbon in steel, aluminium, cement and fertilisers, began with reporting in 2024 and applies the levy from January 2026. The 2024-25 Survey found that the goods it covers made up 10.5 percent of India's exports to the EU in 2023, up from 6.3 percent in 2014, and warned that it risked "negating the comparative advantage of developing members like India".40 The 2023-24 Survey had called such taxes contrary to "the spirit of the Paris Agreement".41

Trade talks have moved slowly. Negotiations on a broad trade and investment agreement had gone through 15 rounds by December 2012.34 Talks on a free trade agreement were relaunched in June 2022, and on 27 January 2026 "the EU and India concluded negotiations for a Free Trade Agreement", which will cut tariffs on more than 96 percent of EU goods exports to India. The Commission put the saving to EU exporters at about €4 billion a year, and EU firms already held €132.8 billion of direct investment in India in 2024. The Commission sent the agreement to the Council for signature on 11 September 2026; it is not yet in force.42 India's 2025-26 Survey recorded the "free trade agreement with the European Union after three years of negotiations" in its preface, noting that the European Parliament must still ratify it.43 In November 2026 the EU also imposed anti-dumping duties on cold-rolled steel from India and other countries.42

Key terms

Spread
The gap between the interest rate on one government's bonds and another's, here Germany's; a wider spread means investors see more risk.
Outright Monetary Transactions
The ECB's 2012 programme to buy, without a preset limit, the short-term bonds of a euro-area country that has agreed a rescue programme.
Negative interest rates
A policy rate below zero, so that banks pay to keep money with the central bank.
NextGenerationEU
The EU's pandemic recovery fund, financed by joint borrowing by the European Commission on behalf of the EU.
Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism
An EU charge on imports of carbon-intensive goods such as steel and aluminium, matching the carbon price paid by EU producers.
